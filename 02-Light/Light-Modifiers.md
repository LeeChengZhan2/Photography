# Light Modifiers

> [!summary] In one line
> A modifier is anything that changes light between the source and the subject. It can
> make the light softer, send it somewhere else, add it back into the shadows, or take
> it away, and most of them can be made from things you already own.

## What it is

| Modifier | What it is | Quality | Direction | Contrast |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reflector | A light surface that bounces light into the shadows | Adds a second, softer light | From wherever you hold it | Lower |
| Diffuser | Translucent material between the light and the subject | Softer, and dimmer | Same as the light | A little lower |
| Scrim | A large diffuser on a frame, often held over a subject in sun | Much softer | From the scrim | Lower |
| Flag | An opaque panel, usually black, that blocks light | Unchanged | Stops light reaching part of the scene | Higher |
| Negative fill | A black surface beside the subject that absorbs light | Unchanged | — | Higher |
| Bounce surface | A wall, ceiling or board you aim a light into | Much softer | From the surface | Lower |
| Softbox | A box with a diffusing front around a light | Soft, controlled | Wherever you aim it | Depends on distance |
| Umbrella | Light shot through or bounced off an umbrella | Soft, spreads widely | Broad | Lower |

## How it works

Every modifier does one or more of four things:

1. **Makes the source bigger.** A diffuser or a bounce surface becomes the new source.
   A bigger source, seen from the subject, gives softer light. → [[Quality-of-Light]]
2. **Redirects the light.** A reflector or a bounce surface changes where the light
   comes from.
3. **Adds light to the shadows.** A reflector lowers the [[Contrast-Ratio]].
4. **Takes light away.** A flag or negative fill blocks or absorbs light, raising
   contrast. A flag can also stop stray light from hitting the lens and causing flare.

Every modifier costs light. A diffuser absorbs and scatters some of it. A bounce spreads
it over a large area and a longer path. Expect to raise the exposure or the power.

### Reflector surfaces

- **White** scatters light in all directions. It is soft, neutral and forgiving to aim,
  but fairly weak.
- **Silver** acts more like a mirror: light leaves at the same angle it arrives. It is
  brighter and harder, so you must aim it carefully, and in full sun it can dazzle.
- **Gold** warms the light. On skin it can look orange, so use it sparingly.
- **Black** is negative fill: it bounces almost nothing.

A reflector follows the [[Inverse-Square-Law]] too: move it closer for more fill.

### Scrims, softboxes and umbrellas

In stills photography, a **scrim** usually means a large diffuser on a frame, held
between the sun and the subject. On film sets, the word can also mean a metal mesh that
dims a lamp without softening it.

A **softbox** wraps a light in a box with a diffusing front. Most of the light goes
forward, so it spills less onto walls and background. Its shape shows in the
catchlights.

An **umbrella** is cheaper and faster to set up. *Shoot-through* umbrellas are
translucent and the light passes through; *reflective* ones bounce it back off the
inside. Both spread light widely, so they give less control than a softbox.

Two small extras: a **grid** (a honeycomb over the front) narrows the beam to keep
light off the background, and a **snoot** (a tube) makes a small spot of light.

## How to use it

Start from the problem, not the gadget:

| Problem | Reach for |
|---|---|
| Light too hard | Diffuser, scrim or bounce |
| Shadows too dark | Reflector, or a bounce surface on the shadow side |
| Light too flat | Flag or negative fill on one side |
| Light spilling onto the background | Flag, grid, or turn the light away |
| Flare from a light pointing at the lens | Flag between the light and the lens |

Placement tips:

- **Put a diffuser as close to the subject as you can**, because the diffuser is now
  the source and its relative size sets the softness.
- **Hold a reflector on the shadow side at about face height**, angled so you can see
  the light land on the face. Too low and it lights the face from below.
- **For a midday portrait, hold a scrim over the subject.** The face gets soft light,
  while the background stays in hard sun and goes brighter. Expose for the face.

### Free alternatives

| Instead of | Use |
|---|---|
| Reflector | White foam board, a white bedsheet, a sheet of A3 paper, a white wall, pale pavement |
| Silver reflector | Kitchen foil, crinkled and glued to cardboard, or a car windscreen sunshade |
| Diffuser or scrim | A thin white curtain, baking paper, tracing paper, a white shower curtain, a bedsheet held by two friends |
| Flag or negative fill | Black cloth, a dark T-shirt or towel, a book, your hand |
| Bounce surface | Any white or light grey wall or ceiling |

> [!warning] Heat
> Never put paper or cloth against a hot tungsten or halogen lamp: it can catch fire.
> LEDs and phone torches run much cooler, but check before you tape anything on.

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving the diffuser far from the subject.** It becomes a small source again and
  the light turns hard. Bring it close.
- **Holding the reflector too low.** Light from below looks unnatural. Raise it to
  face height and angle it.
- **Starting with a gold or silver reflector.** Gold turns skin orange and silver can
  blind people in sun. Start with white.
- **Forgetting that modifiers cost light.** Check the exposure after every change.
- **Only ever adding light.** Sometimes the fix is to take light away with a flag or
  black cloth.

## Try it

Twenty minutes beside a window with direct sun, any device. Use a person or a piece of
fruit. Keep the framing fixed and shoot five versions:

1. Bare light.
2. A white card or foam board on the shadow side.
3. Kitchen foil on card in the same place.
4. A black cloth in the same place.
5. Baking paper or a thin curtain between the window and the subject, close to the
   subject.

Compare the shadow side and the shadow edges. Frames 2 to 4 preview the reflector fill
in **A2.5**; frame 5 is one step of **A2.2**.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- You hang a thin white curtain over a sunny window. What happens to quality, direction and contrast?
> The light gets softer, because the curtain becomes a large source. The direction
> stays the same, from the window. Contrast drops a little, and the light gets dimmer.

> [!question]- Why does a silver reflector need more careful aiming than a white one?
> Silver reflects like a mirror, sending the light off in one direction at the same
> angle it arrived. White scatters light in all directions, so it is easier to aim but
> weaker.

> [!question]- A portrait in a white-walled room looks flat. Which modifier helps?
> A flag or negative fill: a black cloth on the shadow side. It absorbs light that the
> walls would bounce into the shadows, so contrast rises and the face gets shape.

> [!question]- You put a large diffuser 3 m from your subject, yet the shadows are still sharp. Why?
> The diffuser is now the light source, and at 3 m it looks small from the subject.
> Move it close to the subject and the light softens.
