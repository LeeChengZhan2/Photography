# Balancing Flash and Ambient

> [!summary] In one line
> Every flash photo is two exposures in one frame: the existing light, set mainly by the
> shutter, and the flash, set by its power. Aperture and ISO affect both. Set the
> background first, then add the flash.

## What it is

**Ambient light** is everything already there: the sky, the sun, room lamps, street
lights. **Flash** is the light you add. In one frame they act as two separate
exposures, each with its own controls:

| Control | Ambient exposure | Flash exposure |
|---|---|---|
| Shutter speed (at or below the sync speed) | Yes | Almost none |
| Aperture | Yes | Yes |
| ISO | Yes | Yes |
| Flash power | No | Yes |
| Flash distance | No | Yes |

**Balancing** means choosing how bright the flash-lit subject is compared with the
ambient-lit background.

## How it works

The flash burst is far shorter than the shutter time. The shutter only has to be open
when it fires; leaving it open longer doesn't add any flash. Ambient light, though,
builds up for the whole time the shutter is open. → [[Flash-Fundamentals]]

So the controls split neatly:

- **Shutter speed** moves the background (ambient) only.
- **Flash power and distance** move the subject (flash) only.
- **Aperture and ISO** move both together. Use them to set the overall level, then use
  the shutter and the power to split it.

A worked example at dusk. With the flash off, the meter says f/2.8, 1/30 s, ISO 400
for the sky. You want the sky one stop darker and richer, so you set 1/60 s. Your
friend's face is now dark. Turn the flash on and raise its power until the face looks
right at f/2.8 and ISO 400. Sky too bright? Use a faster shutter. Face too bright? Use
less flash power.

How strong to make the flash:

| Flash on the subject compared with ambient | Look |
|---|---|
| 1–2 stops weaker | Subtle fill: shadows lifted, looks natural |
| About equal | Clearly lit, still balanced |
| 1–2 stops stronger, background darker | The subject "pops"; dramatic |

### Dragging the shutter

**Dragging the shutter** means using a slow shutter speed with flash, such as 1/15 s.
The flash freezes the subject, while the long exposure lets the room lights, the city
or the sky register. If ambient light also falls on the subject, it records a second,
blurred "ghost" around the sharp one. Rear-curtain sync puts that blur behind a moving
subject, where it looks natural. → [[Sync-Speed]]

### Fill flash outdoors

In sun, faces get hard shadows, and backlit faces go dark. Expose normally for the
scene, then add flash about 1–2 stops below the ambient to lift the shadows. In TTL,
that is flash exposure compensation of about −1 to −2. In bright sun the shutter
speed you need may be above the sync speed. Then close the aperture, lower the ISO, or
use high-speed sync if your flash and camera support it.

### Gels to match the colour

Flash is close to daylight, about 5500 K. Indoor lamps are often much warmer, about
2700–3000 K. → [[Colour-Temperature-of-Light]]

- White balance set for the lamps: the room looks right, but the flash-lit face turns
  blue.
- White balance set for the flash: the face looks right, but the room turns orange.

The fix is a **gel**, a sheet of coloured film over the flash. A full **CTO**
(colour temperature orange) gel turns flash into roughly the colour of tungsten light.
Set white balance for tungsten, and both now look neutral. Half and quarter CTO warm
the flash less. Under green-tinted fluorescent tubes, a green gel does the same job.

You can also mismatch on purpose. Gel the flash with full CTO outdoors at dusk and set
white balance for tungsten. The face looks normal, and the sky turns deep blue.

## How to use it

1. **Flash off.** Choose the ISO and the aperture (for depth of field and flash range).
2. **Set the background.** Meter it and choose the shutter speed that gives the
   ambient look you want: normal, or one or two stops darker for drama. Stay at or
   below the sync speed.
3. **Take a frame without flash.** That's your background, settled.
4. **Turn the flash on.** Adjust its power, or FEC in TTL, until the subject looks
   right. Don't touch the shutter to fix the subject.
5. **Adjust one side at a time.** Background → shutter. Subject → flash power or
   distance. Both → aperture or ISO.
6. **Check the colour.** Match the flash to the room with a gel, or mismatch on
   purpose.

## On your kit

On the L10, the flash fires only with the mechanical shutter, so the electronic
shutter's high speeds are not available here. The aperture ring makes step 1 quick.
Find the sync speed before you rely on any of this. Wide open at f/1.7 in bright sun,
the shutter you'd need is beyond what the mechanical shutter allows with flash, so
close the aperture or use HSS if your flash and the L10 support it.

## Common mistakes

- **Fixing the subject with the shutter.** It moves the background, not the
  flash-lit face. Change the flash power.
- **Fixing the background with flash power.** It moves the subject only. Change the
  shutter.
- **Too much fill outdoors.** It looks fake. Start about 1.5 stops below the ambient.
- **Ignoring colour.** Blue faces in a warm room mean the flash needs a gel.
- **Dragging the shutter with strong light on the subject.** You get ghosting. Keep the
  subject mainly flash-lit, or use rear-curtain sync.

## Try it

Thirty minutes at dusk with a flash on the L10: the flash-fill part of **A2.5**.

1. Put a friend in front of a bright sky. Flash off, expose for the sky: they become a
   silhouette.
2. Turn the flash on, in manual. Adjust its power until the face looks right.
3. Make the shutter one stop faster. The sky darkens; the face doesn't change.
4. Now try 1/15 s, and see what the ambient light and any movement do.

No flash yet? Do the reflector version of A2.5 instead.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- The subject looks right but the background is too bright. What do you change?
> A faster shutter speed, up to the sync speed. It cuts the ambient light while the
> flash on the subject stays the same.

> [!question]- The background looks right but the subject is too bright. What do you change?
> Lower the flash power, or move the flash further away. Neither affects the ambient
> exposure.

> [!question]- You raise the ISO from 400 to 800. What happens, and how do you get back to the same picture?
> Both the background and the subject get one stop brighter. Make the shutter one stop
> faster for the background and halve the flash power for the subject.

> [!question]- Indoors under warm lamps, the room looks right but the flash-lit face looks blue. Why, and what's the fix?
> White balance is set for the warm lamps, but flash is close to daylight, so it reads
> as blue. Put a CTO gel on the flash to match it to the lamps.

> [!question]- Why can dragging the shutter create a ghost image?
> During the long exposure, ambient light on the subject records a second, blurred
> image as they move. The flash only freezes the part it lights.
