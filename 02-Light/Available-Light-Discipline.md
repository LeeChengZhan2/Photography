# Available Light Discipline

> [!summary] In one line
> Available-light discipline is the habit of asking whether a scene needs any added
> light at all. Often the existing light is the reason the picture is worth taking, so
> your job is to find the best of it and make the camera cope.

## What it is

**Available light** is whatever light is already there: daylight, but also lamps,
candles, shop signs and screens. **Discipline** means choosing not to add light even
when you could, and working with what the scene gives you.

It is a decision, not a lack of gear. A photographer with three flashes still leaves
them in the bag when the light in the room is the story.

## How it works

### Three reasons not to add light

- **Mood.** The light is often the subject: candles at dinner, the glow of a hawker
  stall at night, a dim temple. Flash replaces that light with its own flat, white
  light from the camera. You keep the people and lose the place.
- **Honesty.** In documentary work, the light is part of the record. Flash changes
  what the scene looked like. It also announces you, and people change how they act.
- **Respect and rules.** Flash disturbs, and is often banned, at performances
  (concerts, theatre, dance), in places of worship, in many museums, and around
  wildlife, where it startles animals. Singapore's Night Safari, for example, bans
  flash photography and torches. Rules change, so read the signs and ask when unsure.

### The cost: less light

Without added light, you push every side of the [[Exposure-Triangle]]:

| Control | Push it to | Price |
|---|---|---|
| Aperture | As wide as it goes | Shallow depth of field; focus must be precise |
| Shutter speed | As slow as you can hold steady | Blur from shake or movement |
| ISO | Up to your personal ceiling from A1.3 | Noise |

Protect them in this order: first a shutter speed that freezes the subject, then the
widest aperture, then let ISO take whatever is left. Noise can be reduced in editing;
blur can't be fixed. → [[Noise-Reduction]]

### Expose for the light

In dim places, light falls in pools with darkness between them. The camera's meter
averages the scene and tries to make it mid-grey, so it brightens a dark room, washes
out the mood and can blow the bright areas. Expose for the most important lit area,
such as the face in the lamplight, and let the shadows stay dark. Use negative
exposure compensation, or meter on the lit area itself.

## How to use it

### Find the best light, then move the subject into it

- **Look for pools of light:** a shop window, a doorway, a lamp, a screen, the edge of
  a window.
- **Move the subject**, or wait for them to step into the light. Someone 1 m from a
  window has far better light than someone 3 m into the room.
- **Turn them** so the light falls across the face at about 45°.
- **In street photography**, find the light first, then wait for a person to walk into
  it.

### Use only a reflector

A reflector adds no new source; it redirects the light that is already there. The
light keeps its colour and direction, and only the shadows lift. A white menu, a
newspaper, a white tablecloth or a pale floor all work. The reflector fill in **A2.5**
is exactly this. → [[Light-Modifiers]]

### Hold the camera still

- Lean on a wall or a pillar, tuck your elbows in, and breathe out as you press.
- Use the viewfinder: a camera pressed to your face is steadier than one held out.
- Shoot a short burst of three and keep the sharpest.
- Shoot raw, so you have room for noise reduction and for white balance in mixed light.

## On your kit

- **L10:** f/1.7 is available only at 24 mm, and the widest aperture shrinks to f/2.8
  at 75 mm, so stay wide in the dark. The lens OIS helps against shake, not against a
  moving subject. The electronic shutter is silent, useful at performances and in
  places of worship, but it can show banding under LED lights and makes fast motion
  lean.
- **iPhone:** Night mode combines many frames, which works well for still scenes.
- **Pocket 3:** your best low-light *video* camera. For low-light *stills*, the L10's
  larger sensor wins.

## Common mistakes

- **Reaching for flash out of habit.** First ask what the existing light is doing for
  the picture.
- **Letting the meter brighten a dark scene.** The mood washes out and the highlights
  blow. Expose for the lit areas.
- **Fearing noise more than blur.** A noisy sharp photo can be cleaned up; a blurred
  one can't.
- **Ignoring the rules.** Flash at a performance or in a temple disturbs people. Check
  and ask.
- **Shooting the subject where they happen to be.** One step into better light often
  matters more than any setting.

## Try it

Thirty minutes, one evening, at a hawker centre or a lit street:

1. Use the L10 at f/1.7 (24 mm) and about 1/125 s, and let the ISO rise to suit the
   light (Auto ISO).
2. Find three pools of light. Photograph a person or an object in each, exposing for
   the lit part: spot-meter it, or use minus exposure compensation. How compensation
   works in M with Auto ISO varies, so check the L10's manual, or use A mode instead.
3. Shoot one of them again with the camera metering normally, and compare the mood.
4. If a friend is with you, move them one step into better light, and compare again.

This is also practice for the Module 2 exit test: walk into a place and, within a
minute, point to where you'd put a person and why.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why can flash ruin a photo of a candlelit dinner, even when it's well exposed?
> The candlelight was the mood. Flash replaces it with flat, white light from the
> camera, so the faces are lit but the feeling of the place is gone.

> [!question]- In a dim hawker centre, a stallholder is moving. Which setting do you protect first, and why?
> The shutter speed, fast enough to freeze them. Then open the aperture fully and let
> ISO rise as needed. Noise can be reduced later; motion blur can't be fixed.

> [!question]- The camera turns a dark night scene into a grey, washed-out photo. Why, and how do you fix it?
> The meter tries to make the scene mid-grey, so it brightens it. Dial in negative
> exposure compensation, or meter for the lit area only.

> [!question]- How does a reflector keep the light "available"?
> It doesn't add a new source; it redirects the existing light. The colour and the
> direction stay the same, and only the shadows lift.
