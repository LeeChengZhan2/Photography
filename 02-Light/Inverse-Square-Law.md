# Inverse-Square Law

> [!summary] In one line
> Light from a small source spreads out as it travels: double the distance and the
> subject gets a quarter of the light, two stops less. That makes distance one of your
> strongest lighting controls.

## What it is

The inverse-square law says that the light falling on a subject from a small source
drops with the *square* of the distance:

- Double the distance → 1/4 of the light (2 stops less).
- Triple the distance → 1/9 of the light (just over 3 stops less).
- Halve the distance → 4 times the light (2 stops more).

Photographers call this **fall-off**: how fast light dims as you move away from the
source. The distance that counts is from the light to the subject. Where the camera
stands makes no difference.

## How it works

Picture the light from a bulb spreading out like a cone. At 1 m it covers a patch of a
certain size. At 2 m the patch is twice as wide and twice as tall, so it has four times
the area. The same light is spread over four times the area, so each part of the
subject gets a quarter.

| Distance from the light | Light compared with 1 m | Change in stops |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 m | 4× | +2 |
| 0.7 m | 2× | +1 |
| 1 m | 1× | 0 |
| 1.4 m | 1/2 | −1 |
| 2 m | 1/4 | −2 |
| 2.8 m | 1/8 | −3 |
| 4 m | 1/16 | −4 |
| 5.6 m | 1/32 | −5 |

The distances in the table follow the f-number sequence. That's the same maths: light
goes with the square, so each one-stop step multiplies the number by about 1.4 (the
square root of 2). → [[Stops-and-EV]]

### Ratios, not metres

What matters is the *ratio* between two distances. Close to a light, a small move is a
big ratio. Far from it, the same move is a small ratio.

Two people stand one behind the other, 1 m apart:

| Light to front person | Light to back person | Back person is darker by |
|---|---|---|
| 1 m | 2 m | 2 stops |
| 2 m | 3 m | About 1.2 stops |
| 4 m | 5 m | About 0.6 stops |

The gap is always 1 m. Only the distance to the light changed.

### Where it stops working

The law is exact only for a **small source**: one that is tiny compared with its
distance from the subject.

- **Large sources close up**, such as a window or a big softbox. Up close, different
  parts of the source sit at different distances and angles, so light falls off more
  slowly than the law predicts. As a rough guide, the law works well again once you
  are more than about five times the source's width away.
- **The sun.** It is 150 million km away, so walking a few metres changes the distance
  by almost nothing. Sunlight has no fall-off within a scene: a sunlit face and a
  sunlit wall behind it get the same light.
- **Rooms.** Walls and ceilings bounce light back onto the subject from every side.
  That extra light makes measured fall-off smaller than the law predicts.
- **Focused beams.** A lamp with a lens or deep reflector (a spotlight, many torches)
  falls off a little more slowly, as if the light came from further behind the lamp.

## How to use it

**Darken or brighten the background with distance.** Put a subject 1 m from a lamp,
with a wall 2 m behind them (3 m from the lamp). The wall gets 1/9 of the light, about
3 stops darker than the face. Now move the lamp back to 3 m from the subject: the wall
is 5 m away and only about 1.5 stops darker.

- Dark, dramatic background → light close to the subject, subject far from the wall.
- Even, bright background or an even group → light further away, with more power.

**Know the trade-off.** Moving a light closer makes it softer and its fall-off steeper.
Moving it back makes it harder and more even. One small light can't give you both soft
light and an evenly lit room. → [[Quality-of-Light]]

**Feather the light.** Most lights are brightest in the centre of the beam. Aim the
centre at the person furthest away, so the nearest person gets the dimmer edge. The
weaker edge cancels part of the distance difference. Feathering away from a wall also
keeps light off the background.

**Do the exposure maths.** Move a light from 2 m to 1.4 m and the subject gets one stop
more light. Close the aperture one stop (f/4 → f/5.6) or halve the flash power.

## On your kit

The law applies to any light you add: the iPhone torch, a desk lamp, or a flash on the
L10's hot shoe. To measure it, use the L10 in manual mode. Fix the aperture and ISO,
meter a single spot on the subject, and note the shutter speed that brings the meter
to zero at each distance. On the Pocket 3 and Action 5 Pro you can set the shutter in
Pro / PRO mode; check their manuals for an exposure readout to use the same way.

## Common mistakes

- **Measuring from the camera.** Only the light-to-subject distance counts. You can
  walk the camera anywhere without changing the lighting.
- **Testing in a small white room.** Bounce from the walls fills in, so the numbers
  come out too small. Use a dark room, or go outdoors at night.
- **Expecting the law to fit at a window.** A window is a large source. Close to it,
  light falls off more slowly than the law says.
- **Trying to darken a sunlit background by moving.** The sun has no fall-off over a
  few metres. Use shade, a different angle, or flash on the subject.
- **Moving a light back for evenness and forgetting quality.** Further away also means
  harder light.

## Try it

Thirty minutes at night, in a dark room. This is the first half of **A2.3**:

1. Put the iPhone torch on a shelf at face height. Seat a person, or put a cushion, at
   0.5 m from it. Measure from the torch to the subject.
2. On the L10 in M mode, fix the aperture and ISO. Adjust the shutter until the meter
   reads zero on one spot, for example the cheek. Shoot and note the shutter speed.
3. Repeat at 1 m and 2 m.
4. Predict before you shoot: two stops per doubling, so four stops in total. For
   example, 1/250 s at 0.5 m should become about 1/15 s at 2 m.
5. Then repeat at a window, at 0.5, 1 and 2 m from the glass, and compare.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- You move a flash from 2 m to 1 m from a face. How much brighter is the face, and how do you keep the same exposure?
> Four times brighter, which is two stops. Close the aperture two stops (for example
> f/2.8 → f/5.6), or cut the flash power to a quarter.

> [!question]- One light, a group of people. The front row is 2 m from the light, the back row 3 m. What happens, and how do you fix it?
> The back row gets (2/3)² of the light, about 1.2 stops less. Move the light further
> back (at 4 m and 5 m the gap is only about 0.6 stops), or feather the beam towards
> the back row.

> [!question]- Why can't you darken a sunlit background by moving your subject closer to it?
> The sun is so far away that a few metres change nothing, so the subject and the
> background get the same sunlight. Light the subject with flash and expose for it, or
> choose a background in shade.

> [!question]- At a window your readings drop less than two stops between 0.5 m and 1 m. Has the law failed?
> No. The law describes small sources, and close up a window is a large one. Bounce
> from the room adds light as well. Far enough back, the readings start to fit.

> [!question]- One lamp, a small room, and you want a black background. Where do you put things?
> Lamp close to the subject, subject as far from the wall as possible, and the lamp
> turned so its beam doesn't reach the wall. Fall-off then darkens the background.
