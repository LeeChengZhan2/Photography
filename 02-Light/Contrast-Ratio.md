# Contrast Ratio

> [!summary] In one line
> The contrast ratio, or lighting ratio, compares the light on the lit side of a
> subject with the light on its shadow side. Measured in stops, it sets the mood: low
> ratios look open and gentle, high ratios look dramatic.

## What it is

Light a face from one side. The lit cheek gets the **key light**, the main light. The
shadow cheek gets only **fill**: light from a reflector, a second lamp, the walls or the
sky. The contrast ratio says how much brighter the lit side is.

It describes the *light*, not the subject. A black shirt in perfectly even light is
still dark; that's the subject's own contrast, not the lighting ratio.

People write the ratio in two ways (see [[Glossary]]):

- **(key + fill) : fill.** The lit side gets key plus fill; the shadow side gets fill
  only. Equal lights give **2:1**. This is what you measure on the subject.
- **key : fill.** This compares the two lights themselves. Equal lights give **1:1**.

**This note uses lit side : shadow side, which is (key + fill) : fill**, because that
is what your meter reads on the face. Whenever you write a ratio, say which convention
you used.

## How it works

Each doubling of the ratio is one stop:

| Lit : shadow | Stops between the sides | Key compared with fill | Look |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2:1 | 1 | Equal | Soft, open, nearly flat |
| 3:1 | About 1.6 | Twice the fill | Gentle shape; a classic portrait |
| 4:1 | 2 | Three times the fill | Clear shape, some drama |
| 8:1 | 3 | Seven times the fill | Dramatic, moody |
| 16:1 | 4 | Fifteen times the fill | Shadows close to black |

A worked example shows why the conventions differ. Say the fill reaches the shadow side
two stops weaker than the key, so it is a quarter of the key's strength:

- Lit side: key + fill = 1 + ¼ = 1¼.
- Shadow side: fill only = ¼.
- Lit : shadow = 1¼ : ¼ = **5:1**, about 2.3 stops.
- In the key : fill convention, the same setup is called **4:1**.

Same lights, two different numbers. Hence the rule: always say which one you mean.

Some everyday ratios:

- **Overcast day:** the whole sky lights both sides, so the ratio is low, often 2:1 or
  less.
- **Direct midday sun:** the shadow side gets only skylight and light bounced off the
  ground, so the ratio is high, often around 8:1 or more.
- **Window light in a white room:** the walls bounce light into the shadows, so the
  ratio stays moderate. In a dark room it climbs.

### Measuring it with the spot meter

Use the camera's spot meter, which reads one small area (→ [[Metering-Modes]]):

1. Set manual mode and spot metering.
2. Aim the spot at the lit cheek. Adjust the shutter until the meter reads 0.
3. Without changing anything, aim at the shadow cheek. Read how far under it shows.
4. Convert: −1 = 2:1, about −1⅔ = 3:1, −2 = 4:1, −3 = 8:1.

This works because both cheeks are the same skin, so the difference is purely the
light. A reflected-light meter reads what bounces back, so comparing a white shirt with
dark hair would measure the clothes, not the light. If skin isn't available, hold a
grey or white card at each side in turn.

## How to use it

Decide the mood first, then build the ratio:

- **Open and friendly** (family, products, beauty): 2:1 to 3:1.
- **Shape with some drama** (most portraits, food): 3:1 to 4:1.
- **Dark and moody** (character portraits, night scenes): 8:1 and above.

To **lower** the ratio, add fill:

- A reflector or white card on the shadow side. Move it closer for more fill, because
  of the [[Inverse-Square-Law]].
- A second light or a flash at low power.
- Move the subject to a white-walled spot, which fills the shadows for you.

To **raise** the ratio, take fill away:

- Hang a black cloth or card on the shadow side. This is *negative fill*: it absorbs
  light that would otherwise bounce into the shadows.
- Move away from white walls, or switch off the room lights.
- Move the key light closer. The key gets stronger while the room's fill stays the same.

## On your kit

The L10 is the easiest device to measure with: in manual mode with spot metering, the
meter shows the difference in stops directly. Check the manual for how to select spot
metering. On the other three devices, measure the scene with the L10 first, or judge
the ratio from the photos.

## Common mistakes

- **Giving a ratio without the convention.** "3:1" describes two different setups. Say
  "lit : shadow".
- **Metering two different materials.** You end up measuring the colours, not the
  light. Meter the same skin, or a card, on both sides.
- **Forgetting the room.** White walls quietly fill your shadows. If the light looks
  flat, look for what is bouncing.
- **Treating a low ratio as "correct".** 2:1 can look flat and dull. The right ratio is
  the one that fits the mood.
- **Making the fill stronger than the key.** The fill then becomes the main light, and
  the shadows flip to the other side or double up. Keep the fill no stronger than the
  key.

## Try it

Twenty minutes at a window, any time without direct sun:

1. Seat someone side-on to the window, about 1 m away. Spot-meter both cheeks on the
   L10 and write down the ratio.
2. Hold a sheet of white foam board or a white towel 1 m from the shadow side. Measure
   again. Then move it to 0.5 m and measure again.
3. Replace it with a black cloth or a dark T-shirt. Measure once more.
4. Shoot each version and put the four frames side by side.

This is a warm-up for the reflector fill in **A2.5**.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- The key and the fill are equally bright at the subject. What is the ratio in each convention?
> 2:1 in lit : shadow, because the lit side gets both lights and the shadow side gets
> only the fill. 1:1 in key : fill, because the two lights are equal.

> [!question]- You meter the lit cheek at 0 and the shadow cheek at −2. What is the ratio?
> 4:1 lit : shadow, a two-stop difference. The key is three times as strong as the
> fill at the subject.

> [!question]- A portrait in a white-walled room looks flat. Without adding a light, how do you add shape?
> Reduce the fill. Hang a black cloth on the shadow side, move the subject away from
> the walls, or turn them so the shadow side faces a darker part of the room.

> [!question]- Why meter the skin on both sides rather than the face on one side and the shirt on the other?
> A camera meter reads reflected light, and different materials reflect different
> amounts. Using the same skin on both sides means the only difference is the light.
