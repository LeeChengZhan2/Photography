# Lens Filters

> [!summary] In one line
> A filter is a piece of glass or resin in front of the lens that changes the light
> before it reaches the sensor. A polariser and ND filters do things editing can't; a
> UV filter mostly does nothing.

## What it is

| Filter | What it does | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Circular polariser (CPL) | Cuts reflections and glare; darkens blue sky at some angles | Water, glass, leaves, sky |
| ND (neutral density) | Darkens everything evenly, without changing colour | Long exposures in daylight; video in daylight |
| Graduated ND (GND) | Dark at the top, fading to clear at the bottom | Holding back a bright sky |
| UV or "protective" | Blocks ultraviolet light; covers the front glass | Little effect on a digital camera |

Filters come as screw-in rings for a threaded lens, square sheets in a holder,
magnetic mounts, or clip-on and case-mounted systems for phones.

## How it works

### Polariser

Light travels as a wave that vibrates across its direction of travel. Ordinary light
vibrates in every direction. When light reflects off a non-metal surface, such as
water, glass, a leaf or paint, the reflection vibrates mostly in one direction: it is
*polarised*. A polariser works like a very fine grid that lets through only one
direction. Turn it to block the reflection's direction, and the reflection fades.

A polariser isn't the only way to beat a reflection. A shiny surface reflects light at
the same angle it arrives, like a ball bouncing off a wall. Move the light, or move
yourself, so that angle sends the reflection away from the lens. That is the trick for
glasses in a portrait and for glossy products on a table.

- **Water and glass:** you see into the water or through the window. The effect is
  strongest when you look at the surface at a shallow angle, roughly 30–40° from it.
  Looking straight down at it, a polariser hardly helps.
- **Leaves:** glossy leaves reflect the white sky. Removing that sheen shows their real
  green, which matters in a city as green as Singapore.
- **Blue sky:** skylight is sunlight scattered by the air, and it is most polarised at
  about 90° to the sun. That is where the sky darkens most. Shooting towards or away
  from the sun, little happens. A quick check: point your index finger at the sun and
  stick your thumb out at a right angle. As you turn your hand, your thumb sweeps the
  band of sky where the effect is strongest.
- **Cost:** a polariser takes away about 1–2 stops of light.
- **Wide lenses:** a very wide view takes in sky at many different angles to the sun,
  so the sky darkens unevenly, with a dark band across part of it. Watch for this at
  wide settings such as 24 mm, and expect it with ultra-wides.

"Circular" refers to an extra layer that stops the filter confusing the metering and
autofocus parts of some cameras. It is the standard type.

### ND filters

An ND filter is grey glass that cuts all colours equally. Strength is given in stops,
but makers label it in two ways:

| Label (factor) | Label (density) | Stops |
|---|---|---|
| ND2 | 0.3 | 1 |
| ND8 | 0.9 | 3 |
| ND64 | 1.8 | 6 |
| ND1000 | 3.0 | 10 |

A worked example. Daylight needs f/8, 1/125 s, ISO 100. Add a 10-stop ND, and the
shutter must be 1024 times longer: 1/125 s × 1024 ≈ 8 s. Water turns smooth, clouds
streak and walking people disappear. → [[Long-Exposure]]. In video, ND keeps the
shutter at the speed the frame rate needs in daylight. → [[ND-Filters]]

A *variable* ND is two polarisers turned against each other. It is convenient, but at
strong settings, especially on wide lenses, it can make an uneven dark "X" across the
frame. Strong NDs can also add a colour cast; fix it with white balance.

### Graduated ND

A GND is dark at the top and fades to clear. Lined up on the horizon, it holds back a
bright sky so that the sky and the foreground fit in one exposure. A soft edge suits
uneven horizons; a hard edge suits a flat sea horizon. Anything that rises above the
line, such as buildings or trees, gets darkened too.

The alternatives:

- **Bracket and merge:** shoot several exposures and combine them in software. It
  handles any horizon shape, but the scene must stay still. → [[Photo-Merges]]
- **One raw file and a graduated mask:** darken the sky in editing, as long as it isn't
  clipped. → [[Local-Adjustments]]

A GND works in a single frame, so it still helps with moving water or people.

### UV and "protective" filters

Film was sensitive to ultraviolet light, which showed as haze. Digital sensors already
have their own filters in front of them, so a UV filter changes very little. Every
extra piece of glass can add flare and ghost images, especially at night with bright
lights in the frame. Its one real use is protection from sea spray, sand and
scratches. A lens hood also protects the front glass, and it reduces flare.

### What editing can and can't replace

| Effect | Can you do it in editing? |
|---|---|
| Removing reflections from water and glass | No. Detail hidden under a reflection was never recorded |
| Removing glare from leaves | Not properly, for the same reason |
| A darker blue sky | Partly, with the colour mixer or a sky mask |
| A long exposure | Partly: stacking many short frames imitates it in some scenes |
| A bright sky held back | Mostly: bracket and merge, or a graduated mask |
| A UV filter's effect | Nothing to replace |

## How to use it

- **Polariser:** look at the screen or viewfinder and turn the filter slowly. Stop
  when the reflection or the sky looks right. Check again after you change the camera
  angle or turn it to vertical. Take it off when you don't need it.
- **Strong ND:** compose and focus first, because the view goes very dark once the
  filter is on. Switch to manual focus so the camera doesn't hunt. Then fit the filter,
  lengthen the shutter by the filter's stops, and use a tripod.
- **Keep filters clean.** A dirty or cheap filter can soften a sharp lens.

## On your kit

- **Check each device yourself before relying on a filter.** Whether the L10's lens
  has a filter thread, and its size, should be in the manual's specifications. Check
  whether DJI makes ND or polarising filters for the Pocket 3 and the Action 5 Pro.
  Phones generally need a clip-on or case-mounted system.
- **L10:** there's no built-in ND. For f/1.7 in bright sun, the electronic shutter (to
  1/32000 s) solves the problem without a filter. For a long exposure in daylight,
  nothing replaces an ND.
- **Wide views:** on the iPhone's 13 mm lens and the Action 5 Pro's 155° view, a
  polariser will darken the sky unevenly.

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving the polariser on all the time.** It costs light and can remove reflections
  you wanted, such as puddles after rain. Use it on purpose.
- **Polarising the sky with an ultra-wide lens.** You get an uneven dark band.
- **Expecting editing to remove reflections later.** Do it at capture or not at all.
- **Fitting a UV filter for image quality.** It can only add flare.
- **Focusing after fitting a strong ND.** The view is too dark. Focus first.
- **Stacking filters on a wide lens.** The corners go dark.

## Try it

Twenty minutes at a pond, a canal or a shop window. No polariser? Polarised sunglasses
work the same way.

1. Look at the water at a shallow angle through the filter or the sunglasses. Turn it
   slowly and watch the reflections fade and return.
2. Look at the blue sky at 90° to the sun and turn it again. Then look towards the sun
   and compare.
3. Hold the sunglasses tight against the iPhone's main lens and shoot a pair of frames:
   reflection in, reflection out.
4. Try to turn the "reflection in" frame into the other one in editing. Write down why
   you can't.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why can a polariser remove the reflection on a pond when editing can't?
> The polariser blocks the reflected light before it reaches the sensor, so the camera
> records what is under the surface. In a file, the reflection has already replaced
> that detail.

> [!question]- You polarise the sky with the sun directly behind you, and almost nothing changes. Why?
> Skylight is most polarised at about 90° to the sun. Directly away from the sun it
> is barely polarised, so turn to face across the sun's direction.

> [!question]- Daylight needs f/8, 1/125 s, ISO 100. What shutter speed do you need with a 10-stop ND?
> Ten stops is 1024 times the light, so about 8 seconds (1/125 s × 1024).

> [!question]- Why does a graduated ND struggle with a city skyline?
> The tall buildings rise above the horizon line and get darkened along with the sky.
> Bracketing and merging, or a mask in editing, handles that shape better.

> [!question]- Is a UV filter worth keeping on the L10?
> For image quality, no: digital sensors already block most UV, and the extra glass can
> add flare. It can make sense as protection at the beach, against spray and sand.
