# Direction of Light

> [!summary] In one line
> Where the light comes from decides where the shadows fall, and shadows are how we see
> shape and texture. Change the direction and you change the shape, the texture and the
> mood of the same subject.

## What it is

Direction is described from the camera's point of view: where is the light compared
with the line from the camera to the subject? Height matters too: above, level or
below.

| Direction | Where the light is | What it does | Mood |
|---|---|---|---|
| Front | Behind or next to the camera | Few visible shadows; shape and texture flatten | Open, plain, can look flat |
| 45° | Halfway between front and side, a little above | Shadow on one side of the nose and face | Natural, the classic portrait |
| Side | 90° to the camera | Half lit, half in shadow; strongest texture | Dramatic, moody |
| Back | Behind the subject, facing the camera | Front in shadow; outline glows; silhouettes | Atmospheric, mysterious |
| Rim | Behind and to one side | A bright line along hair, shoulders or edges | Separates subject from background |
| Top | Straight overhead | Dark eye sockets; shadows under nose and chin | Harsh, tired, or theatrical |
| Under | Below the subject | Shadows fall upwards | Eerie, unnatural |

## How it works

Your brain reads three-dimensional shape from shading: the gradual change from light to
shadow across a surface. Direction decides how much of that shading the camera can see.

- **Front light.** Shadows fall straight behind the subject, hidden from the camera.
  With no shading, a round face looks flat. Colour and detail show evenly, which is why
  front light suits documents and catalogue shots.
- **45° and side light.** As the light moves sideways, shadows come into view and shape
  appears. Light that skims across a surface (*raking* light) makes every small bump
  cast a long shadow, so texture jumps out.
- **Back and rim light.** The camera now sees the shadow side. Edges catch light, and
  thin or translucent things glow: hair, leaves, steam, a glass of tea. Dust and rain
  become visible.
- **Top and under light.** We grew up with light from above, mostly the sun. Light from
  directly overhead hides the eyes. Light from below reverses the normal shading, so
  the face reads as wrong and unsettling.

Portrait photographers name the patterns a light makes on a face, with the light a
little above eye level:

| Pattern | Light position | The tell-tale shadow | Suits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Butterfly (Paramount) | In front, high above the camera | A small, butterfly-shaped shadow under the nose | Beauty and glamour; shows cheekbones. Deep-set eyes can go dark |
| Loop | About 30–45° to the side | The nose shadow makes a small loop towards the cheek | Most faces; the safe, everyday portrait |
| Rembrandt | About 45° to the side, higher | A small triangle of light on the shadow-side cheek | Moody, painterly character portraits |
| Split | 90° to the side | Exactly half the face lit | Drama, mystery, strong character |
| Broad | Face turned a little from the camera; light on the side turned *towards* the camera | The far, narrower side of the face is in shadow | Widens the face; suits thin faces and bright, open portraits |
| Short | Face turned a little from the camera; light on the side turned *away* from the camera | The near, wider side of the face is in shadow | Slims the face and adds shape; flattering on most people, especially round faces |

Butterfly is also called Paramount lighting, after the Hollywood studio's glamour
portraits. Broad and short are not extra positions: they say which side of a turned
face the light reaches, so they combine with the patterns above. A loop light can be
broad or short. Ask whether the lit cheek is the one nearer the camera (broad) or
further from it (short).

### Reading direction from a photo

- **Shadows** point away from the light. Follow a shadow back past its object and keep
  going: that's where the light is. Long shadows mean a low light; short ones, a high
  light.
- **Catchlights** are the small reflections of the light in the eyes. Their position
  shows the direction: a catchlight in the upper left of the eye (as you look at the
  photo) means the light was up and to the camera's left. Their shape shows the source
  (a window makes a rectangle, the sun a tiny dot). Their size shows how big the source
  was, so a large catchlight usually means soft light.

## How to use it

1. **Decide what the photo needs.** Shape: 45° or side. Texture: side, low and raking.
   A simple, even record: front. Separation from the background: rim or back. Drama:
   side, or under for something strange.
2. **Keep the main light a little above eye level** for natural-looking faces, because
   that's where we expect light to come from.
3. **Move the subject instead of the light.** You can't move the sun or a window. Turn
   the person, move them, or walk around them. Turning a face 45° away from a window
   changes front light into 45° light. The same sun is front light from one side of
   your subject and backlight from the other.
4. **Decide on backlight before you shoot.** Expose for the face and let the background
   go bright, add fill to the face, or expose for the background and make a silhouette.

### A2.1's eight positions

A2.1 asks for one window, one subject, eight positions. A simple layout: keep the
subject about 1 m from a window with no direct sun coming in. Walk the camera around
them in 45° steps, and have them turn to face the camera each time.

| Position | Where the window is, seen from the camera | Expect |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Directly behind you | Front light |
| 2 | 45° to your left | 45° light |
| 3 | 90° to your left | Side light |
| 4 | 135° to your left, behind the subject | Rim light |
| 5 | Directly behind the subject | Backlight |
| 6 | 135° to your right | Rim light, other side |
| 7 | 90° to your right | Side light, other side |
| 8 | 45° to your right | 45° light, other side |

In position 1 your body may block part of the window, so stand to one side of it. A
window can't give top or under light: find those at midday outdoors, or with a phone
screen held low.

## Common mistakes

- **Always keeping the sun behind you.** It's the old safe rule, but it gives flat
  front light and makes people squint. Turn them until shadows give the face shape.
- **Putting the light too high or too low.** Too high hides the eyes; too low looks
  like a horror film. Start a little above eye level.
- **Shooting backlight without a plan.** The camera exposes for the bright background
  and the face goes dark. Choose: expose for the face, fill it, or make a silhouette.
- **Moving the light when moving the subject is easier.** One step or a quarter turn
  often fixes the light.
- **Ignoring catchlights.** Eyes without them look dull. Turn the face towards the
  light until a catchlight appears.

## Try it

Thirty minutes, any of your devices: a small version of **A2.1**. Use a person, or a
round object with a face drawn on it, such as a ball or an egg. Shoot the eight
positions in the table above with the same framing. Label each frame with its
direction and its quality (hard or soft). Pick the frame that best shows shape, and
write one sentence on why it wins.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why does a flash on top of the camera make faces look flat?
> The light comes from the camera's position, so every shadow falls straight behind
> the subject, where the camera can't see it. With no visible shading, the face loses
> its shape.

> [!question]- The catchlights in a portrait are rectangles high in the upper right of each eye. What do you know about the light?
> It was a rectangular source, most likely a window, placed above and to the camera's
> right. Large catchlights would also tell you the source looked big, so the light was
> probably soft.

> [!question]- Midday in Singapore, and your friend's eyes are dark pits. What is happening, and what can you do with no gear?
> The sun is almost straight overhead, so this is top light: the brow shades the eyes.
> Move them into open shade, facing the bright open side, or stand them where a pale
> sunlit wall or pavement bounces light into their eye sockets.

> [!question]- You want to show the weave of a rattan basket. Where do you put the light?
> Low and to the side, so it skims across the surface. Each strand then casts a small
> shadow, and the texture becomes visible.

> [!question]- The low sun is behind your friend and the camera makes their face dark. What are your three options?
> Expose for the face and let the background go bright; add fill from a reflector or
> flash; or expose for the sky and make a silhouette. That choice is A2.5.
