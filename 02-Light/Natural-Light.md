# Natural Light

> [!summary] In one line
> Natural light is whatever the sun and sky give you: direct, diffused by cloud, bounced
> off a wall, or framed by a window. You can't move it, so the skill is to read it, find
> the best of it, and move yourself and your subject into it.

## What it is

The sun is the only source. Everything else is that light changed on its way to you.
Each kind can be described with the module's four words: quality, direction, colour
and contrast.

| Light | Quality | Direction | Colour | Contrast |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Window light | Soft close to a big window | From the window's side | Daylight; bluer if only sky | Moderate; drops fast into the room |
| Open shade | Soft | From the open side | Cool; green under trees | Low to moderate |
| Overcast | Very soft | Mostly from above | Cool | Low |
| Harsh midday sun | Hard | Almost straight down | Neutral | Very high |
| Golden hour | Hard but gentler | Low, from the side or behind | Warm orange | Moderate |
| Blue hour | Very soft | From the whole sky | Deep blue | Low |
| City light at night | Mostly hard pools | From signs, lamps, windows | Mixed | High |

## How it works

**Direct sun** is a tiny source, so it is hard (→ [[Quality-of-Light]]). In Singapore,
about 1° north of the equator, the noon sun is never lower than about 65° above the
horizon. It is highest around 13:00, not 12:00, because the clocks run about an hour
ahead of the sun. So the harshest overhead light runs from late morning to
mid-afternoon.

**Open shade** is the shade of a building or tree with open sky beside it. The sun's
disc is blocked, so the subject is lit by a big patch of bright sky: a large source, so
soft light. Unlike overcast, it still has a direction, coming from the open side.

**Overcast** turns the whole cloud layer into one huge diffuser. The light is very
soft, but it still comes mostly from above, so heavy cloud can leave dark eye sockets.
Anything overhead, such as an awning or a tree, blocks that top light and makes the
light come from the side.

**Window light** uses the window as the source. Its size and your distance from it set
the quality, and it falls off quickly into the room. Direct sun shining through the
glass makes hard patches instead. In Singapore the sun sits in the northern half of the
sky for part of the year and the southern half for the rest. So the European advice to
use a north-facing window doesn't simply apply here. East windows get morning sun and
west windows get afternoon sun.

**Golden hour** is the time just after sunrise and before sunset. The low sun's light
passes through far more air, which removes blue and dims it, so it is warmer and
gentler. The low angle gives long shadows and makes side light and backlight easy.
Near the equator the sun rises and sets steeply, so golden hour lasts only about 20–30
minutes.

**Blue hour** comes after sunset (and before sunrise). The sun is below the horizon, so
the only light is the deep blue sky. It is very soft and balances well with warm city
lights, but in Singapore it lasts only about 15–25 minutes.

**After rain**, the air is cleaner because rain washes out dust and haze. Distant views
are sharper. Wet surfaces look darker and more saturated, because the water film reduces
the white glare that dry, rough surfaces scatter. Puddles become mirrors.

**City light at night** is many small, hard sources of different colours. Light falls
in pools, with darkness between them.

## How to use it

### Read the light before you raise the camera

Ask the four questions, in order:

1. **Quality:** are the shadow edges sharp or soft?
2. **Direction:** where do the shadows point? Where are the catchlights?
3. **Colour:** what is lighting the shadows: blue sky, green trees, an orange lamp?
4. **Contrast:** how dark are the shadows? Squint: it hides detail and shows contrast.

A quick trick: hold your hand out flat and turn it slowly. You'll see where the light
comes from and how hard it is, before you take a single frame.

### Where to find good light

- **Window:** put the subject close to the window and turn them about 45° to it. Switch
  off the room lights, so their colour doesn't mix in.
- **Open shade:** place the subject just inside the shade line, facing the open sky.
  HDB void decks and covered walkways are ready-made open shade, often with light
  bouncing up off the ground. Keep sunlit areas out of the background.
- **Overcast:** keep the white sky out of the frame. Use an awning or a tree to block
  the top light.
- **Midday:** find shade, shoot backlit, or use the hard light on purpose for strong
  graphic shadows, such as railings and grilles. A pale sunlit wall works as a huge
  reflector for anyone facing it.
- **Golden hour:** choose side light or backlight, and check where the sun will drop
  behind buildings. Arrive 30 minutes early.
- **Blue hour:** brace the camera or use a tripod. Shoot when the sky and the city
  lights look equally bright.
- **After rain:** go out as the storm clears. Look for reflections, wet colour and
  breaking clouds.
- **City at night:** wait for a person to step into a pool of light. Expose for the
  bright areas and let the rest go dark.

### Weather and storms

Singapore gets frequent heavy afternoon and evening thunderstorms. A dark storm cloud
with sun on the buildings in front of it gives some of the most dramatic light you'll
see. But lightning is common here, so take shelter during a storm. Stay off open
fields, rooftops and beaches until it passes. Haze flattens contrast and hides distant
views; on hazy days, work close.

## On your kit

- **L10 in bright sun:** f/1.7 at base ISO needs a shutter faster than 1/2000 s, the
  mechanical limit. Switch to the electronic shutter (to 1/32000 s), or close the
  aperture. There's no built-in ND filter. → [[Lens-Filters]]
- **Rain:** the L10 isn't splash-resistant. Use the Action 5 Pro (waterproof) or the
  iPhone (IP68) in wet weather.
- **Low light:** for stills at blue hour or night, the L10's larger sensor wins. For
  low-light video, the Pocket 3 is your best camera.
- **Humidity:** gear from an air-conditioned room fogs up outside. Let it warm up in a
  closed bag first.

## Common mistakes

- **Facing someone into the midday sun.** They squint, and top light hides their eyes.
  Move them into open shade.
- **Going too deep into the shade.** The light gets dim and flat. Stay near the edge,
  facing the open side.
- **Letting a sunlit background into a shade portrait.** Exposed for the face, it turns
  white. Turn until the background is in shade too.
- **Staying in when the weather turns.** Some of the best light comes just after a
  storm.
- **Ignoring the colour of shade.** Faces in shade go blue, or green under trees. Fix
  white balance, or move.

## Try it

Thirty minutes, any device: a light walk around your block. Find and photograph one
example of each: open shade, hard sun, bounced light (a sunlit wall lighting something
in shade), and window light or a void deck. For each frame, write the four words:
quality, direction, colour, contrast. It's practice for the Gate 2 test, describing
light in four words, cold.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Both are lit by the sun, so why is open shade so much softer than direct sun?
> In open shade the sun's disc is blocked, and the light comes from a large area of
> bright sky. A large source gives soft light; the sun's small disc gives hard light.

> [!question]- On an overcast day, faces still have dark eye sockets. Why, and how do you fix it?
> Cloud light still comes mostly from above, so the brow shades the eyes. Move the
> person under an awning or the edge of a tree, which blocks the top light and lets
> light reach them from the side.

> [!question]- Why is the harshest light in Singapore around 13:00 rather than noon?
> Singapore's clocks run about an hour ahead of the sun, so the sun reaches its highest
> point around 13:00 by the clock.

> [!question]- You want soft window light, but direct sun is shining through the window. What are your options?
> Draw a thin white curtain across it, use another window, wait for the sun to move,
> or move the subject out of the sunbeam into the light bouncing off the room.

> [!question]- A storm clears at 18:30. Why grab the camera?
> The air is clean, wet surfaces are rich in colour, puddles reflect, and low sun under
> breaking clouds can light buildings against a dark sky. It won't last long.
