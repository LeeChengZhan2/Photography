# Off-Camera Flash

> [!summary] In one line
> Taking the flash off the camera lets you choose where the light comes from and how
> far away it is. That gives you control of shape, contrast and fall-off; the price is
> a way to trigger it and something to hold it.

## What it is

With off-camera flash, the flash sits on a stand, a clamp or a friend's hand, and the
camera fires it remotely. You can put the light anywhere: at 45° for shape, at the side
for drama, behind the subject for a glowing edge. With one flash and the ideas in
[[Direction-of-Light]], you can make most classic portrait lighting.

## How it works

### Triggering the flash

| Method | How it works | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Radio | A transmitter on the hot shoe sends a radio signal to a receiver on, or built into, the flash | Reliable; works around corners, in sun, over long distances; some systems set power, TTL and HSS remotely | Transmitter and receiver must be compatible |
| Optical | A flash on the camera sends light pulses; a sensor on the remote flash sees them | Built into many flashes | Needs line of sight; weak in bright sun |
| Cable | A sync cable links the camera and the flash | Simple and cheap | Short; people trip over it |

Simple optical slaves fire at the first flash they see. That can be someone else's
flash, or the TTL pre-flash, which fires them too early. Some slaves have a mode that
ignores the pre-flash.

With some radio systems you change the remote flash's power from the camera. With
simpler triggers you walk over and change it on the flash.

### Holding the light

A light stand with a flash bracket is the usual choice. A clamp on a railing, a door
or a shelf works too, and so does a friend. Outdoors, weigh the stand down with a bag,
because wind catches umbrellas and topples stands.

### Distance and direction

- **Direction** decides where the shadows fall, and so the shape of the face.
- **Distance** decides softness and fall-off. A light with a modifier close to the
  subject is softer and brighter, and the background falls away darker. Further away it
  is harder and more even. → [[Inverse-Square-Law]]
- **Height:** a little above eye level looks natural, like the sun or a ceiling lamp.

### Feathering

**Feathering** means aiming the light so the subject is lit by the edge of the beam,
not its centre. Most lights are brightest in the middle. Turning the light slightly
away does three things:

1. The light on the subject is gentler, with a smoother change into shadow.
2. Less light hits the background or the floor.
3. With two people at different distances, aim the centre at the further one, so the
   nearer one gets the weaker edge and both come out evenly.

## How to use it

### One-light setups

| Setup | Where the flash goes | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Key at 45° | 45° to one side, a little above eye level, angled down | Classic shape; a loop or Rembrandt pattern on the face |
| Side light | 90° to one side, about eye level | Half the face in shadow; drama and texture |
| Backlight or rim | Behind the subject, hidden from the camera, aimed at their back or hair | A glowing outline that separates them from the background |
| High front | Above the camera, angled down | Glamour look; a small shadow under the nose |
| Under light | Below the face, aimed up | Strange and unsettling, on purpose |

A working order:

1. **Set the ambient exposure first**, at or below the sync speed. Decide how dark the
   background should be. → [[Balancing-Flash-and-Ambient]]
2. **Place the light** at 45°, about 1–1.5 m from the subject, a little above eye level.
3. **Set manual power.** Start at about 1/8, take a test shot, and adjust in whole stops.
4. **Fine-tune with distance and feathering.**
5. **Change one thing at a time**, then move the light to the next position.

A bare flash gives hard light. A shoot-through umbrella or a softbox close to the
subject makes it soft. → [[Light-Modifiers]]

### Beyond one light

Bigger setups add lights one at a time, and each extra light has one job:

| Light | Where it goes | What it adds |
|---|---|---|
| Fill | Near the camera, or on the shadow side, 1–2 stops weaker than the key | Lifts the shadows and lowers the [[Contrast-Ratio]] |
| Hair or rim light | Behind the subject, high or to one side, hidden from the lens | A bright edge that separates dark hair or clothes from a dark background |
| Background light | Behind the subject, aimed at the background | Sets the background's brightness separately from the face, or a glow behind the head |

The syllabus keeps you to one light on purpose (**A2.6**). With one source you see
exactly what each move does, and a reflector or a white wall can already do the fill's
job. More lights are a next step after the course.

## On your kit

What the L10 needs, in general terms:

- **A flash that can fire away from the camera**, with a built-in radio receiver or
  optical slave, or a separate receiver.
- **Something in the hot shoe to fire it:** a radio transmitter, a flash acting as an
  optical master, or a sync cable connection. The L10 has no built-in flash, so it
  can't send an optical signal on its own.
- **For TTL or HSS off the camera**, a trigger that works with the camera's flash
  system. For manual power, simple triggers usually work. Check the trigger's
  documentation and the L10's manual.
- **The mechanical shutter**, because the L10 won't fire a flash with the electronic
  one, and a shutter speed at or below the [[Sync-Speed]].

The other three devices can't fire a flash. Until you have one, practise every setup
above with a desk lamp or an LED panel on a stand: the light falls the same way.

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving a small modifier far away.** It turns hard again. Bring it close.
- **Putting the light at eye level or below by default.** It looks odd. Start a little
  above eye level.
- **An unweighted stand outdoors.** Wind plus an umbrella knocks it over. Weigh it down
  or have someone hold it.
- **An optical slave firing on the TTL pre-flash.** Set the master to manual, use a
  slave mode that ignores the pre-flash, or use radio.
- **Changing several things at once.** You won't know what made the difference. Move
  one thing, shoot, then the next.

## Try it

Thirty minutes, one person, one light, no reflector: a small version of **A2.6**. Keep
the camera settings fixed. Shoot the five setups in the table above, changing only the
light's position and power. For each frame, write one word for the feel. No flash yet?
Use a desk lamp on a shelf or a stack of books as the light.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why is a flash on a stand at 45° more flattering than the same flash on the camera?
> From 45°, the light creates shading across the face, which shows its shape. On the
> camera, the shadows fall straight behind the subject and the face looks flat.

> [!question]- Your remote optical slave fires, but the photo shows no flash. What happened?
> It probably fired on the TTL pre-flash, before the shutter opened. Set the master to
> manual, use a slave mode that ignores the pre-flash, or switch to radio.

> [!question]- Two people sit side by side with one light on the left, and the right-hand person is darker. How does feathering help?
> Aim the centre of the beam at the right-hand person. The left-hand person, who is
> closer, then gets the weaker edge of the beam, and the two come out more evenly lit.

> [!question]- Why can't the L10 fire an optical slave by itself?
> It has no built-in flash to send a light signal. It needs a flash or a radio
> transmitter in the hot shoe, or a sync cable.

> [!question]- In a one-light portrait indoors, the background is too bright. How do you darken it without changing the face?
> Use a faster shutter, up to the sync speed, to cut the ambient light. Move the subject
> further from the wall, or feather the light away from it. A light moved closer to the
> subject at lower power also makes the background fall off faster.
