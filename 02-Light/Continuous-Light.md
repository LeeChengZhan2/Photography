# Continuous Light

> [!summary] In one line
> Continuous lights stay on, so you see exactly what you'll get, and they work for
> video as well as stills. They are much weaker than flash, and cheap ones can have
> poor colour and flicker on camera.

## What it is

A continuous light is any light that stays on: an LED panel, a lamp, an LED tube, a
ring light, a desk lamp, even a window. The same four properties apply as for any light
(quality, direction, colour, contrast). The difference from flash is time.

| | Continuous light | Flash |
|---|---|---|
| See the result before you shoot | Yes | No |
| Works for video | Yes | No |
| Brightness | Low | Very high, for an instant |
| Freezes motion | No; only the shutter does | Yes; the short burst does |
| Shutter speed changes its exposure | Yes, like any ambient light | Hardly at all |
| Works with all four of your devices | Yes | Only the L10 |

## How it works

### What you see is what you get

The light is on while you compose, so you can see the shadows, the highlights, the
catchlights and any reflections before you press. The camera meters it like any other
light. That makes it ideal for learning, and it's why A2.2 and A2.3 use a phone torch.

### Why it's so much weaker

A flash releases its stored energy in about a thousandth of a second. A continuous
light spreads its power over the whole time it's on. So during a 1/125 s exposure,
even a small flash usually delivers several stops more light than a small LED panel.
In practice you raise the ISO, open the aperture, or slow the shutter and risk blur.
Moving the light closer helps most: half the distance gives two stops more light.
→ [[Inverse-Square-Law]]

### Colour accuracy: CRI and TLCI

A white LED doesn't produce an even spread of colours. Its light can have gaps, so
some colours, especially deep reds and skin tones, look dull. Its white can lean green
or magenta. Two scores measure this, both out of 100:

- **CRI (colour rendering index)** compares how colours look under the light with how
  they look under a reference light. The usual score, *Ra*, averages eight soft test
  colours and leaves out strong red (*R9*). A light with a good Ra can still render
  red poorly, so check R9 if it's listed.
- **TLCI (Television Lighting Consistency Index)** is built around how video cameras
  see colour.

Aim for about **95 or higher** on either. Lower scores mean duller skin and more colour
correction later.

### Bi-colour and RGB

- **Bi-colour** panels mix warm and cool white LEDs to set the colour temperature,
  often across a range of roughly 3200–5600 K. They are simple and usually good on
  skin.
- **RGB** panels mix red, green and blue LEDs and can make any colour, which replaces
  gels for creative colour. Whites made from RGB alone tend to render colour worse, so
  better panels add white LEDs too (often sold as RGBWW).

### Flicker and 50 Hz mains

Singapore's mains electricity runs at 50 Hz, so many mains-powered lights pulse 100
times a second. Your eyes don't notice, but the camera can:

- **Video:** if the shutter speed doesn't capture whole pulses, the picture pulses or
  dark bands roll through it. At 1/50 s or 1/100 s, every frame catches whole pulses,
  so the flicker disappears. That is one reason 25 and 50 fps suit Singapore.
  → [[Shutter-Angle]]
- **Stills:** at fast shutter speeds, each frame catches a different part of the pulse,
  so brightness and colour change from frame to frame. A rolling shutter can also show
  bands across the frame.
- **Battery lights** avoid mains flicker, but some cheap ones dim by switching on and
  off very fast, which can flicker too. Test a light at the speeds you plan to use.

Old tungsten bulbs flicker very little, because the hot filament keeps glowing between
pulses.

## How to use it

### Free and cheap alternatives

- **A desk lamp:** hard when bare, soft when bounced off a wall.
- **A phone torch:** a small, hard source for A2.2 and A2.3.
- **A window with a thin curtain:** the original continuous softbox.
- **A laptop or TV showing a blank white page:** a weak, fairly soft light for close-ups.

> [!warning] Heat
> Tungsten and halogen lamps get very hot. Never cover them with paper or cloth.

### When continuous light beats flash

- **Video**, because a flash can't light it.
- **Learning**, because you see the result as you move the light.
- **Still life, food and products**, because the subject doesn't move: use a tripod and
  a slow shutter, and watch the reflections on shiny objects.
- **Quiet situations**, where repeated bursts would bother people.

### When flash wins

- **Freezing motion:** children, pets and sport indoors.
- **Competing with the sun** outdoors.
- **A lot of light from a small, battery-powered unit.**

## On your kit

- **All four devices** work with continuous light, unlike flash, which only the L10
  can use. It is the Roadmap's substitute for week 8 if you don't have a flash.
- **For video under mains lights**, shoot 25 or 50 fps at 1/50 s or 1/100 s.
  → [[Frame-Rate]]
- **Rolling shutters:** the iPhone, Pocket 3 and Action 5 Pro use one for every shot,
  and so does the L10's electronic shutter. Under flickering LEDs they can show bands.
  For L10 stills, switch to the mechanical shutter if bands appear.
- **The iPhone's LED** is a continuous light you already own.

## Common mistakes

- **Judging a light by brightness alone.** Check its CRI or TLCI, and test it on skin.
- **Filming at 1/60 s under 50 Hz lights.** Expect flicker. Use 1/50 s or 1/100 s.
- **Expecting continuous light to freeze motion.** It can't; only a fast shutter does.
- **Mixing a daylight panel with warm room lights.** You get two colours. Match the
  panel's colour to the room, or switch the room lights off.
- **Placing the light far away.** It gets weaker and harder. Bring it close.

## Try it

Twenty minutes, in a room with mains-powered LED ceiling lights:

1. On the L10 or the Pocket 3, with the shutter set manually, film 5 seconds at 25 fps
   and 1/50 s. Then 5 seconds each at 1/60, 1/200 and 1/500 s.
2. Watch the clips at full size. Note which speeds show pulsing or bands.
3. Then switch off the ceiling lights and light a mug with a desk lamp: bare, through
   baking paper close to the mug, and bounced off a wall. Compare the shadow edges.

You'll meet the flicker test again in **A5.1**, and the lamp steps are **A2.2**.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why does an LED panel need a higher ISO than a flash for the same photo?
> A continuous light spreads its power over the whole time it's on, so during a short
> exposure it delivers far less light than a flash burst. The camera makes up the
> difference with ISO, aperture or a slower shutter.

> [!question]- Indoors under LED ceiling lights, your video at 1/60 s shows rolling dark bands. Why, and what is the fix?
> The 50 Hz mains makes the lights pulse 100 times a second. At 1/60 s, frames don't
> capture whole pulses. Use 1/50 s or 1/100 s.

> [!question]- A light has a CRI of 96, yet skin looks dull under it. What might explain that?
> The usual CRI score (Ra) leaves out strong red (R9), which matters for skin. The
> light may render red poorly despite its good average. Check R9 or the TLCI.

> [!question]- When is a desk lamp a better choice than a flash, and when isn't it?
> For still life, video and learning, because you see the light as you move it. Not
> for moving subjects or for competing with the sun, where you need flash's brief,
> powerful burst.
