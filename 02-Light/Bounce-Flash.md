# Bounce Flash

> [!summary] In one line
> Bouncing means pointing the flash at a ceiling or wall instead of at the subject. The
> lit patch on that surface becomes a large, soft light, so a small, harsh flash turns
> into gentle, directional light, at the cost of power.

## What it is

A flash head is only a few centimetres wide, so straight at the subject it gives hard
light. Point it at a ceiling or a wall instead, and the light spreads into a patch a
metre or more wide. That patch becomes the new light source. You need a flash whose
head tilts up and, ideally, turns sideways.

## How it works

### Why bare straight-on flash flattens faces

Direct flash from the camera causes four problems at once:

- **Flat shape.** The light comes from almost the same place as the lens, so shadows
  fall straight behind the subject, where the camera can't see them. With no shading,
  the face looks flat. → [[Direction-of-Light]]
- **Hard edges.** The small head throws a sharp shadow outline on the wall behind and
  makes shiny spots on skin.
- **Red-eye.** Light goes straight into the pupils, reflects off the back of the eye,
  and comes straight back into the nearby lens.
- **A dark background.** The subject is close to the flash and the background is far,
  so fall-off turns the room behind into a black cave.

Bouncing fixes all four. The source becomes big (soft), it comes from above or the side
(shape), it is far from the lens axis (no red-eye), and the light spread around the room
lights the background more evenly.

### Where the light comes from

The subject is lit from the bounce patch, not from the flash. So:

| Bounce off | Light comes from | Look |
|---|---|---|
| The ceiling in front of you | Above and in front | Even, natural indoor light |
| A side wall | The side | More shape, more mood |
| The wall or ceiling behind you | Roughly the camera's direction, but large | Soft, flattering front light |

A matte surface scatters light in all directions, so the light comes from wherever the
patch is. Aim so the patch sits between you and the subject, a little in front of
them. Tilting the head about 45° towards the subject does this on a normal ceiling.
Pointing straight up works when the subject is a few metres away. If they're close,
the patch ends up nearly overhead and their eyes fall into shadow.

### Colour

The surface tints the light. A yellow wall gives yellow skin; a wooden ceiling gives
orange. White and light grey are neutral. You can correct one colour with white
balance, but not if other light in the frame needs a different correction.

### The power cost

The light travels further (flash to ceiling to subject), spreads over a large patch,
and the surface absorbs some of it. You often need two or three stops more power than
for direct flash, and more under a high or dark ceiling. TTL makes up the difference
until the flash runs out of power.

### Bounce cards and bouncing behind you

A **bounce card** is a small white card on the flash head; some flashes have one built
in. It kicks a little light forward, mainly to put a catchlight in the eyes, while most
of the light still goes to the ceiling. It is small, so it doesn't make the light soft.

**Bouncing behind you** means turning the head to hit the wall or ceiling behind you or
over your shoulder. It works like a big window at your back: soft front light. Stop any
direct light from the head reaching the subject with a small black card or a piece of
black foam on the flash, so only the bounce lights them.

## How to use it

1. **Look around before you shoot.** Find a white or light surface near the subject.
   Most homes have a low white ceiling, which is ideal.
2. **Choose the direction:** the ceiling for even light, a side wall for shape, the
   wall behind you for soft front light.
3. **Aim the head**, then use TTL, or manual power with a test shot.
4. **Check the result.** Dark eye sockets: tilt towards the subject or add a bounce
   card. A colour cast: pick another surface.
5. **When you turn the camera vertical**, turn the flash head too, so it still points
   at the same surface.

A starting point indoors: M mode, f/2.8–f/4, 1/60–1/100 s, ISO 400–1600. A higher ISO
lets more room light in and means less flash power, so it recycles faster.

## On your kit

Bounce needs an external flash with a tilting head on the L10's hot shoe; the L10 has
no built-in flash. The iPhone's LED is fixed to the phone and points wherever the lens
points, so it can't bounce while you shoot. You can still practise the idea now: aim
the iPhone torch at a white wall and photograph the result with the L10.

## Common mistakes

- **Bouncing off a coloured surface.** A green wall turns skin green. Use white or
  light grey.
- **Pointing straight up with the subject close.** The light comes from overhead and
  the eyes go dark. Tilt towards the subject.
- **Bouncing under a high or dark ceiling.** Very little comes back. Use a nearby light
  wall, a white board held by a friend, or take the flash off the camera.
- **Letting direct light spill from the head.** It adds a hard shadow. Flag the front
  of the flash with black card.
- **Trying to bounce outdoors.** There's nothing to bounce off. Use direct fill flash
  or [[Off-Camera-Flash]].

## Try it

Twenty minutes indoors, with someone sitting 2–3 m away. With a flash on the L10, shoot
the same frame five ways: direct, ceiling at 45°, ceiling straight up, a side wall, and
the wall behind you. For each, write the shadow edge, the direction and the colour.
Without a flash, do the same with a desk lamp or the iPhone torch aimed at the ceiling
and walls. That's also the "wall bounce" step of **A2.2**.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why does bouncing off a ceiling make a flash softer?
> The lit patch on the ceiling becomes the light source. Seen from the subject, it is
> far bigger than the flash head, and a bigger source gives softer light.

> [!question]- You bounce off a pale green wall and the skin looks sickly. Why, and what do you do?
> The wall reflects mostly green light, so the flash arrives green. Bounce off a white
> ceiling or wall instead.

> [!question]- In a hawker centre with a high, dark ceiling, bounce flash does almost nothing. Why?
> The light has a long way to travel, so it spreads out and weakens, and the dark
> surface absorbs most of what reaches it. Use a nearby light wall, available light,
> or an off-camera flash.

> [!question]- Your bounced flash leaves dark eye sockets. Name two fixes.
> Tilt the head towards the subject so the patch is in front of them, or add a bounce
> card for a catchlight and a little front light. Bouncing off the wall behind you
> also works.

> [!question]- Why does direct flash cause red-eye when bounce flash doesn't?
> Direct flash sits close to the lens, so light reflected from the back of the eye
> returns straight into it. Bounced light arrives from far off that line.
