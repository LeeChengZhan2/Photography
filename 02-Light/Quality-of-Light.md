# Quality of Light

> [!summary] In one line
> Light is hard or soft depending on how big the source looks from the subject. Hard
> light makes crisp shadows and shows texture; soft light makes gentle shadows and
> flatters skin.

## What it is

"Quality" describes the edge of a shadow. Look at the line where light turns into
shadow on a face, a cup or a wall:

| Quality | Shadow edge | Highlights | How it feels |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hard | Sharp, crisp line | Small, bright, glinting | Graphic, dramatic, harsh |
| Soft | Slow, blurred change | Large and gentle | Calm, flattering, quiet |

Quality is not the same as brightness. A dim candle gives hard light. A bright overcast
sky gives soft light.

## How it works

Every point on a light source throws its own shadow. A tiny source throws one shadow,
so the edge is sharp. A large source throws many slightly shifted shadows. They overlap
and blur the edge into a soft band between light and shadow.

What matters is not the source's real size but its **relative size**: how big it looks
from the subject. That depends on size *and* distance.

| Source | Distance | How wide it looks from the subject | Quality |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct sun | 150 million km | A small disc, about 0.5° across | Hard |
| Overcast sky | All around you | Most of the sky | Very soft |
| Bare bulb or phone torch | 1 m | A dot | Hard |
| Window 1 m wide | 1 m | About 50° | Soft |
| The same window | 4 m | About 14° | Much harder |

The sun is enormous, but it is so far away that it looks tiny. Clouds make it soft
because the whole cloud layer lights up and becomes the new, very large source.

There are three ways to make light softer. All three make the source bigger as seen
from the subject:

1. **Move the light closer.** Halve the distance and the source looks about twice as
   wide. It also gets brighter. → [[Inverse-Square-Law]]
2. **Diffuse it.** Put a translucent material (tracing paper, a thin white curtain)
   between the light and the subject. The diffuser glows and becomes the new source,
   so now *its* size and distance set the quality.
3. **Bounce it.** Aim the light at a large matte surface, such as a wall, a ceiling or
   a white board. That surface becomes the new source.

The reverse is also true. A large softbox moved far away becomes hard, because from the
subject it now looks small.

Soft light also *wraps*. Light from the edges of a big source reaches around a rounded
subject, so the shadow side gets some light too. That is why soft light often looks
lower in contrast, even though quality and [[Contrast-Ratio|contrast]] are separate
ideas.

## How to use it

Decide what the subject needs, then choose the quality:

- **Texture and shape** (brick, bark, fabric, wrinkles, bread crust): hard light,
  usually from the side, because every small bump then casts its own small shadow.
- **Skin and calm** (portraits, babies, flowers, products): soft light, because the
  small bumps cast no clear shadows, so pores and blemishes fade.
- **Graphic drama** (street, architecture): hard light, because strong shadows become
  shapes you can compose with. Expose for the bright areas.

A rule of thumb for soft light: make the source at least as wide as the subject, and
keep it no further away than about one to two times its own width. Further than that,
it starts to look small from the subject and the shadows sharpen.

Quick fixes on location:

- Harsh sun on a face → move the person into open shade, or wait for a cloud.
- Direct sun coming through a window → draw a thin white curtain across it.
- A harsh bare lamp → turn it to face a white wall and use the bounce.

## On your kit

Quality belongs to the light, not the camera, so all four devices see the same thing.
Two useful details:

- The **iPhone's LED torch** is a ready-made small, hard source for A2.2. Use it as the
  lamp and shoot with the L10 in manual mode, so you control the exposure.
- The **L10's hot shoe** takes an external flash. A bare flash head is only a few
  centimetres wide, so it is hard light unless you bounce or diffuse it.
  → [[Bounce-Flash]]

## Common mistakes

- **Thinking a bigger light is always softer.** Distance counts as much as size. A
  large softbox across the room can be harder than a small one close up.
- **Confusing soft with dim.** Quality is about the shadow edge, not brightness. Judge
  the edge, not the exposure.
- **Diffusing a light but leaving the diffuser small or far away.** The diffuser is now
  the source. Make it big and bring it close to the subject.
- **Using soft light for everything.** On a textured subject, soft light can look flat
  and dull. Hard side light is what makes texture visible.
- **Ignoring the background.** The face may be in soft light while a hard patch of sun
  on the wall behind steals attention. Check the whole frame.

## Try it

Twenty minutes, in a dark room. A small version of **A2.2**:

1. Put a textured object (an orange, a crumpled paper ball) on a table. Place the phone
   torch about 50 cm to one side, roughly level with it.
2. Shoot with the bare torch. Look at the shadow edge.
3. Tape a sheet of baking paper straight over the torch. Shoot again.
4. Now hold the same paper about 10 cm from the object, with the torch still 50 cm
   away behind it. Shoot again.
5. Finally, point the torch at a white wall or a large white card near the object, and
   shoot the bounced light.
6. Keep the aperture and ISO fixed; change only the shutter speed to keep the
   brightness similar. Describe each shadow edge in one word.

Step 3 barely changes anything. Step 4 changes a lot. Work out why before you read on.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- The sun is far bigger than any softbox. Why is direct sunlight hard?
> Because it is so far away that it looks tiny from the subject, a disc about half a
> degree wide. Quality depends on how big the source looks, not on its real size.

> [!question]- You tape tracing paper straight onto a phone torch. Why do the shadows hardly change?
> The glowing patch of paper is only a few centimetres wide, so the source is still
> tiny. A diffuser softens light only when it is large and close to the subject.

> [!question]- You move a softbox from 3 m to 1 m from a face. What happens to the quality and the brightness?
> The light gets softer, because the box now looks about three times wider from the
> face. It also gets much brighter, by roughly three stops, although a large source
> this close doesn't follow the inverse-square law exactly.

> [!question]- You want a portrait that shows every line in an old fisherman's face. Which light do you choose?
> Hard light from the side. A small source makes each wrinkle cast its own crisp
> shadow, and side light makes those shadows long enough to see.

> [!question]- It's overcast and you want to show the texture of a brick wall. What's the problem?
> Overcast light is very soft and comes from the whole sky, so the bricks cast almost no
> shadows. Come back when direct sun rakes across the wall from the side.
