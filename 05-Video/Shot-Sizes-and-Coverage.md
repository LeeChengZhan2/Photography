# Shot Sizes and Coverage

> [!summary] In one line
> Shot sizes describe how much of the subject fills the frame, and each size does a
> different job. Coverage means filming an action in enough sizes and angles that it
> can be cut together, because you can only edit the shots you took.

## What it is

Shot sizes are named by how much of a person the frame shows:

| Shot | Frames a person as | Its job |
|---|---|---|
| Extreme wide (EWS) | Tiny, in a big place | Where are we? |
| Wide (WS) | Head to feet, with surroundings | Who is where; what's happening |
| Medium (MS) | Waist up | The action and body language |
| Close-up (CU) | Head and shoulders | Emotion and reactions |
| Extreme close-up (ECU) | Eyes, a hand, a detail | Intensity; details the viewer would miss |

Four more terms you'll use:

- **Over-the-shoulder (OTS):** filmed from behind someone, with their shoulder in the
  foreground, looking at what they see.
- **Insert:** a close shot of a detail *inside* the action: the kettle switch, the spoon
  stirring, the key turning.
- **Cutaway:** a shot of something *outside* the main action: the clock, the street
  outside, someone watching. It lets you cover a cut or show time passing.
- **B-roll:** supporting footage played over the main sound, such as an interview or a
  voice-over. The interview itself is the A-roll.

**Coverage** means filming the same action from enough sizes and angles that an editor
can build a scene from it.

## How it works

### Why the size has to change at a cut

A cut is a jump from one shot to another. If two shots are the same size, from almost
the same angle, the subject seems to twitch on the spot. That's a jump cut, and it looks
like a mistake unless you mean it. → [[Cuts-and-Transitions]]

A clear change avoids it: wide to close, or the camera moved at least about 30° around
the subject. This is often called the 30-degree rule. The viewer then reads the cut as
a new view of the same moment, not a glitch.

### Coverage gives the editor choices

With one wide shot, your film lasts exactly as long as the action, and every fumble
stays in. With coverage you can:

- **Shorten time.** Making coffee takes three minutes. Cut from the wide to a close-up
  of the kettle, then to the pour, and a minute vanishes without anyone noticing.
- **Hide mistakes.** Cut away from a fumble to the face, then back.
- **Choose the best moment** from each angle.
- **Control the pace.** More, shorter shots feel faster. → [[Pacing-and-Rhythm]]

### The five-shot sequence

The five-shot sequence is a compact way to cover one person doing one task. It's
assignment A5.2:

| Shot | Answers | Example: making coffee |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Hands, close up | What is happening? | Spooning coffee into the cup |
| 2. Face, close up | Who is doing it? | Their eyes on the kettle |
| 3. Wide | Where are they? | The whole kitchen, with them in it |
| 4. Over the shoulder | What do they see? | Their view of the cup filling |
| 5. An unusual angle | Something fresh | Low from the counter, or straight down on the cup |

With these five you can cut 30 seconds that make sense with no words. The hands and the
face carry the story, the wide places it, and the other two add variety.

### Hold the shot, and leave handles

Hold every shot for at least about 10 seconds. The first and last moments are often
shaky, so the editor needs a clean stretch from the middle, and room to choose the
exact moment.

Leave **handles**: start recording a few seconds before the action begins, and keep
recording a few seconds after it ends. The editor needs those extra frames to place the
cut precisely, to let a dissolve overlap two shots, or to let the sound lead or trail
the picture.

## How to use it

1. **Start wide** to show the place, then work in: medium, close, inserts.
2. **Ask for the action again** for each size. A non-actor making coffee can make two
   or three cups. Keep the action the same each time (same hand, same order), so the
   shots match when you cut on the movement.
3. **Change the angle as well as the size** between set-ups: at least about 30°.
4. **Grab cutaways** before you leave: the place, the details, the people around.
5. **Hold each shot about 10 seconds**, with a few seconds of handles before and after.
6. **Shoot more close-ups than feels natural.** They carry emotion, and they read best
   on small phone screens.

A quick check before you leave a location: could an editor cut this action to half its
length? If not, shoot more inserts and cutaways.

## On your kit

Each device suits different sizes:

- **Lumix L10:** the 24–75 mm zoom covers wide to close from one spot. In AF Macro it
  focuses down to 3 cm at 24 mm, which suits extreme close-ups of hands and details.
- **iPhone 15 Pro Max:** 13, 24 and 120 mm. The 5× (120 mm) gets close-ups from a
  distance without crowding anyone. The ultra-wide focuses to about 2 cm for macro
  details.
- **Pocket 3:** a fixed 20 mm, so it's naturally wide. For close-ups, move in: it
  focuses from 20 cm.
- **Action 5 Pro:** 155°, with focus fixed from 35 cm. Ideal for the unusual angle: at
  ankle height, on a bike, in water. Poor for close-ups, since anything nearer than
  35 cm goes soft. Use Standard (Dewarp) when people or buildings are in the frame.

Moving closer and zooming in don't look the same, because distance changes the
perspective. → [[Focal-Length-and-Perspective]]

## Common mistakes

- **Shooting everything wide.** There's nothing to cut to. Get close, then closer.
- **Stopping the moment the action ends.** No handles, so no room to cut. Count to three
  before and after.
- **Shots of two or three seconds.** The editor can't find a clean piece. Hold for about
  10 seconds.
- **Two similar shots back to back.** The cut jumps. Change the size clearly, or move at
  least about 30°.
- **Forgetting cutaways.** You can't hide a mistake or skip time. Grab a few before you
  leave.

## Try it

A small version of **A5.2**, in 20 minutes, with one person making a drink:

1. Film the five shots from the table above. Hold each for 10 seconds, with handles.
2. Add two cutaways: something in the room, and something outside the window.
3. Cut the clips into a 20-second sequence, with no words.
4. Show it to someone and ask what happened. Note the shot you wish you'd taken.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why does a cut from a medium shot to a slightly tighter medium shot, from the same angle, look like a mistake?
> The change is too small, so the subject seems to twitch instead of the view changing.
> That's a jump cut. Change the size clearly, or move the camera at least about 30°,
> and the cut reads as a new view.

> [!question]- Your friend makes coffee in three minutes, and you need 30 seconds. How does coverage make that possible?
> You cut between sizes and angles and leave out the dull stretches. An insert of the
> kettle, then the face, then the pour: each cut can skip time without anyone noticing.
> With only one wide shot, you'd have to show it all, or jump.

> [!question]- What's the difference between an insert and a cutaway?
> An insert shows a detail inside the action, like the spoon stirring. A cutaway shows
> something outside it, like the street or someone watching. Both give you somewhere to
> cut to.

> [!question]- Why roll a few seconds before the action starts?
> To give the editor handles: room to pick the exact cut point, to overlap a dissolve,
> or to let the sound start before the picture. It also gives you and the camera time
> to settle.

> [!question]- Which device would you use for an extreme close-up of hands, and why not the Action 5 Pro?
> The L10 in AF Macro, or the iPhone's ultra-wide in macro. The Action 5 Pro's focus is
> fixed from 35 cm, so anything closer goes soft, and its 155° view makes hands small in
> the frame.
