# Camera Movement

> [!summary] In one line
> The camera can stay still, turn on the spot, or travel through the space. Each move
> says something to the viewer, so a move needs a reason, a steady speed, and a still
> frame at each end for the editor.

## What it is

| Move | What the camera does | What it tends to say |
|---|---|---|
| Static (locked off) | Doesn't move at all | "Watch this." Calm; the action does the moving |
| Pan | Turns left or right on the spot | Follows something, or reveals a space |
| Tilt | Turns up or down on the spot | Reveals height; looks up at or down on something |
| Push in | Travels towards the subject | Draws attention; builds tension |
| Pull out | Travels away from the subject | Reveals the context; an ending; loneliness |
| Tracking / follow | Travels with a moving subject: beside, behind or ahead | Keeps you with them; a journey |
| Orbit (arc) | Circles around the subject | Shows them from all sides; makes a moment feel big |
| Handheld | Held by hand, with natural movement | Urgency, closeness, "being there" |

A zoom is not a camera move. The camera stays put and the lens magnifies the picture.

## How it works

### Parallax: why travelling feels three-dimensional

When the camera travels, near objects slide across the frame faster than far ones.
That difference is called parallax, and it's how a flat screen shows depth. A push in
or a tracking shot has parallax, so it feels like moving through the space.

A pan or tilt turns the camera on the spot, so there's almost no parallax. It feels
like turning your head. A zoom has none at all: everything grows at the same rate, so
it looks flat, like enlarging a photo. → [[Focal-Length-and-Perspective]]

### Movement needs a motive

A move is **motivated** when something in the scene gives the camera a reason:

- **The subject moves:** you follow them.
- **There's something to reveal:** a pan from the cook to the queue at the stall.
- **There's a feeling to underline:** a slow push in as someone realises something.

Without a reason, the viewer starts noticing the camera instead of the subject.

### Speed and smoothness

At 25 fps with a 180° shutter, a fast pan judders. Each frame shows the scene in a very
different place, with too little blur to bridge the jumps. Your devices' rolling
shutters also make upright lines lean during fast pans. → [[Shutter-Angle]]

A common rule of thumb: let anything you pan across take at least about 7 seconds to
cross the frame. Keep the speed even, and ease in and out: start slowly, and slow down
before you stop.

### Start still, move, end still

Hold the frame still for 2–3 seconds before the move begins, and again after it ends.
The editor then has three choices: the still start, the move, or the still end. A move
that is already running at the first frame, or never settles, gives the editor no clean
place to cut.

## How to use it

1. **Default to static.** Move only when you can name the reason.
2. **One move per shot.** Panning, tilting and zooming all in one shot, like waving a
   garden hose, is called hosepiping. It looks undecided.
3. **Rehearse the move once** before you record, so you know where it starts and ends.
4. **Match the subject's speed** when you follow. Let them lead, with space ahead of
   them in the frame. → [[Screen-Direction]]
5. **Push in by walking, not zooming**, when you want the viewer to feel they're moving
   closer.
6. **Cut moving with moving, still with still.** A moving shot cut to another moving
   shot in the same direction flows. A sudden switch between moving and still feels
   like a bump, unless you want one.

## On your kit

- **Pocket 3:** your best camera for movement. Its gimbal modes:
  - **Follow:** for walking. The camera follows your turns smoothly.
  - **Tilt Locked:** holds the pitch steady, so the camera doesn't nod up and down as
    you move.
  - **FPV:** rolls with your movement, for a more energetic, swooping feel.
- **Action 5 Pro:** mount it on something that moves, such as a bike, for tracking and
  point-of-view shots. Its electronic stabilisation smooths the ride.
- **Lumix L10:** lens OIS for handheld moves. You can zoom, but a zoom during a shot
  reads as a zoom, not a move. Walk in when you want a push.
- **iPhone 15 Pro Max:** keep moves on the 1× or 2× lens, which have OIS. The 5× has
  OIS too but magnifies every wobble, and the 0.5× has no OIS, though its wide view
  hides some shake.

## Common mistakes

- **Moving because you can.** The viewer watches the camera instead of the subject.
  Stay static unless you can name the reason.
- **Panning too fast.** It judders, and upright lines lean. Slow down, or cut between
  two still shots instead.
- **No still frame at the start or end.** The editor has nowhere clean to cut. Hold
  2–3 seconds at each end.
- **Zooming instead of moving.** It looks flat. Walk in.
- **Waving the camera around.** Pick one subject and one move, and commit to them.

## Try it

Twenty minutes, with the Pocket 3, at a hawker centre or on a busy street:

1. Pick one subject, such as a stall and its cook (ask the stallholder first).
2. Film five shots of about 15 seconds each: static, pan, tilt, push in (walking), and
   follow (someone walking past). Start and end each one still.
3. Watch them back. For each, say what the move adds, or whether static would have been
   better.

When you write the shot list for **A5.6**, give every shot a size and a movement (or
"static"), with its reason.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- What's the difference between zooming in and pushing in, and how does it show on screen?
> A zoom magnifies the picture from one spot, so everything grows at the same rate and
> nothing shifts: it looks flat. A push in moves the camera, so near things grow faster
> than far things and slide past them. That parallax feels like moving into the space.

> [!question]- Why start and end every move on a still frame?
> It gives the editor clean places to cut: before the move, after it, or through it. A
> move that starts at the first frame or never settles leaves no clean cut point.

> [!question]- A pan across a hawker centre judders on playback. Why, and what are two fixes?
> At 25 fps with a 180° shutter, a fast pan moves the scene too far between frames for
> the blur to bridge. Pan more slowly, so things take at least about 7 seconds to cross
> the frame, or replace the pan with two or three still shots.

> [!question]- When might handheld be a better choice than the gimbal?
> When you want urgency, closeness, or the feeling of being there: a crowd, a chase, a
> tense moment. A little natural movement tells the viewer a person is holding the
> camera. The gimbal's smoothness can feel detached in those moments.
