# Pacing and Rhythm

> [!summary] In one line
> Pacing is how fast a film feels; rhythm is the pattern of cuts, movement and sound
> that makes it feel that way. You control both mainly through how long each shot lasts
> and the exact moment you cut.

## What it is

- **Pacing** is how quickly the film seems to move: calm, steady or urgent. It depends
  on how fast new information arrives, not only on how many cuts there are.
- **Rhythm** is the pattern underneath: the lengths of the shots, where the cuts land,
  the movement inside each shot, and the sound.
- **Shot length** is how long one shot stays on screen. It's your main tool for both.

A slow film isn't a bad film, and a fast one isn't a good one. The pace should suit the
subject: a quiet morning wants long, calm shots; a football match wants short, sharp
ones.

## How it works

**A shot should last as long as the viewer needs to read it.** A wide of a busy market
holds a lot of information, so the eye needs a few seconds to explore it. A close-up of
a hand holds one thing and reads in a second. Cut before the viewer has read a shot and
the film feels rushed or exciting. Hold after they've read it and it feels calm, tense
or, if held too long, boring.

**Rhythm comes from change, not from any one length.** Ten shots of exactly 2 seconds
become a metronome, and the viewer stops noticing the cuts. Long, short, short, long
feels alive, as it does in music. Shortening the shots towards a moment builds tension;
a long hold after it lets the moment land.

**Music gives you a ready-made rhythm.** Its speed is measured in **beats per minute
(BPM)**. Beats group into **bars**, usually of four, and bars group into **phrases**,
often of four or eight bars. Listeners feel the start of a new phrase as "something new
is starting". So small changes go on beats, and big changes (a new place, a new part of
the story) go where a phrase begins.

The numbers at 25 fps:

| Tempo | One beat | One 4-beat bar | One 8-bar phrase |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 BPM | 0.6 s = 15 frames | 2.4 s = 60 frames | 19.2 s |
| 120 BPM | 0.5 s = 12.5 frames | 2 s = 50 frames | 16 s |

At 120 BPM a beat isn't a whole number of frames, so cuts on single beats alternate
between 12 and 13 frames apart. Cuts on every bar land exactly.

**Walter Murch's rule of six.** In *In the Blink of an Eye*, the film editor Walter
Murch lists six things an ideal cut does, in order of importance. He even gives them
rough weights:

| Rank | Criterion | The cut… | Weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Emotion | is true to what the audience should feel at that moment | 51% |
| 2 | Story | moves the story forward | 23% |
| 3 | Rhythm | comes at a moment that is rhythmically right | 10% |
| 4 | Eye-trace | respects where the viewer is looking in the frame, and where their eye goes next | 7% |
| 5 | Two-dimensional plane of the screen | keeps screen direction and the line of action consistent | 5% |
| 6 | Three-dimensional space of action | keeps where people are in the room, and in relation to each other, consistent | 4% |

An ideal cut satisfies all six at once. When it can't, Murch says to give things up
from the bottom of the list first. Emotion is at the top, and on its own it outweighs
the other five together. So a cut that breaks screen direction but feels right beats a
tidy cut that kills the feeling. → [[Screen-Direction]]

## How to use it

### Starting shot lengths

Starting points only. Watch at full speed and trust what you feel.

| Shot | Calm film | Fast film |
|---|---|---|
| Wide | 4–8 s | 2–3 s |
| Medium | 3–5 s | 1–2 s |
| Close-up or insert | 2–3 s | 0.5–1 s |

Shots with movement, or a face reacting, can hold longer, because there's still
something new to read.

### Cutting to music

1. Choose the music before the fine cut, so the cut can follow it.
2. Play it and tap the marker key on every bar (M in Resolve and Premiere Pro). Or zoom
   into the audio waveform and look for drum hits: they show as sharp spikes.
3. Put most cuts on bar lines. Use single beats for short, fast passages.
4. Save the big changes for the start of a phrase.
5. Don't cut on every beat for the whole film. Let some shots run across several bars.
6. If the cuts feel late at full speed, try moving them one or two frames earlier.

To fit the music to the film, decide where it must **end** first. Line up the music's
real ending with the film's last shot, and let the start fall where it falls. If the
music is too long, remove whole bars or phrases, and join the two halves on a beat with
a short audio crossfade.

### Holding a shot for emotion

The urge is to cut as soon as the action ends. After an important moment, don't. Hold
a reaction, an empty room or a face a second or two longer than feels comfortable.
That pause is where the viewer feels what just happened. Cut when the feeling peaks,
not when the action stops.

### Varying the rhythm

Plan the speed like a piece of music: a calm opening, faster cuts as the film builds,
a long hold at the payoff, then a calm ending. → [[Story-Structure]]

In **A5.3** you cut the same footage twice. The calm one-minute version uses longer
shots, L-cuts and natural sound. The fast 30-second version uses short shots on the
beat. Same footage, two different films.

## Common mistakes

- **Every shot the same length.** It turns into a metronome. Vary the lengths on
  purpose.
- **Cutting on every single beat.** It's tiring and predictable. Cut mostly on bars and
  phrases, and keep single beats for short bursts.
- **Keeping a shot because it was hard to get.** The viewer doesn't know what it cost
  you. If it doesn't serve the film, cut it.
- **Cutting away from emotion too soon.** Hold the reaction. The feeling needs time to
  arrive.
- **Judging pace while scrubbing, or in pieces.** Pace only exists at full speed. Watch
  the whole film from the start after each pass.

## Try it

Thirty minutes, a small version of **A5.3**:

1. Take 10–12 clips from one walk or outing.
2. Find music you're allowed to use, at roughly 100–120 BPM. → [[Sound-Mixing]]
3. Cut 30 seconds with a cut on every bar.
4. Duplicate it. Re-cut so the shot lengths vary: a few single beats, some shots that
   hold for two or three bars, and one long hold near the end.
5. Watch both at full speed. Write down which one feels alive, and where.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- In Murch's rule of six, a cut that feels emotionally right breaks screen direction. Which should win, and why?
> Emotion. Murch ranks it first and says to give things up from the bottom of the list.
> Screen direction (the two-dimensional plane of the screen) is fifth. Viewers forgive
> a direction slip far more easily than a moment that feels false.

> [!question]- Your music is 100 BPM and your timeline is 25 fps. How long is one 4-beat bar, in frames?
> One beat is 60 ÷ 100 = 0.6 s, which is 15 frames. A bar of four beats is 60 frames,
> or 2.4 s.

> [!question]- Every shot in your edit is good, and every shot is 2 seconds long. Why does it feel boring?
> Rhythm comes from change. Equal lengths become a steady tick that the viewer stops
> noticing. Varying the lengths creates contrast, and contrast holds attention.

> [!question]- Should a wide of a busy hawker centre or a close-up of a hand usually stay on screen longer? Why?
> The wide. It holds much more information, so the eye needs longer to explore it. The
> close-up shows one thing and is read in a second.

> [!question]- When should you hold a shot longer than feels comfortable?
> After an important moment, such as a reaction or the payoff. The pause gives the
> viewer time to feel it. Cutting away at once throws the feeling away.
