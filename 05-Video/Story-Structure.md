# Story Structure

> [!summary] In one line
> Even a one-minute film needs a beginning, a middle and an end: a hook that raises a
> question, a build that develops it, and a payoff that answers it. Planning that shape
> before you film tells you which shots to take.

## What it is

| Part | Its job | In a one-minute film |
|---|---|---|
| **Hook** | Raise a question the viewer wants answered | The first 3–5 seconds |
| **Build** | Add something new with each shot, moving towards the answer | Most of the middle |
| **Payoff** | Answer the question; deliver the moment the build promised | Near the end |
| **Ending** | Let it land, then stop | The last few seconds |

This is the old beginning, middle and end, cut down to fit. A **sequence** is a group
of shots that together show one action or one idea, like the five shots of making
coffee in **A5.2**. A short film is one sequence, or a few.

## How it works

**Viewers keep watching to get an answer.** The hook raises a question: what is this,
what will happen, why is that person there? Each shot in the build adds information or
raises the stakes. The payoff answers the question. Once nothing is left unanswered,
there's no reason to keep watching, so end soon after the payoff.

**A film is *of* something and *about* something.** It's *of* a hawker stall. It's
*about* the hours of work before the first customer arrives. That second sentence is
the question you ask of a photograph in [[Subject-and-Intent]], with time added. It
decides which shots stay: a shot that doesn't serve the "about" goes, however pretty it
is.

**Cutting lets the viewer fill the gaps.** Ten minutes of making coffee fits into 30
seconds because the viewer joins the dots. A hand reaches for the kettle; then water is
pouring. Nobody needs to watch the kettle boil. Cutting out the middles of actions is
what makes a sequence move. → [[Cuts-and-Transitions]]

**Show, don't tell.** A caption saying "it was a hot day" tells. Sweat on a neck, a dog
asleep in the shade and a fan turning show. Showing is stronger, because the viewer
works it out, and people believe what they work out themselves. Use words only for
what pictures and sound can't say: a name, a date, a number.

## How to use it

### A shape for one minute

| Seconds | Part | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5 | Hook | A striking detail, a sound or a question. Not a slow wide |
| 5–15 | Set-up | Where we are and who is there. This is where the wide goes |
| 15–45 | Build | Three to five steps, each adding something, getting faster or closer |
| 45–55 | Payoff | The moment the film was heading for |
| 55–60 | Ending | A last image that lets it land: a hold, a quiet shot, or back to the start |

### From idea to shot list

1. **Write the "about" in one sentence.** "The storm arrives faster than anyone
   expects."
2. **Write the beats:** one line each for the hook, the build steps, the payoff and the
   ending.
3. **List the shots for each beat:** a wide to show where, mediums for the action,
   close-ups for detail and feeling, and at least one cutaway.
   → [[Shot-Sizes-and-Coverage]]
4. **Add the sound** each beat needs: an ambience, a key sound effect, room tone.
5. **Plan the filming order separately.** Film by place and light, not in story order,
   but number the shots in story order so the edit is easy. → [[Shoot-Planning]]

### A worked example: "The storm arrives"

A Singapore afternoon thunderstorm, filmed from home. Nobody needs to agree to be in
it.

| # | Beat | Shot | Size | Sound |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hook | The first heavy drop hits a dusty window | Close-up | One sharp tap |
| 2 | Set-up | The estate under a darkening sky | Wide | Distant thunder |
| 3 | Build | Washing on the poles, flapping | Medium | Rising wind |
| 4 | Build | Leaves flipping over in the gusts | Close-up | Wind in the trees |
| 5 | Build | A wall of rain crossing the estate | Wide | The rain approaching |
| 6 | Payoff | The downpour | Wide | Full rain |
| 7 | Payoff | Rain bouncing off a railing | Close-up | Rain on metal |
| 8 | Ending | The same window, now streaming with water | Close-up | Rain softening |

The sound grows with every beat of the build. The ending returns to the window from the
hook, now changed. That circle tells the viewer the film is complete.

### Other shapes that fit a minute

- **A process:** one task from start to finish, like **A5.2**. The payoff is the
  finished thing.
- **Before and after:** a place empty, then full; quiet, then loud.
- **A circle:** end on the image you started with, now changed, as the storm example
  does.

## Common mistakes

- **Opening with a slow wide.** Nothing has been asked yet, so nothing makes the viewer
  stay. Open on a detail or a sound, and bring the wide in second.
- **A montage with no payoff.** Nice shots in a row aren't a story. Ask what your last
  shot answers.
- **Captions that repeat the pictures.** Cut the caption, or cut the shot.
- **Filming without a shot list.** You come home with twenty wides and no close-ups.
  Write the list first, even on your phone.
- **Trying to say everything.** One minute holds one idea. Keep the one sentence and
  drop the rest.

## Try it

Thirty minutes, a planning rehearsal for **A5.6**:

1. Choose a subject you can film this week.
2. Write its "about" in one sentence.
3. Write the beats and a shot list with sizes and sounds, like the table above.
4. Film only the hook and the payoff. Watch the two shots back to back. If the second
   doesn't answer the first, change the plan before you film the rest.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Your film opens with ten seconds of a beautiful wide. Why might viewers leave, and what could you open with instead?
> A wide sets the scene but asks nothing, so there's no reason to wait. Open on a
> detail or a sound that raises a question. Show the wide once the viewer wants to know
> where they are.

> [!question]- What is the difference between what a film is "of" and what it is "about"?
> "Of" is what you point the camera at, such as a hawker stall. "About" is the idea,
> such as the hidden work before opening time. The "about" decides which shots stay.

> [!question]- Why write the shot list from the structure instead of filming whatever looks good?
> You can only edit the shots you took. The structure tells you which shots each beat
> needs, including the close-ups and cutaways you'd otherwise forget, and it stops you
> spending time on shots no beat needs.

> [!question]- A caption in your film says "It was very hot." How could you show it instead?
> With pictures and sound: sweat on a neck, heat shimmer over a road, a dog flat in the
> shade, a fan whirring. The viewer works out "hot" for themselves, which is stronger.

> [!question]- What makes a payoff feel earned?
> It answers the question the hook raised, and the build has been leading towards it.
> A big moment with no build feels random. A build with no payoff feels unfinished.
