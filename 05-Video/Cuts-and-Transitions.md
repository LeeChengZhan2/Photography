# Cuts and Transitions

> [!summary] In one line
> A cut joins two shots. The best cuts go unnoticed because they follow the viewer's
> attention. Dissolves and fades say something specific, such as "time has passed", so
> use them only when you mean it.

## What it is

Every join between two shots is an edit. These are the ones you'll use most:

| Edit | What happens | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| **Straight cut** | Shot A ends and shot B starts on the next frame | Almost everything |
| **Cut on action** | The cut happens during a movement | Hiding the cut |
| **Match cut** | A shape, movement or colour carries across the cut | Linking two places or times |
| **Jump cut** | Same subject, nearly the same framing, with time skipped | Squeezing time, unsettling the viewer |
| **J-cut** | The next shot's sound starts before its picture | Leading the viewer into a new shot |
| **L-cut** | The last shot's sound carries on over the new picture | Letting a moment linger |
| **Cutaway** | A shot of something else in the scene, then back | Hiding a jump, adding context |
| **Insert** | A close shot of a detail of the main action | Showing what matters |
| **Dissolve** | One shot fades through into the next | Time passing, a soft link |
| **Fade** | The picture fades in from black, or out to black | Starts, endings, chapter breaks |

## How it works

**Why a plain cut feels natural.** Your eyes jump from one thing to another several
times a second, and your brain hides the jumps. A cut that goes where the viewer's
attention was already going feels like one of those jumps. The film editor Walter Murch
adds that people tend to blink when one thought ends and the next begins. A good cut,
he argues, lands at that kind of moment. → [[Pacing-and-Rhythm]]

**A cut makes meaning.** Put two shots side by side and the viewer connects them. Film
teachers call this the Kuleshov effect, after a Soviet film-maker's tests around 1920.
In the classic version, a neutral face followed by a bowl of soup reads as hunger. The
same face followed by a coffin reads as grief. → [[Juxtaposition]]

**Cutting on action hides the cut.** When something moves, the eye follows it. If the
cut happens during the movement, the eye carries the motion into the next shot and
hardly notices the change of view. Cut after the movement has finished and the viewer
sees one still picture swap for another.

**A match cut links two things by shape or movement.** In *2001: A Space Odyssey*
(1968), a bone spinning in the air cuts to a spacecraft of a similar shape, and
millions of years pass in one cut. A small version: a round bowl seen from above, then
a round clock face.

**A jump cut looks like a mistake** because the two shots are almost the same. The eye
compares them and sees things jump. Change the angle by at least 30°, or change the
shot size clearly, and the brain reads a new view instead. This is the **30-degree
rule**. Jean-Luc Godard made jump cuts famous on purpose in *Breathless* (1960), and
vloggers use them every day to cut out pauses.

**Sound and picture don't have to change together.** In life you often hear something
before you look at it. A **J-cut** copies that: the next shot's sound arrives first and
pulls the viewer across the cut. An **L-cut** does the reverse: the sound stays while
the picture moves on, for example to show someone listening. The names come from the
shapes the clips make on the timeline:

```
J-cut   video  |AAAAAAAA|BBBBBBBB|        L-cut   video  |AAAAAAAA|BBBBBBBB|
        audio  |aaaaa|bbbbbbbbbbb|                audio  |aaaaaaaaaaa|bbbbb|
```

**Dissolves and fades are punctuation.** A dissolve shows two moments at once, so the
viewer reads "time has passed" or "these two belong together". A fade to black works
like a full stop at the end of a chapter. Because they carry meaning, they confuse when
there's no meaning to carry.

**Flashy transitions point at the editing.** Spins, page turns, glitches and zooms make
the viewer look at the join instead of the film. They add no meaning, they look the
same in every video that uses the template, and they break the rhythm. Movement
*inside* the shots is different. A fast pan at the end of one shot into a fast pan at
the start of the next (a whip pan) is a match cut made with the camera.

## How to use it

Use a straight cut unless you can say why something else is better.

### Cutting on action, step by step

1. Film the action twice, in two shot sizes: a wide of a hand reaching for a cup, and
   a close-up of the same hand. → [[Shot-Sizes-and-Coverage]]
2. Put the wide on the timeline. Play it and stop when the hand starts to move.
3. Cut out of the wide early in the movement, about a third of the way through.
4. Start the close-up at the same point in the same movement.
5. Play across the cut at full speed. If the movement stutters or skips, roll the cut a
   frame or two at a time until it flows. → [[Timeline-and-Trimming]]

Check that things match across the cut: the same hand, the cup in the same place. The
viewer's eye is on the action, so a mismatch there shows at once.

### Making a J-cut or an L-cut

1. Make a normal straight cut first.
2. Select only the audio side of the cut: turn off linked selection, or Alt-click
   (Option-click on a Mac) the audio in Resolve or Premiere (formerly Premiere Pro).
3. Roll the audio edit: earlier for a J-cut, later for an L-cut. Half a second to two
   seconds is a good start.
4. Turn linked selection back on.

### Cutaways and inserts

- **Hide a jump.** An interview answer has a stumble in the middle. Cut out the
  stumble, then cover the join with a cutaway of the hands or the room.
- **Squeeze time.** Ten minutes of cooking in 20 seconds: an insert of each step, with
  the slow middles cut out.
- **Add context.** A cutaway to the stall's signboard tells the viewer where they are.

### Dissolves and fades

- **Dissolve:** about 0.5–1 s (12–25 frames at 25 fps) for time passing. Longer feels
  dreamy.
- **Fades:** in from black at the very start, out to black at the very end, about
  1–2 s each.
- **Fade the sound with the picture,** or the film ends on sudden silence or a cut-off
  note.
- **Clicks at audio cuts:** add a very short audio crossfade, a frame or two long.

## Common mistakes

- **Using a dissolve to hide a bad cut.** It makes the problem slower, not smaller. Fix
  the cut with a roll trim or a cutaway.
- **Accidental jump cuts.** Two shots of the same subject from the same spot. When you
  film, move at least 30° or change the shot size.
- **Cutting after the action ends.** The viewer sees the join. Cut during the movement.
- **Changing every sound with every picture.** It feels choppy. Let some sound lead or
  trail with J- and L-cuts.
- **Template transitions.** Spins and glitches distract from the film and date it. A
  straight cut almost always looks better.

## Try it

Thirty minutes, a small version of **A5.2**:

1. Film one simple action twice, as a wide and as a close-up: pouring water into a
   glass. Start recording before the action and stop after it.
2. Cut from the wide to the close-up on the action. Roll the cut until the pour flows.
3. Add a third shot of your face, and make an L-cut so the pouring sound carries on
   over it.
4. Duplicate the sequence and replace every cut with a one-second dissolve. Watch both
   versions, and write down which feels more natural and why.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why does cutting during a movement hide the cut better than cutting after it?
> The eye is following the movement. When the cut comes mid-movement, the eye carries
> the motion into the next shot and hardly notices the change. After the movement
> ends there's nothing to carry, so the viewer sees one picture swap for another.

> [!question]- Two shots of the same person from almost the same angle feel like a glitch. What is it, and how do you fix it?
> A jump cut. In the edit, put a cutaway between the two shots. Next time, change the
> angle by at least 30° or change the shot size, so the second shot reads as a new
> view.

> [!question]- You let the market noise start one second before the picture cuts to the market. What kind of cut is that, and why does it feel smooth?
> A J-cut: the sound leads the picture. The viewer's ears arrive first, so when the
> picture changes it feels expected rather than sudden.

> [!question]- When is a dissolve better than a straight cut?
> When you want the viewer to feel time passing or to link two moments, such as a stall
> at dawn dissolving to the same stall busy at noon. If you can't say what a dissolve
> means, use a cut.

> [!question]- Why do spin and glitch transitions usually hurt a film?
> They make the viewer look at the editing instead of the story. They add no meaning,
> look like a template, and interrupt the rhythm the cuts were building.
