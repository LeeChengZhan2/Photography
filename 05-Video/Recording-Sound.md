# Recording Sound

> [!summary] In one line
> Sound carries as much of a film as the picture does, and bad sound is much harder to
> fix. Get the microphone close, keep the levels below the top, block the wind, and
> record the room as well as the voices.

## What it is

Recording sound for video means capturing a few different things, not just voices:

| Layer | Example | How you get it |
|---|---|---|
| Dialogue | Someone talking | A microphone close to their mouth |
| Effects | A pour, a door, footsteps | A microphone close to the action |
| Ambience | A market's murmur, traffic, birds | A separate recording of the place, 1–2 minutes |
| Room tone | A room's "silence": fridge hum, air-con | 30 seconds of quiet, recorded on location |

You balance these layers later, in the mix. → [[Sound-Mixing]]

People will watch soft or grainy footage. They stop watching when they can't make out
the words, or when the sound is harsh. Sound also tells the viewer where they are:
close your eyes in a hawker centre and you still know where you are.

## How it works

### Distance matters more than the microphone

Decibels (dB) measure how much louder or quieter one sound is than another. Sound
follows the same [[Inverse-Square-Law|inverse-square law]] as light: double the
distance from mouth to mic, and the voice arrives about 6 dB quieter, a quarter of the
power. The background noise and the room's echo don't change. So every time you double
the distance, the voice loses about 6 dB against everything else.

| Mic to mouth | Voice level, compared with 15 cm | Sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| 15 cm (a clip-on mic) | 0 dB | Close and clear |
| 30 cm | About −6 dB | Clear |
| 60 cm | About −12 dB | Fine in a quiet room |
| 1.2 m | About −18 dB | Roomy and echoey |
| 2.4 m (a camera across a room) | About −24 dB | Distant, sunk in the noise |

Turning the voice up later doesn't help, because the noise and echo come up with it.
The balance between voice and background is fixed when you record. That's why a basic
mic close to the mouth beats a better one on the camera.

### Microphones, in general terms

- **Built-in:** on the camera, so near you and far from the subject. It hears
  everything around it, plus your breathing and your hands on the camera. Good for
  ambience and a reference track; poor for a voice a few metres away.
- **Lavalier (lav):** a tiny clip-on mic, worn on the chest about a hand's width below
  the chin. Close and consistent. Watch for clothing rubbing against it.
- **Shotgun:** a long, directional mic. It hears mostly what it points at, and less from
  the sides. It doesn't pull in sound from far away: it still has to be close, just out
  of frame.
- **Wireless:** a small transmitter on the subject sends their mic's sound to a receiver
  on the camera. No cables, but watch the batteries and the occasional dropout.
- **The free option:** a phone recording audio, in the speaker's pocket or on the table
  near them, works as a lav you already own. You sync it later.

### Levels and dBFS

Digital audio is measured in **dBFS**: decibels below full scale. 0 dBFS is the loudest
value the file can hold. Anything louder is cut off flat. That's **clipping**: a harsh
crackle that can't be repaired. So recording levels are always negative numbers.

- **Aim for speech peaking around −12 dBFS.** That leaves headroom for a laugh or a
  shout.
- **Never hit 0.** One clipped laugh can ruin a take.
- **Don't record too low either.** Peaks around −30 dBFS mean turning it up later, and
  the hiss comes up with it.

Many devices set the level automatically (auto gain). In quiet moments it turns the
gain up, so the background swells in every pause; at a loud sound it ducks. Use manual
levels where a device allows it. Where it doesn't, keep the mic close, so the voice
dominates.

### Wind

Moving air hitting the microphone makes low rumbles and pops. They drown out speech and
are very hard to remove later. Even a light breeze does it.

- **Windscreens** help: foam for a light breeze or indoors, a furry cover outdoors.
- **Free fixes:** stand with your back to the wind and shield the mic with your body,
  shelter behind a wall or a pillar, or cover a small mic with a scrap of fabric.
- **In Singapore:** sea breezes at East Coast and Marina Barrage, and standing fans in
  hawker centres, blow straight across a mic.

A low-cut filter (a setting or an editing effect that removes the lowest sounds) takes
out some rumble, but not gusts.

### Room tone and ambience

**Room tone** is 30 seconds or more of the location's own "silence", with everyone
still and the mic in the same place. When you cut dialogue, gaps appear. Fill them with
room tone, and the background stays continuous instead of dropping to dead silence.

**Ambience** is a minute or two of the place's sound: the market, the street, the rain.
Lay it under the edit, and the cuts between shots feel like one place. A5.4 is built on
it.

### Monitoring

Meters show the level, not the quality. Only headphones tell you about wind, clothing
rustle, hum or a voice that's too far away. Wired headphones are best, because
Bluetooth adds a delay. If a device has no headphone output, record a 10-second test and
play it back on headphones before the real take.

### Syncing separate sound with a clap

When the sound is recorded on a separate device, you have to line it up with the
picture. Clap once, sharply, in view of the camera and within earshot of both mics. In
the editor, line up the clap's sharp spike in the two sound waveforms, or match the
spike to the frame where the hands meet.

Many editors can also sync automatically by matching the two waveforms. That only works
if the camera records its own sound too, so always keep the built-in mic on as a
reference. In long takes, separate devices can drift apart slightly, so check the sync
at the end as well.

## How to use it

Before each take:

1. **Listen first.** Thirty seconds on headphones. Find the noise: fans, air-con,
   traffic, a fridge. Move away from what you can't switch off.
2. **Get the mic close**, just outside the frame.
3. **Set the level.** Ask the person to speak as loudly as they will in the take. Aim
   for peaks around −12 dBFS.
4. **Fit wind protection outdoors** before the wind finds you.
5. **Clap at the start** if the sound is recorded separately.

Before you leave:

- **Record 30 seconds of room tone** and a minute or two of ambience.
- **Listen back** to one take on headphones.

## On your kit

- **All four devices** record through built-in microphones that sit with you, not the
  subject. Their best jobs are ambience, effects you can get close to, and a reference
  track for syncing.
- **iPhone 15 Pro Max as a recorder:** while you film on another camera, put the phone
  near the speaker, recording audio. Clap at the start to sync. That's a free lav.
- **Pocket 3:** when you film yourself at arm's length, the mic is close enough for your
  own voice. For anyone else, get closer or record their voice separately.
- **Action 5 Pro:** waterproof without its case, but inside the case it will hear very
  little. Record sound on another device when it's in the case.
- **Check each device's audio settings** for a manual level, a wind-noise filter and
  support for an external mic, and note what each one offers.

## Common mistakes

- **Mic on the camera, speaker three metres away.** The voice sinks into the room. Move
  the mic, or the camera, closer.
- **Peaks hitting 0 dBFS.** The clipping is permanent. Lower the level and leave
  headroom.
- **No wind protection outdoors.** You get rumble you can't remove. Use a windscreen,
  your body or a wall.
- **Not listening.** You find the hum, the rustle or the wind at home. Wear headphones,
  or play a test back on location.
- **Skipping room tone.** The gaps between lines drop to dead silence. Record 30 seconds
  before you leave.

## Try it

A small version of **A5.4**, in 15 minutes, with the iPhone and a friend:

1. In a room with a fan or the air-con running, record your friend reading the same
   sentence at 30 cm, 1 m and 3 m from the phone.
2. Listen on headphones. Notice how the voice sinks into the hum as the distance grows.
3. Record 30 seconds of room tone in the same spot.
4. Step outside and record one minute of ambience. Listen with your eyes closed: can you
   tell where you are?

## Check yourself

> [!question]- A voice recorded 3 m from the camera sounds worse than one at 30 cm, even after you turn it up. Why?
> Ten times the distance makes the voice about 20 dB quieter, but the background and the
> room's echo stay the same. Turning it up raises everything together, so the voice
> stays buried. The balance is fixed when you record.

> [!question]- Your speech peaks sit at −2 dBFS, and then someone laughs. What happens?
> The laugh is louder than the speech, so it goes past 0 dBFS and clips. Clipping is a
> harsh distortion that can't be repaired. Peaks around −12 dBFS would have left room.

> [!question]- Why record 30 seconds of room tone?
> Cutting dialogue leaves gaps. Without room tone, the background drops to dead silence
> in each gap, and the viewer hears the edits. Room tone fills them with the same quiet
> hum.

> [!question]- Why can't a shotgun mic record a voice clearly from across the street?
> A shotgun narrows what it hears; it doesn't reach further. The voice still loses about
> 6 dB every time the distance doubles, while the traffic stays loud. It still has to
> be close.

> [!question]- Why clap at the start of a separately recorded take?
> A clap makes a sharp spike in both recordings, and a clear frame where the hands meet.
> Lining up the spikes, or the spike with that frame, puts sound and picture in sync.
