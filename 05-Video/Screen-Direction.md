# Screen Direction

> [!summary] In one line
> Screen direction is which way people face and move on screen. Keep the camera on one
> side of an imaginary line through the action, and left and right stay the same in
> every shot, so the viewer never loses their place.

## What it is

Draw an imaginary line through the action: between two people who are talking, or
along the path of someone walking. This is the **axis of action**, usually just called
**the line**. The **180° rule** says: keep the camera on one side of it. Anywhere in
that half-circle (180°) keeps left and right the same.

It's a different rule from the one with the same name in [[Shutter-Angle]]:

| | The shutter's 180° rule | The camera's 180° rule |
|---|---|---|
| Controls | Shutter speed | Where you put the camera |
| Says | Shutter speed of about 1 ÷ (2 × frame rate), e.g. 1/50 s at 25 fps | Stay on one side of the line |
| Breaking it changes | How motion blur looks | Which way people face and move |

## How it works

The screen is flat, so the viewer builds a map of the space from left and right: A is
on the left, B is on the right, the cyclist is heading right. Every cut asks the viewer
to check that map.

```
            other side: a camera here swaps left and right
 ─────────── A ───────────────────────── B ───────────   the line
               C1           C2           C3
            your side: C1, C2 and C3 all keep A on the left
```

From any camera on your side, A is on the left looking right, and B is on the right
looking left. Cross to the other side and A appears on the right. Nothing in the scene
changed, but on screen it looks as if they swapped places, or turned away from each
other.

Movement works the same way. If a cyclist rides left to right in one shot and right to
left in the next, the viewer reads that she turned round, or that it's a different
cyclist.

**Eyelines** are the directions people look. When two people are filmed in separate
shots, A must look slightly off-screen to the right and B slightly to the left. Then
they seem to look at each other. If both look the same way, they seem to be looking at
someone else. When someone looks off-screen and you cut to what they see, the direction
of their look tells the viewer where that thing is.

Walter Murch ranks this, "the two-dimensional plane of the screen", fifth of the six
things a cut should respect, below emotion and story. It matters, but a cut that feels
right is allowed to break it. → [[Pacing-and-Rhythm]]

## How to use it

### On location

1. Find the line: between the two people, along the path of the movement, or along
   someone's look towards an object.
2. Pick a side, and film every shot of the scene from it.
3. Write the side into your shot list, for example "camera on the road side".
4. For a conversation, film both over-the-shoulder shots (over one person's shoulder,
   at the other) from your side. A's shoulder is on the left of the frame in one, and
   B's shoulder is on the right in the other. → [[Shot-Sizes-and-Coverage]]
5. For a journey, choose a direction, say left to right, and keep it. Reverse it only
   when the subject really turns round, such as going home.

### Crossing the line on purpose

You can change sides if the viewer sees how you did it:

- **Move the camera across the line during a shot.** The viewer watches the space turn,
  so they stay oriented. → [[Camera-Movement]]
- **Cut to a neutral shot**, taken on the line itself: the subject walking straight
  towards the camera, or straight away. It has no left or right, so the next shot can
  come from either side.
- **Let the subject cross.** If the person turns or walks round, the line moves with
  them. Follow the new line.
- **Re-establish with a wide.** A new wide that shows the whole space resets the map.
- **Break it to disorient.** In a fight, a moment of panic or a sudden change of power,
  a deliberate jump across the line makes the viewer feel lost on purpose.

### Fixing it in the edit

One shot turns out to be from the wrong side. In order of preference:

1. Put a neutral shot or a cutaway between the two shots.
2. Flip the clip horizontally in the editor. Only do this if nothing gives it away:
   writing, signs, a watch on the wrong wrist or a side parting all reverse.
3. Keep it anyway, if the emotion of the cut is right.

## On your kit

- **One scene on several cameras (A5.5):** put all of them on the same side of the
  line. A cut between two cameras on opposite sides flips the scene.
- **The Action 5 Pro facing forward as you walk or ride** looks along the direction of
  travel. Its shots are often neutral, which makes them useful for changing sides.
- **The Pocket 3 in Follow mode, walking beside someone:** stay on the same side of
  them for the whole walk. Switching sides between takes reverses their direction on
  screen.

## Common mistakes

- **Filming each shot from wherever was easiest.** Choose the side first, then find
  your angles within it.
- **Reversing a journey by accident.** A walk that goes left to right and then right to
  left looks like a return. Keep the direction until the story turns round.
- **Mismatched eyelines.** Two people both looking towards frame right seem to ignore
  each other. Check that their looks point at each other across the cut.
- **Flipping a clip with writing in it.** Mirrored signs give it away at once. Use a
  neutral shot instead.

## Try it

Twenty minutes with a friend, as practice for **A5.2** and **A5.6**:

1. Film them walking past from left to right in three shots: a wide, a medium and a
   close-up of their feet. Stay on one side of their path.
2. Film one more medium shot from the other side of the path.
3. Cut the three shots together. Then swap the medium shot for the one from the other
   side, and watch the walk seem to reverse.
4. Fix it with a neutral shot: your friend walking straight towards the camera.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Your notes mention two different "180° rules". What does each one control?
> The shutter's 180° rule sets the shutter speed, about 1/50 s at 25 fps, for natural
> motion blur. The camera's 180° rule keeps the camera on one side of the line of
> action, so left and right stay consistent between shots.

> [!question]- A cyclist rides left to right in the wide, then right to left in the close-up. What does the viewer think, and how do you fix it?
> That she turned round, or that it's another cyclist. Film the close-up from the same
> side. In the edit, put a neutral head-on shot between them, or flip the close-up if
> nothing in it would read backwards.

> [!question]- In a conversation, A is on the left looking right. Where must A be in the reverse shot over B's shoulder?
> Still on the left of the frame, looking right, with B's shoulder on the right. If A
> appears on the right, the camera has crossed the line.

> [!question]- Why does a shot of someone walking straight at the camera let you change sides?
> It's taken on the line, so the movement has no left or right. Nothing in it
> contradicts the viewer's map, so the next shot can come from either side.

> [!question]- Why put every camera on the same side of the line in A5.5?
> A cut between cameras on opposite sides swaps everyone's left and right. The scene
> looks as if it flipped, even though nothing moved.
