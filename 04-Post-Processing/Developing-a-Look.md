# Developing a Look

> [!summary] In one line
> A look is a consistent set of choices about white balance, tone and colour that makes
> many photos read as one body of work. A preset gets you started, but each photo still
> needs its own adjustments, because no two photos start from the same place.

## What it is

A **look** is a set of editing choices you repeat: how warm or neutral things are, how
much contrast, how deep the blacks, which colours are strong and which are muted. Apply
the same choices across a set and the photos feel like they belong together.

A **preset** is saved slider settings that you apply in one click. It's the usual way
to carry a look from photo to photo. But a preset applies the same *changes*, not the
same *result*. That difference is the whole skill.

## How it works

### Why one preset doesn't fit every photo

Say you build a preset on a dim, bluish evening frame: +0.5 exposure and a warmer white
balance. On that frame it looks right. Apply it to a bright, sunny frame and the same
changes overexpose it and turn it orange. The preset didn't fail. The two photos
started in different places.

So split your settings into two groups:

| Same across the set (the look) | Different for each photo (the correction) |
|---|---|
| Tone curve shape: contrast, black point | Exposure |
| Colour grading | White balance values |
| Colour mixer: the palette | Highlights and shadows |
| Saturation strategy | Local adjustments and masks |
| Grain, vignette style | Crop and straightening |
| Black and white, or colour | Lens and perspective fixes |

Only the left column goes in the preset. The right column you set for each photo.

### A white balance "approach"

The kelvin numbers will differ between sessions, because the light differs. What stays
the same is the rule: for example, "greys neutral, skin slightly warm", or "keep the
warmth of the evening light". You apply the rule to each photo, not the number.
→ [[White-Balance]]

### What the eye notices first

When a set looks inconsistent, it's usually one of four things: overall brightness,
contrast, white balance, or one colour that's stronger in one photo. Check those before
anything subtle.

Editing can only match so much. A harsh midday frame and a soft blue-hour frame won't
become a pair with sliders. Choosing photos that share similar light does half the
work. → [[Culling-Discipline]]

### Reference images

Pick two to four reference images: your own best frame, or work you admire. Put one
next to your photo and describe it in words:

- Are the blacks deep, or lifted to grey?
- How saturated are the greens and blues?
- Is skin warm or neutral? Are the highlights tinted?

Then match one property at a time. Copying a look this way teaches you how it's built;
the aim is a look of your own. → [[Personal-Style]]

### Presets and LUTs

A preset is a set of parameters inside your raw developer. It's applied before the
image is rendered, so it works on each file's full raw data, and you can reopen and
change any slider. A LUT is a fixed table applied to finished colours. It travels to
other apps and to video, but it can't adapt or be reopened. For stills, a preset is
usually the better tool. → [[LUTs]]

## How to use it

The workflow for a consistent set, such as **A4.5**:

1. **Choose the set first.** Pick the twelve before you edit, favouring similar light.
2. **Pick a hero image:** a strong, typical frame. Correct it to neutral, then build
   the look on it. → [[Colour-Grading]]
3. **Save the look as a preset, with only the look settings ticked.** Most apps let you
   choose what a preset includes. Leave out exposure, white balance, crop and masks.
4. **Apply it to the rest,** with your app's sync or copy-and-paste settings.
5. **Adjust each photo:** exposure, white balance by your rule, highlights and shadows.
6. **Review all twelve as a grid.** Find the odd one out: brighter, warmer or more
   saturated than the rest. Fix it, or swap it.
7. **Look again the next day,** with fresh eyes.

Preset packs, free or paid, are someone else's look, tuned for their photos. Open one
and read which sliders it moves to learn from it. Then build your own.

## On your kit

- **Mixing devices:** each one renders colour differently. Correct each device's files
  to neutral first, then apply the look. A separate base preset per device can help.
- **Lumix L10:** Real Time LUT bakes a look into the JPEG or HEIF. Shoot RAW alongside,
  so the look stays a decision. V-Log stills need their technical LUT before any look.
- **iPhone 15 Pro Max:** Photographic Styles bake in at capture on this model and can't
  be used with ProRAW. Leave Standard on if you plan to edit. ProRAW already carries
  Apple's tone mapping, so it starts from a different place than an L10 RW2.
- **Pocket 3 and Action 5 Pro:** DNG stills take the same preset approach. For video,
  matching cameras is [[Shot-Matching]], in Module 5.

## Common mistakes

- **Applying a preset and stopping.** Each photo still needs its own exposure and white
  balance.
- **Putting exposure, white balance or crop in the preset.** Those differ per photo.
  Save only the look settings.
- **Editing each photo alone.** Consistency is about the set. Review it as a grid.
- **Following a trend instead of the subject.** Choose the mood the photos need.
- **Mixing black and white with colour, or different crops, without a reason.** The set
  stops reading as one.
- **Forcing very different light to match.** Choose frames that share light instead.

## Try it

Thirty minutes, a small version of **A4.5**:

1. Pick five photos from three different days.
2. Build a look on the strongest one. Save it as a preset, with only the look settings.
3. Apply it to the other four, then adjust each one's exposure and white balance.
4. View all five as a grid. Name the odd one out and fix it.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why does the same preset look different on two photos?
> A preset applies the same changes, not the same result. The photos started with
> different exposure, light and colours, so the same changes land in different places.

> [!question]- Which settings belong in a look preset, and which don't?
> The tone curve, colour grading, the colour mixer and grain belong in it. Exposure,
> white balance values, crop and masks don't, because each photo needs its own.

> [!question]- Why review a set as a grid rather than one photo at a time?
> Consistency is a relationship between photos. Seen alone, each one looks fine,
> because your eyes adapt. Side by side, the odd one out is obvious.

> [!question]- The kelvin values differ in every photo of your set. How can the white balance still be consistent?
> Consistency comes from the rule, not the number: for example, "greys neutral, skin
> slightly warm". Each photo gets whatever value makes it follow that rule.
