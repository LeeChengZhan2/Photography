# Culling Discipline

> [!summary] In one line
> Culling is choosing which frames to keep and edit. Do it fast, ask the same questions
> in the same order, and decide once: the skill is saying no.

## What it is

Culling means selecting. Most frames from any shoot are not worth editing. **A4.3** asks
for 10 keepers from 100 frames, and the Gate 4 test for 12 from 200: that's 10% and 6%.
Rejecting the rest is normal, not failure.

Editors give you three ways to mark a frame:

| Mark | Values | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| **Flag** | Pick, reject, or none | The first yes-or-no decision |
| **Star rating** | 0 to 5 stars | Ranking the picks |
| **Colour label** | A handful of colours | Your own meanings, such as "to edit" or "for the set" |

The marks only help if they mean the same thing every time. Write your system down once,
for example:

- **Flags** on the first pass: pick or reject, nothing else.
- **Stars** on the second pass: 3 = good, 4 = candidate for a set, 5 = among your best.
- **Colour labels** for jobs: green = edited, blue = in the A4.5 set.

In Lightroom Classic, P picks, X rejects and U clears a flag; the number keys set stars.

## How it works

### Two passes, two speeds

**Pass 1 is fast:** a few seconds per frame. You answer one question: "maybe, or no?"
Reject the obvious failures and flag anything with a chance. At about 4 s a frame, 200
frames take around 13 minutes.

**Pass 2 is slow,** but only on the picks: perhaps 30–40 frames. Now you compare, rank
and cut down to your target number, at 20–30 s per frame.

Two passes work because the jobs are different. Pass 1 clears away the obvious, so
pass 2 can spend real attention where it matters. Doing both at once means agonising
over frames you would have rejected in two seconds.

The speed of pass 1 is deliberate. Someone scrolling past gives your photo a moment, not
a minute. If a frame doesn't hold you for that moment, more looking rarely rescues it.

### The questions, in order

Ask these four questions in this order, and stop at the first "no":

1. **Focus.** Is the part that matters sharp? Missed focus and motion blur can't be
   fixed in editing, because sharpening only strengthens edges that are already there.
   Check at 100% zoom: a small preview hides soft focus. → [[Sharpening]]
2. **Exposure.** Is the information there? Clipped highlights are gone for good. A raw
   frame one or two stops too dark can usually be brightened, at the cost of more noise.
   → [[Histogram]]
3. **Moment.** Is it the right instant: the expression, the gesture, the gap in the
   crowd? → [[Gesture-and-Moment]]
4. **Composition.** Does the frame work? Small problems at the edges can be cropped
   away, but a crop only removes. It can't add what's missing. → [[Crop-and-Straighten]]

The order runs from "can't be fixed" to "a matter of judgement". It also runs from fast
to slow: focus takes a second to check, composition takes thought. So most rejects go
early, before you spend time on them.

One exception: a strong moment can beat slightly soft focus. Keep it if nothing else
from that moment works, and know it's a compromise.

### Similar frames, side by side

Bursts and repeated attempts give you groups of near-identical frames. Don't judge them
one at a time. Put the group side by side (Compare or Survey view in Lightroom Classic),
zoom to 100% on the eyes or the main subject, and keep **one winner per group**. Reject
the rest, however good they are, because a set never needs the same moment twice.

### Delete or keep the rejects?

Rejecting a frame only hides it. Deleting removes the file. Both are reasonable:

| | Keep the rejects | Delete the rejects |
|---|---|---|
| For | You can re-cull later, and your mistakes teach you | Less clutter, smaller backups |
| Against | Uses storage; a messier library | No second chance |

During Module 4, keep them: A4.3's cold re-cull needs them, and your Module 1 rejects
show how far you've come. Later, delete only clear technical failures, such as blank
frames or badly missed focus.

## How to use it

1. **Set up for speed.** Full-screen view, sliders hidden, a timer running. Turn on
   auto-advance, so the next frame appears after each flag (Caps Lock does this in
   Lightroom Classic).
2. **Pass 1:** flag picks and rejects. Zoom in only to check focus on a frame you are
   about to pick.
3. **Filter the view to show the picks only.**
4. **Pass 2:** group similar frames, choose one winner from each, then rank with stars.
5. **Cut to the target number.** If you need 12 and have 20, compare the two weakest
   and drop one. Repeat.
6. **Write one line on why** for each close call. It's the syllabus's third principle:
   "Cull ruthlessly, and write down why."

Don't edit while you cull. A raw frame looks flat before editing, so judge the picture,
not the polish. → [[Editing-Workflow]]

## On your kit

- **RAW + JPEG on the L10:** if you shoot both, make sure your editor shows each pair as
  one photo. Otherwise you cull everything twice.
- **Action 5 Pro:** its focus is fixed and sharp from about 35 cm, so focus is rarely
  the problem. Check for motion blur instead, because its stills get no stabilisation.
- **Mixed devices in one shoot:** the iPhone's processed files can look finished next to
  a flat L10 raw. Again, judge the picture, not the polish.

## Common mistakes

- **Culling and editing at the same time.** Each frame takes ten times longer. Cull the
  whole shoot first.
- **Keeping five versions of one moment.** Choose one winner per group and reject the
  rest.
- **Judging focus from thumbnails.** A small preview hides soft focus. Zoom to 100% on
  your possible picks.
- **Keeping a frame because it was hard to get.** The viewer can't see your effort, only
  the result.
- **Re-opening the rejects.** Decide once. Second-guessing doubles the time and rarely
  changes the result.

## Try it

A small version of **A4.3**. Take 50 frames from an earlier shoot. Set a timer for 8
minutes and cut them to 5 keepers using two passes. The next day, cull the same 50
again without looking at your first choice. Compare the two sets, and write down which
frames changed and why.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why check focus before composition?
> Missed focus can't be fixed, and it takes a second to check. Composition takes
> thought, and small problems can be cropped. Checking focus first throws out hopeless
> frames before you spend time on them.

> [!question]- You have eight near-identical frames of a friend laughing. How do you choose, and what happens to the other seven?
> Put them side by side, zoom to 100% on the eyes, and keep the one with the best focus
> and expression. Reject the other seven. A set never needs the same moment twice.

> [!question]- Your first pass on 200 frames took 40 minutes. What went wrong?
> You were doing pass 2's job: comparing and ranking. Pass 1 only asks "maybe or no", at
> a few seconds per frame. At that pace, 200 frames take about 13 minutes.

> [!question]- A day later, your cold re-cull for A4.3 keeps only 6 of your first 10. Did you fail?
> No. That difference is what A4.3 is for. Right after a shoot you remember how each
> frame felt to take. A day later you see only what's in the frame, which is all a
> viewer will ever see.

> [!question]- Why use flags rather than stars on the first pass?
> A flag is a yes-or-no decision, which is fast. Stars invite you to rank, and ranking
> is slow. Rank only the frames that survived.
