# Subject and Intent

> [!summary] In one line
> Every photo is *of* something you can point at and *about* something you want the
> viewer to feel or understand. Decide what it's about before you press the shutter,
> because that one decision chooses the lens, the light, the moment and the crop.

## What it is

The **subject** is what the photo is *of*: the thing you could point at. A hawker, a
bicycle, a puddle. The **intent** is what the photo is *about*: the idea or feeling you
want the viewer to take away. Tiredness at the end of a shift. A city that never
stops. A quiet morning.

The same subject can carry many intents, and each intent needs a different photo:

| Of (subject) | About (intent) | What the photo might look like |
|---|---|---|
| An uncle making kopi | Forty years of the same routine | Close on his hands, the worn pot, the same movement again |
| An uncle making kopi | The morning rush | Wider, the queue blurred in front, steam and movement |
| A void deck | How big the world is to a child | Camera at a child's height, the pillars towering |
| A bus stop in the rain | Waiting feels endless | People in a still row, flat grey light, nothing happening |

A photo with a subject but no intent is a record: it proves something was there. A
photo with an intent is a statement. Module 3's exit test asks for exactly this: for
ten frames, state the subject, the intent and one thing you removed.

## How it works

A viewer gives a photo a second or two before deciding whether to keep looking. In
that time their eyes jump to whatever stands out most: faces, the brightest area,
strong contrast, saturated colour, text. Then they ask, without words, "what am I
meant to see here?" → [[Visual-Weight-and-Balance]]

If one thing clearly wins and everything else supports it, the question has an answer
and the viewer stays. If three things are equally strong, the eye jumps between them,
finds no answer, and moves on. That's why a clear intent makes a stronger photo: it
gives you a test for every element in the frame.

Intent is not a separate step. It is the reason behind every other choice:

| Choice | Intent: "the stall is cramped and busy" | Intent: "he is alone in the crowd" |
|---|---|---|
| Distance and focal length | Wide (24 mm) and close, so the walls and pots crowd in | Long (75–120 mm) from far back, so the crowd stacks into a wall behind him |
| Light | The stall's mixed, messy light | One pool of light on him, the rest darker |
| Moment | Several hands moving at once | A still moment when he isn't serving anyone |
| Crop and space | Tight edges, things cut by the frame | Space around him |
| Exposure | Bright and busy | Darker, so he is the brightest thing |

Without an intent, you choose these by habit. The zoom stays where it was, the light is
whatever is there, and you press when you happen to look. The photo is then *of* the
scene and *about* nothing in particular.

### Of versus about, in practice

Ask two questions, in order:

1. **"This is a photo of ___."** Easy. A noun.
2. **"It's about ___."** Harder. An idea, a feeling, a relationship, a contrast.

If you can only answer the first, you've found a subject but not yet a photo. Keep
looking, move, or wait until the scene gives you something to say about it.

## How to use it

Before you raise the camera, write or say one sentence:

> A photo of **[subject]** that shows **[idea]** through **[one main choice]**.

For example: "A photo of my grandmother's hands that shows patience, through filling the
frame with her hands and the half-peeled beans." Or: "A photo of the 6 a.m. bus queue
that shows how tired the city is, through a long lens that packs the faces together."

Then:

1. **Check each element against the sentence.** Does it help? If not, remove it by
   moving, changing the angle or waiting. → [[Simplification]]
2. **Choose your tools from the sentence**, not from habit: distance and
   [[Focal-Length-and-Perspective|focal length]], viewpoint, light, shutter speed, the
   moment.
3. **Shoot, then test.** Show the frame to someone without the sentence and ask what
   it's about. If their answer is close to yours, it worked.

Rules of thumb, each with its reason:

- **One idea per frame.** If you need "and" to join two separate ideas, make two
  photos, because two ideas split the viewer's attention.
- **Intent can be found while shooting.** You often discover what a scene is about
  after twenty frames. Write the new sentence, then shoot towards it. That's what
  A3.1 is for.
- **Intent can change the subject.** You came for the temple; the photo is really
  about the old man feeding birds outside it. Follow it.
- **Decide at capture, not in the edit.** Editing can strengthen an intent, but it
  can't add the moment or the light you didn't wait for.

## Common mistakes

- **Naming a place instead of an idea.** "Marina Bay" is a location. Say what about
  it: its emptiness at 6 a.m., or how small people look beside it.
- **Mixing up the subject and the intent.** "It's about a cat" is still a subject.
  About the cat's what? Its boredom? Its ownership of the doorstep?
- **Squeezing two ideas into one frame.** Make two frames, each with one clear winner.
- **Deciding afterwards.** A crop and a filter can't add an intent that wasn't there
  at capture. Decide first, then edit towards the decision.
- **Holding on to the plan when the scene changes.** If something better happens,
  change the sentence and shoot that.

## Try it

Twenty minutes, any device. A small version of **A3.1**:

1. Pick one ordinary subject near home: your kopi cup, a shoe rack, a void deck bench.
2. Write three different "about" sentences for it, for example *morning calm*,
   *something left behind* and *the heat of the afternoon*.
3. Make one frame for each sentence. Change at least two things each time: distance,
   angle, light or moment.
4. Show the three frames to someone without the sentences. Ask what each is about.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- "A photo of a durian stall." Is that an intent?
> No. It's a subject. An intent says what about the stall: "the seller's pride in his
> best fruit", or "how the smell takes over the whole street". Each of those needs a
> different photo.

> [!question]- Your frame has a laughing child, a bright red car and a big shop sign, all equally strong. Why does it feel weak?
> The viewer's eye jumps between three strong things and can't tell which one matters.
> With no clear winner there's no message. Decide which one the photo is about, then
> remove or weaken the other two.

> [!question]- Your intent is "he is alone in the lunchtime crowd". Would you shoot it wide and close, or long and from further away?
> Long and from further away usually works better. From a distance the crowd's faces
> are drawn at similar sizes and stack into a wall around him. Wide and close makes him
> big and the crowd small and scattered, which says less about being surrounded.

> [!question]- Your intent sentence reads "the market's colours and the seller's tiredness". What does that tell you?
> It's two ideas joined by "and". Make two photos, or find the one frame where the
> colours *show* the tiredness, so the sentence becomes one idea.

> [!question]- Can you decide the intent after you've shot?
> You can discover it while shooting or reviewing, and that's normal. But then go back
> and reshoot towards it. Editing can only remove and adjust. It can't add the moment,
> light or position you didn't choose.
