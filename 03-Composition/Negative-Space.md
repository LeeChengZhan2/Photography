# Negative Space

> [!summary] In one line
> Negative space is the empty area around your subject. It isn't wasted: it isolates
> the subject, gives it room to look or move into, and sets the mood, as long as it's
> genuinely empty.

## What it is

The subject is the **positive space**. The plain, empty area around it is the
**negative space**: sky, still water, a bare wall, fog, a dark shadow, the white
overcast sky behind a bird.

Negative space is part of the composition, not what's left over. Its size, its shape
and its tone all change how the subject reads.

Examples:

- A single swimmer in a huge blue pool, seen from above.
- One person walking along an empty beach at dawn, with a pale sky filling most of the
  frame.
- A bird on a bare branch against a white overcast sky.
- A hawker against a large, plain painted wall.
- A face lit by a window, with the room behind falling into black.

## How it works

### Isolation adds weight

When nothing else competes, the eye goes straight to the subject and stays there. A
small subject in a big empty space can carry more weight than a large one in a busy
scene. → [[Visual-Weight-and-Balance]]

### Space sets size and feeling

Lots of empty space makes the subject look small in its world. Depending on the
subject and the tone, that often reads as solitude, calm, freedom or scale. A dark
empty space feels heavy and quiet; a pale one feels open and light.

### Room to look and move

Your brain predicts where a moving thing will go next, and it follows where people
look. Space in front of a face, or ahead of a moving subject, lets the look or the
movement carry on *inside* the frame. Space behind them instead makes them seem to be
leaving, or about to hit the edge. This space in front is often called **lead room**
or **looking room**.

### The empty space has a shape

The space around a subject makes a shape of its own. A clean, simple shape helps. A
ragged one, with bits poking in from the edges, distracts. And because the space is
plain, anything small inside it, a stray leaf, a dust spot, a distant pole, becomes
very noticeable.

### Minimalism

Minimalist photos use very few elements and a lot of negative space, with simple tones
or colours. They depend entirely on what's left being worth looking at.

### How much is enough?

There's no correct percentage. Use three tests:

- **The space has a job:** isolation, direction, mood or scale. If it does nothing for
  your intent, cut it.
- **The space is clean.** If it holds distractions, it isn't negative space; it's
  clutter.
- **The subject still reads at viewing size.** Look at the photo at the size people
  will see it, usually on a phone. If you can't tell what the subject is, there's too
  much space, or the subject doesn't stand out enough.

## How to use it

- **Find a plain background first:** sky, water, a painted wall, deep shade, an
  overcast sky, haze. Then put the subject in front of it.
- **Change your height to reach it.** Go low to put the sky behind someone; go high to
  put water, grass or a floor behind them. → [[Point-of-View]]
- **Make black space with exposure.** Expose for a subject in bright light and let the
  shade around it go dark. Singapore's hard midday sun makes shadows deep enough for
  this.
- **Put the space where it works:** in front of the subject's gaze or movement, with
  more in front than behind.
- **Watch the meter.** A big bright sky can make the camera darken the whole photo, and
  a big black area can make it brighten everything. Correct it with exposure
  compensation. → [[Exposure-Compensation]]
- **Choose the frame shape.** A wide 16:9 frame holds sideways space; a tall frame
  holds sky above. → [[Frame-and-Aspect-Ratio]]
- **Clean it in the edit.** Remove dust spots and small distractions from the empty
  area. → [[Retouching]]

## Common mistakes

- **Space that isn't empty.** A bin, a wire or a bright patch in the "empty" area
  becomes the thing people look at. Move, or wait until it's clean.
- **A subject too small to read.** On a phone screen it disappears. Move closer, or
  make the subject stand out more in tone or colour.
- **No room in front.** A runner at the edge, running out of the frame, feels cut off.
  Give the space to the side they're heading towards.
- **Confusing a dull background with negative space.** A random grey wall isn't
  negative space unless its emptiness serves the intent.
- **Letting the meter decide.** A big white sky turns grey and a black background turns
  muddy. Use exposure compensation to keep each the tone you want.

## Try it

Twenty minutes. A good warm-up for **A3.4**, because space is what you get when you
subtract. One small subject: a person, a chair or a potted plant, against sky or a
plain wall.

1. Shoot it filling about half the frame, then a quarter, then a tenth, then smaller.
2. Move the subject, or yourself, so the space is in front of it; then behind it.
3. Look at all of them on your phone at normal size. Where does the feeling change?
   Where does the subject get lost?

## Check yourself

> [!question]- A runner is at the left edge, running left, with all the space behind them on the right. How does it feel, and why?
> They seem to be leaving the photo. The viewer's brain predicts where they'll go next,
> and that's outside the frame. Put them on the right, with the space ahead of them.

> [!question]- You photograph a lone tree against a white overcast sky, and the sky comes out grey. Why, and how do you fix it?
> The meter tries to make the large bright sky a middle grey, so it darkens the photo.
> Add about +1 to +2 EV of exposure compensation to make the sky white again.

> [!question]- When is negative space "too much"?
> When it no longer does a job for the intent, or when the subject can't be recognised
> at the size people will see the photo.

> [!question]- Why does one person in an empty space seem to matter more than the same person in a crowd?
> Nothing competes with them, so all the viewer's attention goes to them. Isolation
> adds visual weight.
