# Focal Length and Perspective

> [!summary] In one line
> Focal length decides how much of the scene fits in the frame. Perspective, how big
> near things look compared with far things, is set only by where you stand. Choose
> your distance first, then the focal length that frames it.

## What it is

Two different things get mixed up here:

| Term | What it means | What sets it |
|---|---|---|
| **Field of view** | How much of the scene fits in the frame | Focal length (and sensor size) |
| **Perspective** | How big near and far things look compared with each other, and how far apart they seem | Your distance from each thing |

**Focal length** is measured in millimetres. A longer focal length magnifies more, so it
shows a narrower slice of the scene. Double the focal length and everything in the frame
is drawn twice as tall and twice as wide. From 2 m away, a 24 mm frame is about 3 m
wide, a 75 mm frame about 1 m, and a 120 mm frame about 60 cm.

In this vault, focal lengths are **35 mm equivalent**: the focal length that would give
the same field of view on a full-frame camera. That lets you compare your four devices
directly, whatever their sensor size. → [[Sensor-Size-and-Crop-Factor]]

| Equivalent focal length | Diagonal field of view (about) | Usually called |
|---|---|---|
| 13 mm | 120° | Ultra-wide |
| 20 mm | 94° | Wide |
| 24 mm | 84° | Wide |
| 48 mm | 48° | Normal |
| 75 mm | 32° | Short telephoto |
| 120 mm | 20° | Telephoto |

## How it works

### Why distance, not the lens, sets perspective

A thing's size in the photo depends on its real size divided by its distance from the
camera. The focal length then multiplies *everything* by the same amount. So it changes
how big the whole scene is drawn, but not how big near things are *compared with* far
things. Only distance changes that.

A worked example. Two friends of the same height stand 8 m apart, one behind the other.

| You stand | Near friend | Far friend | Near friend is drawn |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 m from the near friend | 2 m away | 10 m away | 5× taller than the far one (10 ÷ 2) |
| 10 m from the near friend | 10 m away | 18 m away | 1.8× taller than the far one (18 ÷ 10) |

At 2 m you'd use 24 mm to fit the near friend in. At 10 m you'd need 120 mm (five times
the focal length for five times the distance) to make them the same size in the frame.
The near friend looks the same in both photos. The far friend looks almost three times
bigger in the second (5 ÷ 1.8). The lens didn't do that. Stepping back did.

### "Telephoto compression", explained properly

People say long lenses "compress" space: the background looks bigger and closer, and
things in a row look packed together. The effect is real. The cause is distance.

From far away, the gaps between things are small compared with your distance to them,
so they're drawn at similar sizes and seem stacked. A long lens is simply the tool that
lets you fill the frame from that far away. The proof: shoot at 24 mm, then crop the
middle until it matches a 120 mm shot taken from the same spot. The perspective is
identical. The crop just has fewer pixels.

A long lens also shows a much narrower slice of background. At 120 mm you see a fifth
of the width you'd see at 24 mm from the same spot, so one tree or one wall can fill
the whole background. That's why long lenses are good at simplifying.

The opposite is **expansion**. With a wide lens close to the subject, near things loom
large, far things shrink, and the space between them seems to stretch.

### Why wide-angle close-ups distort faces

A face has depth: the tip of the nose is about 10 cm closer to you than the ears.

- From 40 cm, the ears are about 50 cm away. The nose is drawn about 25% bigger,
  compared with the ears, than it would be if both were the same distance away.
- From 2 m, the ears are about 2.1 m away. The difference drops to about 5%.

At 40 cm the nose looks big, the face narrows and the ears start to disappear. That's
why head-and-shoulders portraits are usually shot from more than a metre away, with a
longer focal length. Again, the cause is the distance. A wide lens only tempts you to
stand that close.

A second, separate effect: a wide lens stretches things near the edges and corners of
the frame. A face at the edge of a 13 mm group photo looks pulled outwards. Keep people
away from the edges of ultra-wide frames.

Neither of these is **lens distortion** (barrel or pincushion), which bends straight
lines and is fixed with a lens profile in your editor. → [[Lens-Corrections]]

## How to use it

Decide in this order:

1. **Distance first.** It sets perspective. Do you want the background big and close
   behind the subject (stand far back), or small and far away with lots of space
   around it (get close)?
2. **Then focal length**, to frame the subject from that distance.
3. **Then fine-tune by moving your feet**, not by turning the zoom.

| You want | Do this | Because |
|---|---|---|
| A big, looming background, like a block of flats behind a friend | Stand far back, 75–120 mm | From far away, the background stays large compared with the subject |
| The subject plus lots of setting | Get close, 13–24 mm | Close up, the subject is big and the background small and broad |
| A flattering face | 1.2 m or more away, 75–120 mm | The nose and ears are at nearly the same relative distance |
| A packed, crowded street | Far back, long lens | People at different distances are drawn at similar sizes |
| Drama and depth, a big foreground | Very close to the foreground, ultra-wide | Near things grow much faster than far things as you approach |
| A clean background | Long lens, from further back | A narrow slice of background is easier to keep plain |

Rules of thumb:

- **Zoom is for framing; feet are for perspective.** Zooming from a fixed spot is the
  same as cropping. A3.2, the "prime jail", trains this habit.
- **For portraits, start at the long end:** 75 mm on the L10, from about 1.2 m.
- **For depth, get very close to something in the foreground** with your widest lens.

## Common mistakes

- **Zooming instead of moving.** The zoom changes how much fits, not how near and far
  things relate. Choose where to stand first.
- **Saying "the long lens compressed it".** Your distance did. Knowing that tells you
  to step back, not to reach for more zoom.
- **Shooting portraits at 24 mm from arm's length.** The nose grows and the face
  narrows. Step back to 1.2 m or more and use 75 mm.
- **Putting faces at the edges of an ultra-wide frame.** Edge stretch pulls them out
  of shape. Keep people nearer the centre at 13 mm.
- **Going wide "to fit everything in".** Far things shrink to nothing and the photo
  feels empty. Get close to one strong foreground instead.
- **Tilting a wide lens up at a building.** The top is further away than the base, so
  the sides lean in. Keep the camera level, or step back. → [[Perspective-Correction]]

## Try it

Twenty minutes. A small version of **A3.3**. You need a friend and a background with
depth: a block of flats, a row of trees, a road.

1. L10 at 24 mm. Hold the camera vertically and fill the frame with your friend's head
   and shoulders. You'll be about 40–50 cm away. If it won't focus, switch the focus
   selector to AF Macro: normal AF stops at 50 cm.
2. L10 at 75 mm. Step back until the head and shoulders are the same size again:
   about 1.2 m.
3. iPhone at 5× (120 mm). Step back again: about 2 m.
4. Put the three side by side. Compare the face shape, how big the background looks,
   and how much of it you can see.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- You shoot at 24 mm, then crop the middle to look like 75 mm, without moving. Is the perspective different from a real 75 mm shot from the same spot?
> No. Both were taken from the same place, so near and far things keep the same size
> relationship. The crop just has fewer pixels.

> [!question]- Why do cars on a long road look packed bumper to bumper in a 120 mm photo?
> You were standing far away. The gaps between the cars are small compared with your
> distance to them, so they're drawn at similar sizes and look stacked. The long lens
> only made it possible to fill the frame from there.

> [!question]- A friend says their nose looks huge in your close-up. What do you change, and why does it work?
> Step back to 1.2 m or more and zoom to 75 mm, or use the iPhone's 120 mm. From
> further away, the nose and ears are at nearly the same relative distance, so they're
> drawn in nearly true proportion.

> [!question]- You want a small person dwarfed by a huge building behind them. Wide and close, or long and far?
> Long and far. From a distance the building keeps its real size compared with the
> person, so it towers over them. Wide and close does the opposite: the person becomes
> big and the building shrinks into the background.

> [!question]- You stay where you are and zoom from 24 mm to 75 mm. What changes, and what doesn't?
> The field of view narrows, so everything is drawn about three times bigger and less
> fits in. The perspective doesn't change at all, because you haven't moved.
