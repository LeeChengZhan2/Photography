# Juxtaposition

> [!summary] In one line
> Juxtaposition is putting two things side by side so that together they say something
> neither says alone: a contrast, a joke, an irony or a sense of scale. The meaning
> lives in the relationship, so both parts must be clear.

## What it is

Two, sometimes three, elements share the frame, and the viewer reads them as connected.
The connection creates the meaning:

| Kind | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Contrast | Opposites side by side | A row of old shophouses in front of a glass tower |
| Irony | A sign or picture says one thing; the scene shows another | A sunny beach-holiday poster above a crowded, rain-soaked bus stop |
| Humour | A visual coincidence | A dog with the same fluffy hair as its owner |
| Scale | Something small against something huge | A gardener trimming the base of a giant rain tree |
| Echo | Two similar shapes, colours or poses | A woman in a striped shirt beside a striped awning |

## How it works

### Viewers assume that things in one frame belong together

When two things share a frame, the viewer assumes they're related and tries to work
out how, much like reading a sentence. The meaning isn't printed in the photo; the
viewer builds it. That's why a good juxtaposition is satisfying: the viewer gets the
joke or the idea themselves, so it sticks.

Film editors know the same effect as the **Kuleshov effect**: the same neutral face,
cut next to different shots, seems to show different feelings. A photo does it inside
one frame instead of across a cut.

### Similarity first, then difference

The eye groups things that look alike: the same shape, colour, size or pose. Once two
things are grouped, their *difference* becomes the message. Shophouses and a tower are
all buildings, but some are old and small and one is new and huge. That difference
says *a city changing*.

### Both parts must be readable

If one element is too small, too blurred or too far from the other, the viewer never
links them and sees only one subject. The two parts need similar visual weight, and a
clear path for the eye between them. → [[Visual-Weight-and-Balance]]

### A third element dilutes it

A juxtaposition is a sentence with two words. A third strong element turns it into
noise, so simplify around the pair. → [[Simplification]]

## How to use it

### Finding it

1. **Collect stages.** Posters, murals, statues, shop signs and billboards are
   ready-made halves. Note where they are.
2. **Wait for the other half.** Stand at the stage and wait for a person, an animal or
   a vehicle that completes the idea. Elliott Erwitt is the master of this kind of wit:
   look at how often his photos depend on two things lining up for a moment.
   → [[Photographers-to-Study]]
3. **Make both parts readable.** Get close enough, or use a longer focal length, so
   neither part is tiny. Keep both sharp enough to recognise: f/5.6–f/8 on the L10; the
   other three devices keep most things sharp anyway.
4. **Place them so the eye travels between them:** on opposite thirds, or one near and
   one far.
5. **Time it.** Many juxtapositions last a second, like a person passing under a
   poster. → [[Gesture-and-Moment]]

Your intent is still one sentence: "*this* next to *that* says *something*". The pair is
one idea, not two.

### Finding or staging?

| | Found | Staged |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | You notice it and wait; you only change your position and timing | You arrange it, for example by asking a friend to stand under a sign |
| Viewers assume | It really happened | Nothing, unless you tell them |
| Fine for | Street, documentary, travel | Personal, fun, commercial and conceptual work |
| The rule | — | Never present a staged scene as candid |

Choosing where to stand and when to press is composition, not staging. Arranging the
people or objects is staging. In documentary and news work, staging a scene and
presenting it as real is a serious breach of trust. → [[Documentary-and-Photojournalism]]

### Ethics in street photography

Juxtaposition often makes someone the punchline. Before you post, ask:

- **Who is the joke on?** Laughing at a situation is different from laughing at a
  person's body, age, poverty or disability. Aim the humour at the situation.
- **Would I be comfortable if they saw it, with this caption?** If not, don't post it.
- **Is the caption true?** A person can look as if they're reacting to a sign they
  never saw. Don't caption the coincidence as fact.
- **Is it a child?** Take extra care before posting an identifiable child without a
  parent's agreement.

The law is a separate question from ethics. In Singapore, as in many places, taking
photos in public places is generally allowed, but some places forbid it (look for
signs), and how you publish can matter. Laws change, and this isn't legal advice.
→ [[Copyright-and-Consent]]

## Common mistakes

- **One half too small or unclear.** The viewer sees one subject and misses the link.
  Get closer, or use a longer focal length.
- **A third strong element.** It dilutes the pair. Simplify.
- **Punching down.** Mocking someone's looks or circumstances isn't wit. Aim at the
  situation, not the person.
- **Staging and calling it candid.** Say so if you arranged it.
- **Explaining the joke.** A caption that spells out the link kills it. Let the viewer
  find it.

## Try it

Thirty minutes on a street with posters, murals or shop signs:

1. Find three stages: a poster with a face, a sign with a strong word or picture, and
   a mural.
2. At each one, wait for a person or an animal to complete a relationship: an echo, a
   contrast of scale, or an irony.
3. Shoot at least ten frames per stage as people pass.
4. Pick the best one and write its sentence: "*this* next to *that* says *something*".

Waiting at one spot like this is also good practice for **A3.1**.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why does a viewer find meaning in two unrelated things just because they share a frame?
> Viewers assume that things shown together are connected, and they work out how. The
> viewer builds the meaning, which is why a good juxtaposition feels satisfying.

> [!question]- In your photo of a beach poster above a wet bus stop, the poster is a small patch in the corner. Why doesn't it work?
> The poster is too small to read, so the viewer never links the two halves. Move
> closer, or use a longer focal length, so the poster and the queue carry similar
> weight.

> [!question]- You ask a friend to pose under a funny sign. Is that wrong?
> No, as long as you don't present it as a candid moment. Staging is fine for personal
> and fun work; passing a staged scene off as found is the problem.

> [!question]- Your funniest frame depends on a tired, elderly cleaner next to a luxury advert. What should you ask before posting?
> Who the joke is on. If the humour comes from the person's age, work or circumstances,
> it's at their expense. Ask whether you'd be comfortable if they saw it; if not, don't
> post it.
