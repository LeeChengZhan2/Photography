# Gesture and Moment

> [!summary] In one line
> Composition is *where* things are; the moment is *when*. A strong frame becomes a
> strong photo at the instant a gesture, an expression or a movement peaks, and catching
> that instant takes anticipation, not luck.

## What it is

- **Gesture:** a movement of the body or face that shows feeling or intention. A hand
  reaching out, a head thrown back laughing, a shrug, a child mid-jump.
- **Moment:** the instant when everything lines up: the gesture, where the people are,
  the light and the composition.
- **The decisive moment:** a phrase linked with Henri Cartier-Bresson. *The Decisive
  Moment* was the English title of his 1952 book. He described it as recognising, in a
  fraction of a second, both what an event means and the arrangement of shapes that
  expresses it.

His photo *Behind the Gare Saint-Lazare* (1932) is the classic example. A man leaps
over a flooded patch of ground behind a Paris railway station. He's caught in the air,
his heel just above the water, his reflection below him. A fraction of a second later
it would just be a man with a wet shoe.

## How it works

### We read bodies fast

People read faces and body language automatically, in a fraction of a second. A
gesture at its peak, like the widest laugh or the top of a jump, is clear: the viewer
knows at once what's happening and how it feels. A frame between gestures, with a
half-blink or a half-open mouth, looks awkward, because the body is in transition and
doesn't mean anything yet.

### The peak of action

Many movements have a point where they briefly stop: a jumper at the top of the jump,
a swing at the end of its arc, a hawker's noodles at the top of a toss. At that instant
the subject is barely moving. It's the clearest moment, and a slower shutter speed can
freeze it.

### Unfinished action holds tension

The instant *before* something happens, the foot about to land or the hand about to
catch, can be stronger than the event itself. The viewer completes the action in their
head, and that keeps them looking. → [[Diagonals-and-Dynamism]]

### You have to press early

Your reaction time to something you see is roughly 0.2 s, and the camera adds its own
small delay. A person walking at a normal pace covers about 30 cm in 0.2 s. So if you
press when you *see* the moment, you capture the moment just after it. Watch the rhythm
of an action and press just *before* the peak.

### Structure plus timing

A great composition with no moment is an empty stage. A great moment in a messy frame
is a lucky snapshot. The strongest photos put the moment into a structure you built
first: set the frame, then wait for the gesture.

## How to use it

1. **Build the stage first.** Find the light, the background and the composition, then
   wait for someone to walk in. Cartier-Bresson is known for working this way.
   → [[Photographers-to-Study]]
2. **Pre-focus** on the spot where the action will happen, so the camera doesn't hunt
   for focus at the vital instant. → [[Back-Button-Focus]]
3. **Set a fast enough shutter speed:** about 1/250–1/500 s for people walking and for
   hand gestures, 1/1000 s or faster for sport. → [[Shutter-Speed]]
4. **Learn the rhythm.** Repeated actions, like a wok toss, a skipping rope or a
   badminton smash, come round again. Watch two or three cycles, then press just before
   the peak.
5. **Wait for the expression:** the laugh after the joke, the look after the effort.
   Keep the camera up between them.
6. **Work the scene.** Stay longer than feels comfortable. People often stop noticing a
   photographer who stays, and the moments get better.
7. **Choose by gesture first.** When picking between frames, the best gesture usually
   beats the sharpest file. → [[Culling-Discipline]]

### Burst, or one deliberate frame?

| | Burst (many frames per second) | One deliberate frame |
|---|---|---|
| Good for | Fast, unpredictable action: sport, children, animals | Gestures you can read and predict; learning timing |
| Catches the peak? | Often, but the peak can fall between frames | Only if you anticipate, which is what trains you |
| Cost | Hundreds of near-identical frames to sort | Missed moments while you learn |

Even at 10 frames per second there's 0.1 s between frames, and a blink or the instant a
shuttlecock meets the racket can fit in that gap. A good middle way is a short burst of
three to five frames, starting just before the peak.

## On your kit

- **Lumix L10:** for fast gestures, use the mechanical shutter (up to 1/2000 s). The
  electronic shutter goes to 1/32000 s, but it reads the sensor row by row, so fast
  movement leans. Set [Shutter AF] to off and focus with [AF ON], so you can focus on a
  spot early and keep it. Turn [Auto Review] off, so the screen isn't showing the last
  frame while the next moment happens.
- **iPhone, Pocket 3, Action 5 Pro:** all rolling-shutter devices. They're fine for
  people walking and talking, but very fast movement, like a swung racket, can lean.
  On the iPhone, press and hold on the spot to lock focus and exposure (AE/AF LOCK)
  before the action arrives.

## Common mistakes

- **Pressing when you see the moment.** By then it's gone. Watch the rhythm and press
  just before the peak.
- **Spraying bursts instead of watching.** You get hundreds of frames and still miss
  the peak. Watch first, then shoot short bursts.
- **Leaving after one frame.** The first frame is rarely the best. Stay and work the
  scene.
- **Chasing the action with no frame.** You get the moment in a messy composition. Set
  the frame and let the action come to you.
- **A shutter speed too slow for the gesture.** Hands and faces blur first. Use
  1/250 s or faster.
- **Checking the screen after every shot.** The next moment happens while you look
  down. Review later.

## Try it

Twenty minutes somewhere with a repeated action: a hawker tossing noodles, a playground
swing, a badminton game at a community centre.

1. Choose one fixed frame and pre-focus on the spot where the action peaks.
2. Make one deliberate frame per cycle for ten cycles, pressing just before the peak.
3. Then shoot ten short bursts.
4. Count your hits in each set. Which gave you better gestures, not just sharper
   frames?

Twenty minutes of frames from one spot are good material for **A3.1**.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why is the top of a jump easier to freeze than the take-off?
> At the top, the jumper briefly stops rising before they fall, so they're barely
> moving. A slower shutter speed freezes them, and the pose reads clearly.

> [!question]- You press the shutter the instant someone laughs, and the photo shows the laugh fading. Why?
> Your reaction time, about 0.2 s, plus the camera's own delay put the capture after
> the peak. Anticipate: press as the laugh starts to build.

> [!question]- You shoot a burst at 10 frames per second. Why can you still miss the moment?
> There's 0.1 s between frames, and many peaks, like a blink or a shuttlecock meeting
> the racket, are shorter than that. The peak can fall between two frames.

> [!question]- Why set up the frame first and wait, instead of following the subject around?
> Following the subject gives you the moment in whatever frame happened to be there.
> Waiting in a frame you chose lets you control the light, the background and the
> composition, so the moment lands in a strong photo.

> [!question]- On the L10, why use the mechanical shutter for a fast gesture?
> The electronic shutter reads the sensor row by row, so a fast hand or racket can lean
> or bend. The mechanical shutter greatly reduces that.
