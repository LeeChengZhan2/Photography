# Tonal Composition

> [!summary] In one line
> Under the colour and the detail, every photo is a few big shapes of light and dark.
> The eye lands first on the brightest, most contrasting area, so arrange those shapes
> so that it lands on your subject.

## What it is

**Tone** means how light or dark something is, ignoring its colour. **Tonal
composition** is how the light, middle and dark areas, called **masses**, are arranged
in the frame.

**Notan** is a Japanese word for the balance of light and dark. In art teaching it
means a design reduced to two values, black and white, or three with a middle grey.
The American teacher Arthur Wesley Dow brought it into Western art education around
1900. A photo with a strong notan still reads when every detail is gone.

| Key | Mostly | Tends to feel |
|---|---|---|
| High key | Light tones | Open, airy, gentle |
| Low key | Dark tones | Moody, dramatic, quiet |
| Full range | Everything from black to white | Solid and punchy |

## How it works

### Where the eye lands

Your visual system responds to differences in brightness more than to brightness
itself. So the eye goes first to:

1. **The brightest area**, especially in a darker frame.
2. **The point of highest contrast**, where the lightest light meets the darkest dark.

Put your subject there and the viewer finds it at once. Put something else there and
the viewer looks at that instead, however carefully you placed the subject.

Faces pull the eye too. The strongest frames put the face *on* the brightest,
highest-contrast area, so tone and face agree. → [[Visual-Weight-and-Balance]]

### Why masses matter more than detail

Only the centre of your vision sees fine detail; everything around it sees big shapes
and tones. When a viewer first looks at a photo, or sees it as a small thumbnail, they
take in the masses before the detail. If the masses are messy, with bright spots
scattered everywhere, the photo reads as messy before anyone sees what it shows.

Simple tonal structures read fast: two to five big shapes of clearly different tones.
Fan Ho built his Hong Kong photographs from shafts of light and deep shadow. Josef
Koudelka's black-and-white frames rest on big dark and light shapes.
→ [[Photographers-to-Study]]

### The squint test

Squinting blurs the detail and leaves only the masses, much like a first glance. Ask:

- Where does my eye go first? Is it the subject?
- How many big masses are there? Are they simple?
- Does the subject sit against the opposite tone: light on dark, or dark on light?

**A3.7** does the same thing more strictly: convert frames to blurred greyscale. If a
frame stops reading, it was relying on detail.

### Thinking in black and white

Colour can hide a weak tonal structure. A red flower among green leaves looks obvious
in colour, but the red and the green can have the same brightness. In greyscale they
merge into one grey. If your subject only stands out by its hue, the composition is
fragile. → [[Black-and-White-Conversion]]

## How to use it

1. **Look at the light before the subject.** Half-close your eyes and find the light
   and dark shapes in the scene.
2. **Put the subject on the brightest or highest-contrast area**, or wait for it to
   move there: a person stepping into a strip of sunlight in a shaded five-foot way.
3. **Put the subject against the opposite tone:** a lit face against a dark doorway,
   a dark silhouette against a bright sky.
4. **Expose for the important highlight** and let the shadows go dark. Less detail in
   the shadows means simpler masses. Negative exposure compensation, around −1 EV,
   often does it. → [[Exposure-Compensation]]
5. **Use hard light.** Singapore's strong sun from late morning to mid-afternoon makes
   deep, sharp-edged shadows: ready-made graphic shapes. → [[Quality-of-Light]]
6. **Preview in black and white.** If your camera can show a black-and-white preview
   while it saves RAW, the RAW file keeps the colour and you see only the tones.
7. **Shape the masses in the edit.** Darken distracting bright spots and brighten the
   subject. → [[Dodge-and-Burn]]

## Common mistakes

- **Bright spots scattered around the frame.** Gaps of sky through leaves, white cars,
  shiny signs. Each one pulls the eye. Change your angle, or darken them later.
- **A mid-grey subject on a mid-grey background.** It merges. Find a background of the
  opposite tone.
- **Relying on colour for separation.** It disappears in black and white, and it's
  weaker than you think in colour too. Check the tones with a squint.
- **The brightest area at the edge.** It leads the eye out. Recompose, or darken it in
  the edit.
- **Fixing in the edit what one step would fix.** Dodging and burning can shape the
  masses, but moving so a dark wall sits behind the subject is quicker and looks
  natural.

## Try it

Thirty minutes. A small version of **A3.7**:

1. Take ten of your recent frames. Convert each to black and white and blur it heavily,
   or shrink it to a thumbnail.
2. Sort them into "still reads" and "doesn't". For each one that fails, say why.
3. Go out and shoot ten new frames where the subject is the brightest area, or the
   point of highest contrast.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why does the squint test reveal composition problems?
> Squinting removes detail and leaves only the big light and dark masses, which is
> roughly what a first glance or a thumbnail shows. If the masses don't lead to the
> subject, the photo won't either.

> [!question]- A red flower against green leaves looks great in colour but vanishes in black and white. Why?
> The red and the green have about the same brightness. In colour they're separated by
> hue; in greyscale only brightness is left, so they merge.

> [!question]- Your subject stands in shade, with a sunlit white car behind them. Where does the eye go, and what can you do?
> To the car: it's the brightest, highest-contrast area. Move so the car is out of the
> frame or hidden, wait for it to leave, or move the subject into the light.

> [!question]- What does a mostly dark frame with one small bright area do to the viewer?
> It sends the eye straight to the bright area and holds it there. The frame feels
> low-key and quiet, and whatever is in the light becomes very strong.
