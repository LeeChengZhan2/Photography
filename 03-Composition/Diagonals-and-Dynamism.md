# Diagonals and Dynamism

> [!summary] In one line
> The direction of lines sets the mood: horizontals feel calm, verticals feel strong,
> and diagonals feel like movement. To make a still frame feel active, find or create
> diagonals.

## What it is

Every frame has a main direction of lines and shapes, and each direction carries a
feeling:

| Direction | Feels | Because in life it's | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Horizontal | Calm, rest, stability | Things lying down, the horizon, still water | A sea horizon, a person lying on grass, a long low building |
| Vertical | Strength, height, formality | Things standing up and holding firm | Trees, columns, a standing person, a block of flats |
| Diagonal | Movement, energy, tension | Things falling, climbing, running or leaning | A staircase, an escalator, a runner leaning forward, a slanting shadow |

**Dynamism** is the sense that something in a still photo is moving, or about to move.
Diagonals are the main way to build it.

## How it works

### Gravity gives directions their meaning

We live with gravity, so we read every line against it. A horizontal is at rest: it
can't fall any further. A vertical is upright and balanced, but alert. A diagonal is
neither. It looks like something in the middle of changing, falling over or rising up,
so the eye reads it as unfinished motion. That's why a tilted line feels active even
when nothing in the photo is moving.

### Diagonals cross more of the frame

The longest straight line in any rectangle runs from corner to corner. A diagonal can
therefore join a near corner of the scene to a far one, carrying the eye through the
frame and into depth. Horizontal and vertical lines run parallel to the frame's edges,
so they divide the frame instead of crossing it.

### Triangles: three points, three lines

Three points that aren't in a straight line make a triangle, and at least two of its
sides are usually diagonals. The eye travels around it. A triangle sitting on its base,
like a pyramid, feels stable; one balanced on a point, or tilted, feels dynamic. Three
heads at three different heights form a triangle and hold the eye better than three
heads in a row.

### Tilting the camera: the Dutch angle

A **Dutch angle** is a deliberately tilted camera. Every vertical and horizontal becomes
a diagonal, and the whole world seems to slide. Films use it for unease and confusion.
It works in small doses. When every frame tilts, the viewer only notices the tilt. And
a small tilt of two or three degrees doesn't read as a choice; it reads as a crooked
horizon.

### Movement along a diagonal

A cyclist crossing the frame from side to side moves parallel to the frame and stays
the same size. A cyclist riding diagonally towards you grows as they come, crosses the
depth of the scene and covers more of the frame. The second one feels faster and
deeper.

## How to use it

- **Turn to make diagonals.** A railing shot square-on is a horizontal. Turn about
  30–45° to it and it becomes a diagonal. Most lines can be made diagonal just by
  changing where you stand.
- **Run the line from corner to corner**, or close to it, for the strongest effect.
- **Look for ready-made diagonals:** staircases, escalators, ramps, roof lines, leaning
  trees, a hawker's arm tossing a wok, long morning and evening shadows.
- **Build triangles:** three people at different heights, a person with hands on hips,
  a roof against the sky.
- **Anchor the energy.** One calm horizontal or vertical, like the horizon or a wall,
  gives the eye a place to rest, so the diagonals don't turn into chaos.
- **Use the Dutch angle rarely**, and tilt clearly when you do, so no one could mistake
  it for a crooked horizon.
- **Shoot movement on the diagonal:** stand where the runner, bus or cyclist moves from
  a back corner towards a front corner.

In Singapore the sun is high for most of the day, so long, slanting shadows only
appear in roughly the first and last two hours of daylight. Plan shadow diagonals for
then. → [[Direction-of-Light]]

## Common mistakes

- **An accidental tilt.** A horizon a couple of degrees off reads as a mistake, not as
  energy. Level it, or tilt clearly. → [[Crop-and-Straighten]]
- **The Dutch angle as a habit.** Used everywhere, it stops meaning anything.
- **Diagonals that lead out of the frame.** The eye follows them out. End them on the
  subject. → [[Leading-Lines]]
- **Squaring up to everything.** Shooting every building front and railing straight on
  gives you only horizontals and verticals: fine for calm subjects, flat for energetic
  ones.
- **Too many crossing diagonals.** With no calm line anywhere, the frame feels like
  noise. Keep one steady element.

## Try it

Fifteen minutes at a staircase, an escalator or a long railing:

1. Shoot it square-on, so its lines are horizontal.
2. Turn 30–45° and shoot again, so the lines run diagonally.
3. Tilt the camera clearly for a Dutch angle.
4. Wait for a person to move along the diagonal, and shoot that.

Compare the four. Which feels like movement, and which feels calm? Use them as frames
for **A3.1**.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- The same railing, shot square-on and then from a 45° angle. Why does the second feel more active?
> Square-on, the railing is a horizontal, which reads as rest. From an angle it becomes
> a diagonal, which reads as something mid-movement, and it leads the eye into the
> depth of the scene.

> [!question]- Why does a two-degree tilt look worse than a twenty-degree tilt?
> Two degrees reads as a crooked horizon: a mistake. Twenty degrees is clearly a
> choice, so the viewer reads it as unease or energy instead.

> [!question]- Three friends stand in a row at the same height. Then one sits and one stands on a step. Why is the second version stronger?
> Their heads now form a triangle. Its diagonal sides lead the eye from face to face,
> instead of along a flat row.

> [!question]- One cyclist passes you side to side; another rides diagonally towards you. Which shows more depth, and why?
> The diagonal one. They grow as they approach and cross the depth of the scene, and
> their path cuts across the frame instead of running parallel to its edge.
