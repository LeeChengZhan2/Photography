# Rule of Thirds

> [!summary] In one line
> Imagine two lines across the frame and two lines down it, evenly spaced, and place
> the subject on a line or where two lines cross. It's a training wheel: a quick way to
> move the subject out of the dead centre and leave room for the rest of the story.

## What it is

Divide the frame into three equal parts across and three equal parts down. You get a
grid of nine boxes, with four lines and four crossing points.

The rule says:

- Put the main subject, or its most important part, on or near a **crossing point**.
- Put strong lines on a **grid line**: the horizon on the upper or lower line, a
  standing person or a tree on one of the vertical lines.

In a portrait the most important part is usually the eyes, so the eyes go on or near
the upper line. In a landscape the horizon goes on the upper or lower line, not
through the middle.

## How it works

### Why the centre can feel static

A subject in the exact centre has the same space on every side. Nothing pulls the eye
one way or the other, so the eye lands, finds no direction, and stops. That stillness
is right for some photos (→ [[Symmetry-and-Centring]]). Often, though, it makes a photo
feel like a record rather than a moment.

Move the subject off-centre and the spaces become unequal: a small one on one side, a
large one on the other. The eye goes to the subject, travels into the larger space,
then comes back. That movement makes the frame feel alive.

### Why it leaves room

An off-centre subject leaves a large area free. You can use it for:

- **Space to look or move into.** A person on the left third, facing right, has room
  in front of them. → [[Negative-Space]]
- **Context.** The subject on one third, the place that explains them on the other.
- **A second element** that balances the first. → [[Visual-Weight-and-Balance]]

### Why a third, and not a quarter?

There's nothing magic about exactly one third. A subject very close to the edge feels
cramped and pulls the eye out of the frame. One in the exact centre feels static. A
third is a simple, easy-to-see point between the two. Treat the lines as a zone, not a
target.

### The horizon is a decision

A horizon through the middle splits the frame into two equal halves that compete. On
the lower third, the sky gets two thirds of the frame; on the upper third, the land or
the sea does. So placing the horizon really means deciding which half is more
interesting.

### The golden ratio, briefly

The golden ratio is about 1 : 1.618. A "phi grid" puts its lines at about 38% and 62%
of the frame instead of 33% and 67%, so a little closer to the centre. The golden
spiral is a related overlay. Claims that famous paintings follow it exactly are often
stretched. In practice the difference from thirds is small; use either as a rough
guide.

## How to use it

1. **Turn on the grid.** Most cameras and phone camera apps can show a 3 × 3 grid on
   the screen or in the viewfinder. Find the grid setting in each device's menu or
   manual. The grid also helps you keep horizons level.
2. **Place the most important point, not the whole subject.** The eyes, a hand, the
   lit window. The rest of the subject can spread across the grid.
3. **Give space in front.** Use the third that leaves the bigger space on the side the
   subject looks or moves towards.
4. **Choose the horizon line.** Interesting sky: horizon on the lower third.
   Interesting land or water: horizon on the upper third.
5. **Move the focus point onto the subject**, or focus and then recompose. Many
   cameras won't pick an off-centre subject on their own. → [[Autofocus-Modes]]

Some examples:

- A fisherman on the left third of a sea wall at dawn, facing right, with the sea and
  sky in front of him. Horizon on the lower third if the sky has colour.
- A hawker on the right third, with the steam and light of the stall spreading across
  the other two thirds.
- A cyclist entering from the left, placed on the left third, so they ride into the
  frame rather than out of it.

### When to break it

- **Symmetry and reflections:** the centre line is the point.
- **A direct portrait**, with the person looking straight into the lens.
- **A subject that fills the frame:** there's no space left to arrange.
- **A square frame**, which often suits a centred subject.
- **When the "empty" two thirds would be clutter:** centre the subject and crop tight
  instead.
- **When strong lines lead elsewhere:** follow the lines, not the grid.

## Common mistakes

- **Putting every subject on a crossing point.** Your photos start to look the same.
  Use the grid to escape the centre, then judge each frame on its own.
- **Facing the subject out of the frame.** A person on the right third looking right
  takes the viewer's eye straight out. Swap sides.
- **A horizon in the middle by habit.** Decide which half deserves more space.
- **A tilted horizon.** The grid makes a tilt easy to see, so use it: line the
  horizon up with a grid line. → [[Crop-and-Straighten]]
- **Placing the subject and forgetting the other two thirds.** The space must be clean
  or useful. Otherwise it's just a bigger area for clutter.

## Try it

Fifteen minutes, grid on. One subject with a horizon behind it: a person on a beach, a
lamp post by a field, a bench by the water.

1. Shoot it centred, then on the left third, then on the right third.
2. Shoot the horizon through the middle, on the upper third, and on the lower third.
3. Pick the frame that best fits a one-sentence intent, and say what the other two
   thirds are doing in it.

These count as different frames for **A3.1**.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why does a subject in the exact centre often feel static?
> It has equal space on every side, so nothing pulls the eye in any direction. The eye
> lands, settles and stops moving around the frame.

> [!question]- A seascape has interesting rocks and a plain grey sky. Where does the horizon go?
> On the upper third. That gives two thirds of the frame to the rocks and only one
> third to the sky, which has nothing to show.

> [!question]- A person stands on the right third, looking right, out of the frame. What happens, and what's the fix?
> The viewer's eye follows their gaze straight out of the photo. Put them on the left
> third, looking right, so the space they look into is inside the frame.

> [!question]- Name two situations where centring beats the rule of thirds.
> A symmetric scene or a reflection, where the centre line is the point. A direct
> portrait with the person looking into the lens. A small subject alone in a big empty
> space often works centred too.

> [!question]- How different is a golden-ratio grid from a thirds grid?
> Its lines sit at about 38% and 62% of the frame instead of 33% and 67%, slightly
> closer to the centre. The difference is small; both are rough guides.
