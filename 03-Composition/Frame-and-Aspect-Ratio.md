# Frame and Aspect Ratio

> [!summary] In one line
> The aspect ratio is the shape of your frame: its width compared with its height. The
> shape decides which way the eye travels and how much room you have, so choose it, and
> the orientation, for each photo instead of leaving both on the default.

## What it is

**Aspect ratio** is the width of the frame compared with its height. **Orientation** is
whether the long side runs across (horizontal, often called "landscape") or up and
down (vertical, often called "portrait").

| Ratio | Shape | Where you meet it |
|---|---|---|
| 3:2 | 1.5 times as wide as tall | 35 mm film and most interchangeable-lens cameras; 6 × 4 inch prints |
| 4:3 | 1.33 times as wide as tall | Four Thirds sensors such as the L10's; many phones |
| 1:1 | Square | 6 × 6 medium-format film; Instagram's original format |
| 16:9 | 1.78 times as wide as tall | TVs, YouTube, most video |
| 9:16 | 16:9 stood upright | Full-screen phone video: Stories, Reels, Shorts, TikTok |
| 4:5 | A little taller than wide | Tall feed posts on Instagram and similar apps |

Social platforms change their preferred shapes and sizes often. Check the current ones
before a project. → [[Video-Export-Settings]]

## How it works

### The eye travels along the long side

You scan a picture along its longest side, so the shape sets the direction of looking:

- **Horizontal frames** move the eye side to side. That matches how we see: our eyes
  sit side by side and our view of the world is wide. Horizontals feel calm and
  natural, and suit things that spread out: streets, horizons, a row of people.
- **Vertical frames** move the eye up and down. They suit things that stand or rise: a
  person, a tree, a tower, a staircase. They also cut off the sides, which often
  removes clutter.
- **Square frames** have no long side, so the eye tends to circle the centre and
  settle. Squares suit centred, symmetric and still subjects.
- **Very wide frames** (16:9 and wider) look like a still from a film. They suit a
  scene with action spread across it.

### More width means more edge to manage

A wider frame gives more room for side-by-side relationships, and more room for clutter
at the sides. Every edge is a decision about what to cut. Something half in the frame
at an edge tends to pull the eye, because the viewer wonders what it is.

### Choosing a ratio: a crop, or a different frame?

The lens projects a circle of light, called the **image circle**. The sensor is a
rectangle inside it. On most cameras, choosing 16:9 just cuts strips off the top and
bottom of the sensor's own shape. You lose pixels and gain nothing.

The L10 is different. It has a **multi-aspect sensor**: the sensor is a little larger
than any one frame you can record, and each ratio uses its own rectangle, with about
the same diagonal, inside the same circle of light.

| L10 ratio | Width | Compared with 4:3 |
|---|---|---|
| 4:3 | 5200 px | — |
| 3:2 | 5408 px | Wider, a little less tall |
| 16:9 | 5664 px | Wider still, less tall again |

So on the L10, 16:9 at 24 mm really shows *more* of the scene left to right than 4:3
does. Picking a ratio is a framing decision, not a loss.

## How to use it

1. **Ask which way the subject's main line runs.** Across (a street, a horizon, people
   in a row): go horizontal. Up (a standing person, a tree, a tower): go vertical.
2. **Ask where the photo will be seen.** A phone held upright favours 9:16 or 4:5. A
   TV or laptop favours 16:9. A print favours 3:2 or 4:3.
3. **Set the ratio in camera when you know the destination.** You compose to the real
   edges, so nothing important gets cut later.
4. **Shoot both orientations when you aren't sure.** It costs one extra frame.

In camera, or crop later? → [[Crop-and-Straighten]]

| | Set the ratio in camera | Shoot the full frame and crop later |
|---|---|---|
| You see the final edges while shooting | Yes | No, you have to imagine them |
| Pixels | All of them (on the L10, even extra width) | Fewer, sometimes far fewer |
| Flexibility later | Less | More |
| Best when | You know where the photo is going | You need several shapes from one photo |

If you need both 16:9 and 9:16 from one moment (as A5.6 asks for video), leave room.
Shoot a little wider and keep the subject near the centre, where both shapes overlap.

Prints have their own shape. A4 and A3 paper are about 1.41 times as long as they are
wide, between 4:3 and 3:2, so your photo will be trimmed or get a white border.
→ [[Printing]]

## On your kit

- **Lumix L10:** 4:3 is the base shape of the Four Thirds sensor; 3:2 and 16:9 are
  wider frames, not crops (see the table above). Check the manual for the other ratios
  it offers. If you shoot RAW, open one test file in your editor to see whether the
  ratio you chose is fixed or can be undone.
- **iPhone 15 Pro Max:** unless a camera is built like the L10, other ratios are crops
  of the sensor's shape. Compare the pixel sizes of two test frames to see what yours
  does.
- **DJI Pocket 3:** stills come only as 1:1 (3072 × 3072) or 16:9 (3840 × 2160); there
  is no 3:2 or 4:3. Rotate the screen to switch between horizontal and vertical
  framing. For a 4:5 post, shoot 1:1 and trim the sides, or shoot vertically and trim
  the top and bottom.
- **DJI Osmo Action 5 Pro:** its 155° view packs a lot into every edge. Check all four
  edges before you shoot, and use Standard (Dewarp) for people and buildings, so
  straight lines stay straight.

## Common mistakes

- **Shooting everything horizontally.** Cameras are built that way, so it's a habit,
  not a decision. Ask which way the subject runs, every time.
- **Planning to crop a horizontal 3:2 frame to 9:16 later.** A full-height 9:16 crop
  keeps less than 40% of the width. Turn the camera instead.
- **Leaving the ratio on a default you never chose.** Pick one for each shoot, on
  purpose.
- **Composing tight to the edges when you'll need a second shape.** Leave room around
  the subject, or shoot a second frame for the second shape.
- **Forgetting the print.** A 16:9 frame on A4 paper loses a lot to trimming, or gains
  wide white borders.

## Try it

Fifteen minutes, mostly on the L10. One scene; don't move or zoom.

1. Shoot it at 4:3, 3:2 and 16:9. Compare what each frame includes at the sides and at
   the top and bottom.
2. Shoot the same subject horizontally, then vertically. Which shape follows the
   subject's main line?
3. On the Pocket 3, make one 1:1 frame and one vertical 16:9 frame of the same thing.
4. Write one line on which shape suits the subject, and why.

Count these as frames for **A3.1**: a new shape is a genuinely different frame.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- On the L10, you switch from 4:3 to 16:9 without moving or zooming. What do you gain and lose?
> You gain width, 5664 px against 5200, so more of the scene at the sides. You lose a
> little height. Each ratio is its own rectangle inside the lens's circle of light, so
> 16:9 is a different frame, not a crop.

> [!question]- Why does a square frame often suit a centred, symmetric subject?
> A square has no long side to pull the eye one way. Every edge is the same distance
> from the centre, so the centre becomes a natural place for the eye to rest.

> [!question]- A tall tree in a horizontal frame looks small and lost. Why, and what do you change?
> The frame's height limits how big the tree can be, and the width fills with whatever
> stands beside it. Turn the camera vertical: the tree can fill the height, and the
> clutter at the sides goes.

> [!question]- You need a 4:5 post from the Pocket 3, which can't shoot 4:5. What do you do?
> Shoot 1:1 and trim the sides, or shoot vertically in 16:9 and trim the top and
> bottom. Compose with that crop in mind, so nothing important sits where you'll cut.
