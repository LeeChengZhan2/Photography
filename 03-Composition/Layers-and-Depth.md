# Layers and Depth

> [!summary] In one line
> A photo is flat, but you can make it feel deep by building it in layers: something
> near, something in the middle, something far. Each layer must be clearly separate,
> and each must add something.

## What it is

A layered photo has three planes, at three distances from the camera:

| Layer | Usually holds | Example at a hawker centre |
|---|---|---|
| Foreground | Something close to you, often partly cut off or out of focus | The edge of a table and a kopi cup, bottom left |
| Midground | Often the subject | A hawker at the wok, lit by the stall's lights |
| Background | The setting | The queue, the signboards, the ceiling fans, softer and dimmer |

A **near–far** composition is the simplest version: one big thing close to the camera,
one small thing far away, and the space between them implied.

Without layers, everything sits at about the same distance and the photo reads like a
wall. With them, the viewer feels they could walk into the scene.

## How it works

### How the eye judges depth in a flat picture

With one eye closed, or in a photo, you can still judge depth from **depth cues**.
Painters have used them for centuries. A photo feels deep when several of them agree:

| Cue | What happens | Why it reads as depth |
|---|---|---|
| Overlap | A nearer thing covers part of a farther one | Only something in front can block something behind |
| Relative size | Things of the same size look smaller further away | Image size shrinks with distance |
| Height in the frame | Farther things on the ground sit higher, closer to the horizon | That's how the ground looks as it goes away from you |
| Haze | Far things look paler, lower in contrast, often bluer | There's more air, moisture and haze between you and them |
| Focus | One plane sharp, the others blurred | Our eyes do the same when they focus |
| Light | A lit layer against a shaded one | Changes in light mark changes in distance |
| Converging lines | Parallel lines meet in the distance | → [[Leading-Lines]] |

Singapore's humidity makes haze easy to see. Rows of blocks fade layer by layer, each
paler than the one in front.

### Layers need separation

Layers only work if the eye can tell them apart. If the subject's dark hair sits in
front of a dark doorway, the two merge into one shape and the depth collapses. Separate
layers with:

- **Tone:** a dark layer against a light one, or the reverse.
- **Light:** the subject in light, the layer behind in shade.
- **Focus:** a soft foreground or background against a sharp subject.
- **Size and overlap:** a big near shape partly covering a smaller far one.
- **Space:** small gaps between shapes, so they don't touch.

Watch for **tangents**: places where edges in different layers just touch, like a lamp
post that seems to grow out of someone's head. They glue the layers together and flatten
the picture.

### Focal length changes how the layers stack

A wide lens close to the foreground makes it huge and the background small and far, so
the layers spread out. A long lens from far away draws them at similar sizes, so they
stack up tightly. Both work. → [[Focal-Length-and-Perspective]]

### Layered street photos

Street photographers use layers to show several things happening at once. Alex Webb's
colour frames often hold several people at different distances, each in a separate
patch of light or shade. Saul Leiter often shot through glass, rain or a gap between
objects, so a soft foreground shape frames a small figure beyond.
→ [[Photographers-to-Study]]

## How to use it

1. **Find the background first:** the setting that tells the viewer where they are.
2. **Place the subject in the midground**, separated from the background by tone, light
   or focus.
3. **Find a foreground:** a railing, leaves, a doorframe, a passer-by's shoulder, a
   reflection. Get close to it, so it's clearly nearer than the subject.
4. **Test each layer.** Cover it with your thumb on the screen. If the photo loses
   nothing, the layer isn't working.
5. **Choose the focus.** A sharp subject with a soft foreground is the usual choice. To
   keep everything sharp on the L10, use f/5.6–f/8 and focus with care, ideally using
   hyperfocal focusing. → [[Hyperfocal-Distance]]
6. **Check the overlaps:** no tangents, no merging tones, no foreground covering the
   subject's face.

## On your kit

Depth of field decides how much separation can come from focus.
→ [[Depth-of-Field]]

- **Lumix L10:** the only device where focus can separate layers much. At f/1.7–2.8,
  its Four Thirds sensor blurs about as much as f/3.7–6.2 would on full frame: a
  moderate amount. A close foreground blurs well; a distant background blurs less.
  Diffraction starts to show from about f/5.6–f/8, so don't stop down to f/16 to get
  every layer sharp.
- **iPhone, Pocket 3, Action 5 Pro:** fixed apertures and smaller sensors keep almost
  everything sharp. Separate the layers with light, tone, overlap and distance instead
  of blur.
- **Close foregrounds:** the Pocket 3 focuses from 20 cm. The Action 5 Pro's fixed focus
  is sharp only from about 35 cm, so a nearer foreground will be soft, which can work
  if you want it soft.
- **Stacked layers:** the iPhone's 120 mm, from far back, packs layers of signs and
  people tightly together.

## Common mistakes

- **A foreground that adds nothing.** A random blur at the edge is just a smudge. The
  foreground should frame, explain or echo the subject.
- **Merging layers.** The subject's tone matches what's behind them. Move until a
  lighter or darker area sits behind the subject.
- **Tangents.** Edges that just touch flatten the depth. Move a little, so shapes
  either overlap clearly or have a gap between them.
- **Everything at one distance.** If all the interest is equally far away, the photo is
  flat. Find something near.
- **Stopping down to f/16 on the L10.** Diffraction softens everything. Use f/5.6–f/8,
  or let one layer go soft on purpose.

## Try it

Thirty minutes. A small version of **A3.5**:

1. Pick one background (a row of blocks, a street) and one subject in the midground (a
   person at a bus stop, a parked bicycle).
2. Try five different foregrounds: a railing, leaves, a reflection in glass, the edge
   of a doorway, a passer-by.
3. For each frame, cover the foreground with your thumb on the screen. Keep only the
   frames that lose something when you do.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Name three depth cues you can use without changing the aperture.
> Overlap (put something in front of something else), relative size (get close to the
> foreground), and light or tone (a lit layer against a shaded one). Haze and
> converging lines also work.

> [!question]- Your subject's dark hair merges into a dark doorway. Which cue failed, and how do you fix it?
> Tonal separation. Move yourself or the subject until something lighter sits behind
> the head, or wait for light to fall on the subject.

> [!question]- Why do distant blocks of flats look paler than near ones on a humid day?
> There's more moist, hazy air between you and the far blocks. That air scatters light
> into your line of sight, which lifts their shadows and lowers their contrast.

> [!question]- On the Pocket 3, everything is sharp. How do you separate the layers?
> With light, tone, overlap and distance: put a dark foreground against a lit subject,
> let shapes overlap clearly, and get close to the foreground so it's much bigger.
