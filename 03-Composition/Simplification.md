# Simplification

> [!summary] In one line
> A painter starts with an empty canvas and adds; a photographer starts with the whole
> world and has to take things away. Simplification is the habit of removing everything
> that doesn't serve the intent, before you press the shutter.

## What it is

Simplification is subtraction. You decide what the photo is about, then remove every
element that doesn't help it, using your feet, your angle, your lens and your patience.

The main tools:

| Tool | What it removes | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Move closer | Everything around the subject leaves the frame | Faces distort if you get very close with a wide lens |
| Change the angle | Swaps a busy background for sky, ground or a plain wall | Tilting makes vertical lines lean |
| Longer focal length | Shows a narrower slice of background behind the subject | You need room to step back |
| Wider aperture | Blurs the background into soft tone and colour | Limited on your kit; blur doesn't remove bright spots |
| Wait | People, cars and buses leave; the light changes | The moment may go too |
| Exposure | Lets shadows fall to black, hiding what's in them | Lost shadow detail, on purpose |
| Crop later | Trims the edges | Fewer pixels; can't remove what overlaps the subject |

## How it works

### Attention is limited

Your eyes can only look at one small spot at a time, and they jump to a new spot about
three times a second. Every element in the frame competes for those jumps. Each extra
element is another question for the viewer: "What's that? Does it matter?" Elements
that don't help the intent are noise, and they use up the viewer's attention.

Removing them does two things at once. The noise goes, and the subject gains weight,
because it has less competition. → [[Visual-Weight-and-Balance]]

### Why you don't see the clutter while shooting

When you look at a subject, your attention narrows onto it. Your brain stops noticing
the pole behind the head or the bin at the edge. Later the viewer sees the whole frame
at once, with fresh eyes, and the clutter is obvious. That's why simplifying has to be
a deliberate check, not a feeling.

### Where clutter hides

- **The edges:** half-objects poking in, bright patches, cut-off limbs.
- **The background:** poles, signs, wires and other people behind the subject.
- **The gaps:** small bright shapes between the main shapes.

### When to stop

Subtraction has a limit. Remove the stall's signboard and the photo may lose where it
is. Remove the hands and it may lose what the person is doing. **A3.4** says to keep
removing until it breaks: the point where the photo stops saying what you meant. Then
go back one step.

## How to use it

Before you press:

1. **Say the intent in one sentence.** → [[Subject-and-Intent]]
2. **List what's in the frame:** everything, not just the subject.
3. **Ask of each element: does it help?** If not, pick the tool that removes it.
4. **Patrol the edges.** Run your eye around all four sides of the frame.
5. **Check behind the subject's head.** Then shoot.

A worked example: a busy photo of a kopi uncle at a hawker centre. The intent is *his
quiet focus while he makes kopi*.

| In the frame | Helps? | Removed by |
|---|---|---|
| The uncle and his hands | Yes, he's the subject | — |
| The kopi sock (the cloth filter), the pot, the steam | Yes, they say *kopi* | — |
| Three customers waiting | No, they say *busy* | Waiting for a quiet moment |
| A bright drinks fridge on the right | No, it's the brightest thing | Stepping to the left |
| Fluorescent tubes across the top | No | Stepping back and zooming to 75 mm |
| A bin at the bottom edge | No | Moving closer |

Then push one step further: crop to just his hands and the pot. Does it still say
*his* focus, or only *making coffee*? If it lost the person, it broke. Go back.

## On your kit

- **Lumix L10:** the only device with an aperture you can open: f/1.7 at 24 mm, f/2.8
  at 75 mm. On Four Thirds that blurs a background about as much as f/3.7–6.2 would
  on full frame, so blur helps but won't erase a busy background. Use 75 mm from
  further back as well. For small details, AF Macro lets you fill the frame from 3 cm
  at 24 mm.
- **iPhone 15 Pro Max:** the 120 mm (5×) lens is your strongest simplifier, with the
  narrowest slice of background you own. Beyond 5× is digital zoom, which is just a
  crop.
- **Pocket 3 and Action 5 Pro:** fixed wide lenses and deep focus. You simplify only
  with distance, angle, waiting and exposure. On the Action 5 Pro, switch from Ultra
  Wide to a narrower field-of-view setting when the frame fills with clutter.

## Common mistakes

- **Adding things "for interest".** More elements usually mean less interest in each.
  Add only what serves the intent.
- **Looking only at the subject.** The clutter lives at the edges and behind. Patrol
  the edges every time.
- **Using blur to hide everything.** Blur removes detail, not brightness or colour. A
  blurred red sign is still a red blob that pulls the eye.
- **Simplifying past the story.** Remove the context the intent needs and the photo
  breaks. Go back one step.
- **"I'll fix it in the edit."** Removing a pole in software is slow and often shows.
  One step sideways is quicker. → [[Retouching]]

## Try it

Thirty minutes. A small version of **A3.4**:

1. Pick one busy photo you've already taken, somewhere you can go back to.
2. Write its intent in one sentence.
3. Go back and reshoot it, removing one element per frame: a step sideways, a lower
   angle, a longer focal length, a wait.
4. Keep going until the photo stops saying your sentence. Note the last frame that
   still worked.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- A bright red bin sits behind your subject. Name three ways to remove it without editing.
> Step sideways so the subject hides it or it leaves the frame. Change your height so
> something else is behind the subject. Use a longer focal length from further back,
> so a narrower slice of background shows. Opening the aperture helps least: it stays
> a red blob.

> [!question]- Why doesn't a blurred background always fix clutter?
> Blur removes detail, not tone or colour. Bright or saturated shapes stay bright and
> saturated, so they still pull the eye.

> [!question]- Why do you miss clutter at the edges while shooting, but see it at once afterwards?
> While shooting, your attention narrows onto the subject and your brain ignores the
> rest. Afterwards you see the whole frame at once, with fresh eyes.

> [!question]- You removed the stall's signboard, and now the photo could be anywhere. What happened?
> You subtracted something the intent needed: the sense of place. That's where it
> broke. Go back one step and keep the sign, perhaps smaller or at the edge.
