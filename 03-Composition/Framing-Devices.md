# Framing Devices

> [!summary] In one line
> A framing device is something in the scene, like a doorway, a window or a branch,
> that makes a frame inside your frame. It points the eye at the subject and adds
> depth, but only while it stays quieter than the subject.

## What it is

A frame within the frame is any shape in the scene that surrounds, or partly
surrounds, your subject. Common ones:

| Frame | Example |
|---|---|
| Doorways and gates | A kitchen seen through an open door; a round moon gate in a Chinese-style garden |
| Windows | A face behind a bus window; a hawker inside the stall's serving hatch |
| Arches and colonnades | The arches of a shophouse five-foot way, with people walking through |
| Gaps | The space between two blocks of flats, with the sunrise in it |
| Branches and leaves | Overhanging branches in a park, around a jogger on the path |
| Light and shadow | A shaft of light in a dark space; shadow all round a lit patch |
| People | Two shoulders in the foreground, with a third person between them |

A frame doesn't need four sides. Branches across the top, or one wall at the side, can
be enough.

## How it works

### It points inward

A frame is a set of strong edges around an opening. Edges guide the eye, and here they
all guide it the same way: in. The opening is also usually different from the frame,
brighter or more colourful, so the eye has a second reason to go there. Together they
give a clear instruction: look *here*. → [[Visual-Weight-and-Balance]]

### It adds depth

The frame is usually closer to you than the subject. That gives you a foreground and a
background, plus overlap, one of the strongest depth cues. → [[Layers-and-Depth]]

### It adds meaning

The frame itself says something. A window says *inside and outside*: looking in, or
looking out. A doorway says *threshold*: someone about to enter or leave. A narrow gap
between towers says *squeezed in*. Pick a frame whose meaning fits your intent.

### It hides the mess

A dark frame covers the parts of the scene you don't want. The sides of a doorway can
hide the parked cars, bins and signs that would otherwise surround your subject.

### When a frame becomes clutter

A frame is a supporting actor. It fails when it competes with the subject:

- **It's brighter, sharper or more colourful than the subject.** It wins the weight.
- **It's busy.** Detailed leaves or a patterned grille pull the eye around the edges.
- **It touches or cuts the subject.** A head touching the top of a doorway feels
  cramped, and the two shapes merge.
- **It's crooked.** A doorframe slightly tilted against the photo's edges looks like
  an error.
- **It has nothing to do with the intent.** A frame added because "frames are good" is
  just one more element.

## How to use it

1. **Find the frame first, then the subject.** Like Henri Cartier-Bresson, set up a
   strong frame and wait for someone to walk into it. → [[Photographers-to-Study]]
2. **Expose for the opening, not the frame.** Let the frame go darker; it usually is,
   because it's in shade. If the camera brightens everything to suit the dark frame,
   use spot metering or negative exposure compensation. → [[Metering-Modes]]
3. **Focus on the subject.** On the L10 at a wide aperture, a near frame can go soft,
   which keeps it quiet. The other devices will usually keep it sharp, so there it
   needs to be plain or dark.
4. **Line it up.** Stand square to a doorway or window, so its edges run parallel to
   the photo's edges. Or angle it clearly, on purpose.
5. **Leave a gap** between the subject and the edges of the opening.
6. **Use a partial frame** when a full one would be too heavy.

## Common mistakes

- **The frame steals the show.** Make it darker, simpler or softer than the subject.
- **The subject touches the frame.** Move, or wait, until there's clear space around
  the subject inside the opening.
- **Crooked frame edges.** Square up to the doorway, or tilt clearly.
- **Exposing for the frame.** The bright opening blows out to white. Expose for the
  subject and let the frame go dark.
- **Forcing a frame where none fits.** Not every subject needs one. If the frame says
  nothing about the intent, leave it out.

## Try it

Twenty minutes along a row of shophouses or at a void deck. The frame often becomes
your foreground layer, so this is good practice for **A3.5**.

1. Find three frames: an arch or doorway, a gap between pillars, and branches or a
   window.
2. Shoot each one empty, exposing for the opening.
3. Wait for a person to walk into each opening, and shoot again.
4. Compare the empty and the occupied versions. Which frame added meaning, and which
   only added edges?

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why does a dark doorway around a sunlit courtyard pull the eye to the courtyard?
> The doorway's edges all point inward, and the courtyard is much brighter than the
> frame. Both the lines and the brightness send the eye to the opening.

> [!question]- You frame a jogger with branches but keep looking at the leaves. What went wrong, and what can you change?
> The leaves are busy and bright, so they carry more weight than the jogger. Choose
> darker, simpler branches, move so the leaves fall into shade, or let them blur on the
> L10 at a wide aperture.

> [!question]- In a photo through a hawker's serving hatch, the cook's head touches the top edge. Why does it feel wrong, and what's the fix?
> The two shapes merge and the cook looks squeezed. Lower your viewpoint or step back
> until there's a gap, or wait until the cook moves.

> [!question]- Your camera exposes a dark window frame well but blows out the bright street outside. Why?
> The large dark frame pulls the meter's average down, so the camera brightens the
> whole photo. Meter the bright opening instead, or use negative exposure compensation.
