# Visual Weight and Balance

> [!summary] In one line
> Some things in a frame pull the eye harder than others; that pull is their visual
> weight. Make sure the heaviest thing is your subject, then decide whether to balance
> the frame or let it tip on purpose.

## What it is

**Visual weight** is how strongly an element attracts the eye. **Balance** is how that
weight is spread across the frame.

| Carries weight | Why |
|---|---|
| Faces and eyes | We are built to find faces and read them first |
| Text | Reading is automatic: you can't see a word without reading it |
| Brightness | The brightest area wins, especially against a dark background |
| Contrast | A sharp edge between light and dark |
| Saturated colour | A strong colour among muted ones, especially red, orange or yellow |
| Size | Bigger things weigh more, all else being equal |
| Sharpness | In-focus areas beat blurred ones |
| Isolation | One thing alone in empty space weighs more than one in a crowd |

These add up. A small, bright, sharp, isolated face can outweigh a huge dark wall.

## How it works

### Where eyes go first

In the 1960s the psychologist Alfred Yarbus recorded where people's eyes went as they
looked at pictures. Their eyes returned again and again to faces, especially the eyes
and mouth, and skipped plain areas. Later eye-tracking studies agree: viewers go first
to faces, text and strong contrast. These are what the brain treats as information. A
plain wall tells you little; a face tells you who someone is, how they feel and where
they're looking.

Your visual system also responds to *differences* more than to absolute values. A
bright patch matters because it's brighter than what surrounds it. A red shirt matters
because the rest of the scene is grey. That's why the same object can be heavy in one
photo and almost invisible in another.

### Figure and ground

Before the eye can weigh the subject, it has to find it. The subject is the **figure**
and what lies behind it is the **ground**. The figure stands out only where it differs
from the ground right behind it, by at least one of:

- **Tone:** light against dark, or dark against light.
- **Colour:** warm against cool, or vivid against muted.
- **Focus:** a sharp subject against a blurred background.
- **Edge:** a clean outline that nothing behind it touches or crosses.

For example, a hawker in a white singlet in front of white wall tiles almost
disappears. Step sideways so the dark back of the stall is behind him, and he stands
out at once. Check the outline of the head first: that's where merging shows.
→ [[Layers-and-Depth]]

### The seesaw

Think of the frame as a seesaw balanced on its centre. Weight further from the centre
has more leverage. So:

- **Two equal weights, equally far from the centre,** balance. That's **symmetric
  balance**: calm and formal. → [[Symmetry-and-Centring]]
- **A heavy element near the centre and a light one far out** can balance too. That's
  **asymmetric balance**. It feels more lively and natural, because the eye travels
  between the two.
- **All the weight on one side, with nothing to counter it,** is **imbalance**. The
  frame feels as if it's tipping. That tension can be the point.

A worked example: a street at dusk. A large dark block of flats fills the left half,
close to the centre. A small figure stands under a lit lamp at the far right third. The
figure is small, but it is bright, isolated, human and far from the centre. That's
enough to balance the whole building.

### Tension as a choice

Imbalance makes viewers uneasy, and unease can serve the intent. A person squeezed
against the edge of the frame, beside a huge empty wall, can say *trapped* or *alone*.
Choose it; don't fall into it.

## How to use it

1. **Find the heaviest thing before you press.** Squint, or glance at the screen and
   notice where your eye lands first. Is it your subject?
2. **If something else wins, weaken it or remove it:** move so it's hidden, wait for it
   to go, put it out of focus, or exclude it with a tighter frame.
3. **Check the edges.** A bright spot or a word near the edge pulls the eye out of the
   frame.
4. **Decide the balance.** For a calm subject, balance it, symmetrically or
   asymmetrically. For an uneasy one, let it tip.
5. **Adjust in the edit.** Darkening the edges and brightening the subject moves the
   weight where you want it. → [[Dodge-and-Burn]]

Common weight thieves: a white car, a bright gap of sky through leaves, a red sign, a
logo on a T-shirt, a second face looking at the camera.

## Common mistakes

- **Something brighter or redder than your subject.** It wins, and the viewer looks at
  it. Remove it, hide it, or make your subject the brightest thing.
- **Words in the background.** Signs and T-shirts get read whether you want it or not.
  Move so the text is hidden or cut off.
- **Bright spots at the edges.** They lead the eye out. Recompose, or darken them
  later.
- **Two equal centres of attention.** Two faces of the same size and brightness on
  opposite sides split the photo in two. Let one win.
- **Balancing everything, always.** If every frame is calm and settled, the set becomes
  dull. Use tension when the story is tense.

## Try it

Fifteen minutes at a table, any device. A warm-up for **A3.7**, where the squint test
shows you weight without the detail.

1. Put a large dark object (a bag) near the centre and a small bright one (a lemon, a
   red cup) beside it. Shoot.
2. Move the small object step by step towards the far edge, shooting each time. Where
   does the frame feel balanced?
3. Add a newspaper or a box with large print. Where does your eye go now?

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Your friend wears grey and stands in front of a bright orange sign. Where does the eye go, and what are two fixes?
> To the sign: it's brighter, more saturated and has text. Move so a plain area sits
> behind your friend, or get closer with a longer focal length so the sign leaves the
> frame. Blurring it helps less, because the orange stays.

> [!question]- How can a tiny figure balance a huge building?
> The figure gets weight from being human, lit, isolated and far from the centre. On
> the seesaw, weight far from the centre has more leverage, so a small heavy element
> near the edge can balance a big element near the middle.

> [!question]- What's the difference between asymmetric balance and imbalance?
> In asymmetric balance, unequal elements still counter each other, so the frame feels
> settled. In imbalance, nothing counters the weight, so the frame feels as if it's
> tipping. Both are valid if you choose them.

> [!question]- Why can a small bright patch in a corner spoil a photo?
> Brightness attracts the eye, and at the edge it pulls the eye out of the frame, away
> from the subject. A small patch can still carry a lot of visual weight.
