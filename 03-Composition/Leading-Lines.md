# Leading Lines

> [!summary] In one line
> Lines in a scene carry the viewer's eye along them. Use a road, a railing or a shadow
> to lead the eye to your subject, and watch for lines that lead it out of the frame
> instead.

## What it is

A leading line is any line in the photo that guides the eye from one part of the frame
to another, usually towards the subject.

| Kind | Examples |
|---|---|
| Real lines | Roads, kerbs, railway tracks, railings, walkways, fences, a river bank, a row of lamp posts |
| Lines of light and shadow | The edge of a shadow, a shaft of sunlight, a stripe of reflected light |
| Implied lines | The direction someone looks, a pointing hand, the path of something moving, a row of separate objects |

A line can be straight, curved or zig-zag, and each shape feels different.

## How it works

### The eye follows edges

Your eyes don't glide smoothly across a picture. They jump from point to point, and a
continuous edge suggests where to jump next. Psychologists call our habit of seeing a
line as carrying on in the same direction **good continuation**. So a strong line
becomes a path, and the eye tends to follow it to its end. Whatever sits at the end of
the line gets attention, because the eye arrives there expecting something.

Eye-tracking studies add a caution: the eye doesn't trace the line itself, and lines
steer attention best when a clear subject waits at the end.

### Converging lines make depth

Parallel lines in the world, like rails or the two edges of a road, seem to come
together in the distance. The point where they meet is the **vanishing point**. Your
brain reads converging lines as space going away from you, so a frame with converging
lines feels deep, even though it's flat.

The effect grows when you're close to the near end of the lines. Get low beside a
railing with a wide lens and the near part looks huge, while the far part shrinks fast.
→ [[Focal-Length-and-Perspective]]

### Lines can lead out, too

The eye follows a line in both directions. A road running from your subject to a corner
of the frame carries the eye out of the photo. Lines that enter from an edge are fine;
lines that end at an edge are exits.

### Implied lines are just as strong

People follow other people's gaze without thinking. If someone in the photo looks at
something, the viewer looks there too. A pointing hand, a car moving in one direction,
or three people in a row work the same way: the brain joins the dots into a line.

### Shape changes the feeling

- **Straight lines** are direct and fast.
- **Curves**, like an S-shaped path or a river, are slow and calm. The eye wanders
  along them.
- **Zig-zags** are restless and energetic. → [[Diagonals-and-Dynamism]]

## How to use it

1. **Find the line, then find what's at the end of it.** If nothing is there, wait for
   someone to walk into place, or move until the line points at your subject.
2. **Start the line near you, at an edge or a corner** (usually the bottom), and end
   it at the subject. The eye enters where the line enters.
3. **Get low and close to the line with a wide lens** for drama: 24 mm on the L10 or
   13 mm on the iPhone. Stand back with a long lens for a flatter, more graphic line.
4. **Block the exits.** If a line runs out of the frame, move so it ends on the
   subject, crop so it stops short of the edge, or let a darker area close it off.
5. **Use several lines that agree.** Railings, a roof edge and a kerb that all meet at
   one person make a very strong frame.
6. **Leave space in the direction of an implied line:** in front of a face, ahead of
   someone walking.

Places to look in Singapore: covered walkways, park connector paths, HDB corridor
railings, the stripes of a zebra crossing, the long shadows of railings in the first
and last hours of daylight, the painted lines of an empty car park roof.

## Common mistakes

- **A line that leads nowhere.** The eye arrives at the end and finds nothing. Put
  something there, or wait for something to arrive.
- **A line that leads out of the frame.** Move or crop so it ends on the subject.
- **Lines going in every direction.** Strong lines that don't agree make the eye bounce
  around. Find a viewpoint where they point the same way.
- **The subject off the line.** A great road, with the person standing beside it rather
  than on it or at its end. Move them, or move yourself.
- **Forgetting implied lines.** A person at the edge looking out of the frame is a line
  pointing out. Turn them, or give space on the side they face.

## Try it

Twenty minutes along a covered walkway, a park connector or a long corridor:

1. Make five frames where a line starts at the bottom edge or a corner and ends at a
   subject: a person, a bench, a lamp post.
2. Make one where the line runs out of the frame. Compare where your eye ends up.
3. Make one with an implied line: a person looking at something inside the frame.

Good material for **A3.1**: one line seen from five heights is five different frames.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why do converging lines make a flat photo feel deep?
> In real life, parallel lines look closer together the further away they are. The
> brain uses that as a clue to distance, so converging lines read as space going away
> from you.

> [!question]- A road runs from the bottom-left corner out through the top-right corner, and your subject stands beside it halfway up. What happens?
> The eye follows the road past the subject and out of the frame. Move so the road ends
> at the subject, put the subject on the road, or crop so the road stops before the
> edge.

> [!question]- A woman on the left of the frame looks at a food stall on the right. What line is in the photo?
> An implied line, from her eyes to the stall. Viewers follow her gaze, so the line
> connects the two halves of the frame.

> [!question]- Why does getting low and close to a railing with a wide lens make the line more dramatic?
> The near part of the railing is very close, so it's drawn very big; the far part is
> drawn small. That big change in size along the line makes it converge strongly.
