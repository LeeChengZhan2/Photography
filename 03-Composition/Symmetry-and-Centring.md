# Symmetry and Centring

> [!summary] In one line
> Putting the subject in the middle is right when the scene is symmetric, formal or
> facing you directly. It makes a calm, strong frame, but only if it's exact: nearly
> centred looks like a mistake.

## What it is

**Centring** means placing the main subject in the middle of the frame. **Symmetry**
means one half of the frame mirrors the other. The common kinds:

| Kind | What mirrors what | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Left–right | The left half mirrors the right | A temple front, a corridor seen along its length, a face looking at you |
| Top–bottom | The top mirrors the bottom | A reflection in still water or a rain puddle |
| Radial | Shapes repeat around a centre | Looking straight up a spiral staircase, a ceiling fan, a flower |

Centring and symmetry often go together, but not always. A single person alone in a
big empty space can be centred with nothing to mirror.

## How it works

### The eye finds symmetry fast

People are very sensitive to symmetry. Faces, bodies, and many animals and plants are
roughly symmetric, so the visual system spots mirror patterns quickly and reads them as
order. A symmetric frame feels calm, stable, formal and deliberate. Nothing pulls the
eye to one side, so it settles on the centre line.

### When the centre is the right answer

- **Symmetric subjects.** The centre line is the structure of the photo. Placing it
  off-centre cuts the symmetry and leaves one half heavier.
- **Formality and stillness.** A building front, a ceremony, a quiet interior.
- **Direct portraits.** A person square to the camera, looking into the lens. Centring
  holds the viewer in their gaze, with nowhere else to go. Look at Richard Avedon's
  portraits on plain white backgrounds: frontal and simple, with nothing else to look
  at. → [[Portraiture]]
- **Reflections.** The water line goes through the middle, so the scene and its
  reflection get equal space.
- **Long corridors, roads and tunnels.** Their lines all meet at one point, the
  **vanishing point**. Put it in the centre and every line in the frame pulls the eye
  to the middle and into the distance.
- **Isolation.** A small subject centred in empty space, with nothing competing.

### Why it demands precision

Because the eye detects symmetry so easily, it also detects small errors. A horizon one
or two degrees off, or a vanishing point slightly left of centre, doesn't look like a
choice. It looks like you tried and missed. In a loose street photo nobody notices a
small error; in a symmetric frame, everyone does.

### Breaking it on purpose

Perfect symmetry sets up an expectation. One thing that breaks it becomes the subject:
a single person in one doorway of a symmetric corridor, one lit window in a symmetric
building front, one red umbrella among grey ones. The symmetry makes the difference
impossible to miss. → [[Pattern-Repetition-and-the-Break]]

The rule: be exact, or be clearly off. Nearly symmetric is the one version that doesn't
work.

## How to use it

Step by step, for an exact symmetric frame:

1. **Find the axis:** the centre line of the corridor, the doorway or the building.
2. **Stand on it.** Move left or right until both sides look the same width. Use the
   grid display if your camera has one.
3. **Keep the camera level.** Don't tilt it up or down, or vertical lines lean.
4. **Keep it straight.** Don't rotate it, so the horizon stays parallel to the top
   edge of the frame.
5. **Check the edges.** Both sides should end at the same point. A bin on one side
   breaks the mirror.
6. **Fix small errors in the edit**: straighten, and correct perspective.
   → [[Perspective-Correction]]

Places to find symmetry in Singapore:

- An HDB corridor seen straight along its length: doors and windows repeat on both
  sides and meet at the far end.
- A void deck with a row of pillars, seen down the middle.
- A covered walkway or an overhead bridge, seen end to end.
- A temple, church or mosque front, seen from the centre of the path in front of it.
- Puddles after an afternoon storm, reflecting a block of flats.

## Common mistakes

- **Nearly symmetric.** A frame that's 95% symmetric looks like an error. Move until
  it's exact, or move clearly off-centre.
- **Tilting the camera.** Even a slight tilt makes verticals lean and breaks the
  mirror. Keep it level.
- **Centring everything.** Centre when the subject is symmetric, formal or facing you.
  If it moves or looks sideways, it usually needs space in front of it.
- **Ignoring the edges.** One side ends with a wall, the other with a parked car. Move
  until both edges match, or crop.
- **Breaking symmetry by accident.** If something breaks it, make sure it's the thing
  you want people to look at.

## Try it

Twenty minutes in an HDB corridor, a void deck or a covered walkway. Don't point the
camera into people's homes.

1. Make one exactly symmetric frame: stand on the centre line, keep the camera level
   and check both edges.
2. Make a second from half a metre off the centre line. Does it look like a choice or a
   mistake?
3. Wait for a person to step into one side, and make a third frame where they are the
   break.

Use the best one as one of your **A3.1** frames.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why does a horizon two degrees off look worse in a symmetric frame than in a loose street photo?
> Symmetry makes the viewer expect order, and the eye is very good at spotting small
> breaks in a mirror pattern. A loose frame sets up no such expectation, so the error
> goes unnoticed.

> [!question]- In your corridor shot, the left wall looks wider than the right. What went wrong?
> You were standing left of the centre line, or the camera was turned slightly. Step to
> the exact middle and point the camera straight along the corridor.

> [!question]- When is centring a stronger choice than placing the subject on a third?
> When the scene is symmetric or reflected, when a person looks straight into the
> lens, when lines meet at a central vanishing point, or when a small subject sits
> alone in empty space.

> [!question]- A symmetric building front has one open window with a person in it. What is the photo about, and why does the symmetry help?
> The person in the window: they are the break in the pattern. The symmetry makes every
> other window look the same, so the one difference jumps out.
