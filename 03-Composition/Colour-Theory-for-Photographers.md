# Colour Theory for Photographers

> [!summary] In one line
> Every colour has a hue, a saturation and a brightness, and colours placed together
> either calm each other or fight. Choose which colours share your frame, and how many,
> as carefully as you choose the subject.

## What it is

Any colour can be described with three properties:

| Property | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Hue** | Which colour it is | Red, orange, green, blue |
| **Saturation** | How strong or pure it is, from grey to vivid | A faded pink wall against a freshly painted red one |
| **Brightness** (luminance) | How light or dark it is | Pale sky blue against deep navy |

Your editor's HSL panel has a slider for each, for each range of colours.
→ [[HSL-and-Colour-Mixer]]

### The colour wheel

The hues are arranged in a circle, and colours opposite each other are
**complementary**. There are two wheels:

- **The painter's wheel**, with red, yellow and blue as primaries, from art class. Its
  opposite pairs are red–green, blue–orange and yellow–purple.
- **The light wheel**, with red, green and blue as primaries. Screens and cameras mix
  light, so editing software works on this one. Its opposite pairs are red–cyan,
  green–magenta and blue–yellow.

Use the light wheel when you edit, because it predicts what the sliders do. The white
balance **Temperature** slider moves between blue and yellow, and **Tint** between green
and magenta. Both are opposite pairs, which is why adding one removes the other.
→ [[White-Balance]]

## How it works

### Why opposites look strong together

Your eyes sort colour into opposing pairs: one channel signals red against green,
another blue against yellow. Colours at opposite ends of a channel produce the biggest
difference in the signal, so side by side each makes the other look stronger. This is
**simultaneous contrast**: a colour seems to shift away from the colour around it. A
grey patch on a red background even takes on a slight green tint.

### Harmonies: which colours go together

| Harmony | What it is | Feels | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Complementary | Two opposite hues | Vivid and energetic; the subject pops | Warm, lit shophouse windows against a deep blue sky at blue hour |
| Analogous | Three or so neighbours on the wheel | Calm and unified | The greens and yellow-greens of a forest trail at MacRitchie |
| Triadic | Three hues evenly spaced around the wheel | Lively and playful, but hard to control | A playground painted red, yellow and blue |
| Monochromatic | One hue in different strengths and brightnesses | Quiet, focused, moody | A blue corridor with a blue door and blue railings |

With complementary and triadic colours, let one dominate and use the others as small
accents. Equal amounts fight.

### Warm and cool

Reds, oranges and yellows are **warm**: we link them with sun and fire. Blues, greens
and cyans are **cool**: sky, water, shade. Warm colours tend to look nearer and carry
more weight; cool colours tend to sit back. A small warm patch in a cool frame pulls the
eye strongly.

The light itself has a colour. Shade under a blue sky is cool, golden hour is warm, and
indoor bulbs are often warm. Warm and cool light in the same frame gives you warm–cool
contrast for free. → [[Colour-Temperature-of-Light]]

### Kinds of colour contrast

- **Hue contrast:** different hues, strongest when they're opposites.
- **Saturation contrast:** one vivid colour among muted ones. Often the most powerful.
- **Warm–cool contrast:** a warm subject on a cool background, or the reverse.
- **Light–dark contrast:** colours of different brightness. This is the only kind that
  survives in black and white. → [[Tonal-Composition]]

### Colour as the subject

Sometimes the photo is about a colour, not an object: the pink of a shophouse wall, the
exact green of a door. The object only carries the colour. That's what **A3.6** asks
for.

### Restraint

Every saturated colour carries weight. A frame with six vivid colours has six centres
of attention and no order among them. Strong colour photos usually have one dominant
hue, one accent, and everything else muted.

## How to use it

1. **Name the colours** in the scene before you shoot. How many are there, and which is
   the loudest?
2. **Pick a harmony for the intent:** calm, analogous; energy, complementary; mood or
   focus, monochromatic.
3. **Change the background to change the colour.** The background is usually the
   biggest area of colour in the frame. One step sideways can swap a red wall for a
   green hedge.
4. **Wait for the accent:** a grey street, then a person in a yellow raincoat.
5. **Set white balance on purpose.** It shifts every colour in the frame. With RAW you
   can change it later, but decide what you want.
6. **In the edit, quiet the competition.** Lower the saturation of one distracting
   colour with HSL, rather than raising everything else. → [[Colour-Grading]]

## On your kit

Two of your devices can bake a colour look into the file as you shoot:

- **Lumix L10:** [REAL TIME LUT] applies a look to the JPEG or HEIF in camera.
- **iPhone 15 Pro Max:** Photographic Styles bake in at capture on this model and can't
  be used with ProRAW.

While you're learning colour, shoot RAW alongside the L10's JPEGs, and leave the
iPhone's style on Standard, so the colour stays your decision in the edit.

## Common mistakes

- **Too many strong colours.** Everything competes. Find an angle or a moment with
  fewer, or quieten some in the edit.
- **Pushing the Saturation slider.** It makes every colour louder, so nothing becomes
  more important. Adjust one colour at a time instead.
- **Ignoring the colour of the light.** Skin in blue shade looks cold and grey. Move
  the person into warmer light, or correct the white balance.
- **A loud colour behind the subject.** A red sign behind a face wins. Move.
- **Treating harmonies as rules.** They predict a feeling. Your intent chooses.

## Try it

Thirty minutes. A small version of **A3.6**:

1. Before you leave home, pick one colour: the red of a postbox, say, or the yellow of
   road markings.
2. Make ten frames where that colour is the subject. Keep other strong colours out.
3. Make one extra frame where your colour meets its opposite.
4. Compare. Which frames are *about* the colour, and which are just *of* red things?

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why does a warm-lit shophouse look more vivid at blue hour than at noon?
> At blue hour the sky is deep blue, the opposite of the warm orange lights. Your eyes'
> blue–yellow channel signals the biggest possible difference, so both colours look
> stronger side by side.

> [!question]- The Temperature slider moves between which two colours, and why does adding one remove the other?
> Blue and yellow. They are opposites on the light wheel, so they cancel each other:
> adding yellow pushes a blue cast back towards neutral.

> [!question]- A street photo has a red bus, a green tree, a yellow sign and a purple umbrella, all vivid. What's wrong, and what can you do?
> No colour leads, so the eye can't settle. Find a viewpoint with fewer colours, wait
> for the bus to pass, or lower the saturation of all but one in the edit.

> [!question]- How is "a colour as the subject" different from a photo of a coloured object?
> In the first, the colour is the point and the object only carries it; the frame keeps
> out competing colours and may not even show the whole object. In the second, the
> object is the point and its colour is incidental.
