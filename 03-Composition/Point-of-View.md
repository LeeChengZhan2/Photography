# Point of View

> [!summary] In one line
> Point of view is where the camera is: how high, and at what angle. Moving it up or
> down changes what the photo says about the subject and what sits behind it, and it
> costs nothing but bending your knees.

## What it is

Most photos are taken from the same place: standing, with the camera at eye level,
about 1.5–1.7 m off the ground. Choosing a **point of view** means picking another
place on purpose. It has two parts:

- **Height:** where the camera is, from the ground up.
- **Angle:** whether it looks up, straight ahead, or down.

| Viewpoint | How | What it tends to say |
|---|---|---|
| Eye level | Camera at your eye height, held level | Neutral and familiar: "this is what it looked like" |
| The subject's eye level | Camera at the height of the subject's eyes | Equal footing, closeness |
| A child's height | About 1 m or lower | The world as a child or a pet sees it: big and towering |
| Low angle | Camera at waist or knee height, tilted up | Power, size, drama; often sky behind |
| Worm's-eye | Camera on the ground, looking up or along it | Huge scale; the ground becomes a big foreground |
| High angle | From a step, a staircase or an upper floor, looking down | The subject smaller and more exposed; more context around them |
| Overhead (flat lay) | Camera pointing straight down | Graphic and map-like: shapes, patterns, arrangements |

## How it works

### We read angles the way we read people

In daily life you look **up** at things that are bigger or more powerful than you: a
tall building, a parent, a teacher. You look **down** at things that are smaller: a
child, a pet, a phone. A photo borrows those feelings. Shoot a person from below and the
viewer is placed below them, looking up. Shoot from above and the viewer stands over
them.

Shooting at the subject's eye level puts the viewer on equal terms with them. That's
why photos of children and pets usually work best from their height: the viewer meets
their eyes instead of the top of their head.

### An unfamiliar height makes people look

Viewers have seen the standing view of everything thousands of times. A view from the
ground or from above is unfamiliar, so the eye slows down and looks properly. Vivian
Maier made much of her street work with a twin-lens reflex camera held at waist height,
looking down into its viewfinder. Look at how that lower viewpoint changes her street
portraits. → [[Photographers-to-Study]]

### The background is whatever lies behind, along your line of sight

Your line of sight runs from the lens, through the subject, to whatever is behind it.
Move the camera and that line lands somewhere else:

- **Go low and look up:** it ends in the sky, the treetops or a ceiling. These are
  usually plainer than a street.
- **Go high and look down:** it ends on the ground: grass, a road, a floor. Often an
  even texture.
- **Step sideways:** it slides along the background, so a pole or a bin can move behind
  the subject or out from behind it.

This is one of the cheapest ways to clean up a frame. → [[Simplification]]

### Tilting changes the lines

Tilt the camera up at a building and the sensor is no longer parallel to its face: the
top is now further from the sensor than the base. The top is drawn smaller, so the
sides lean in. These are **converging verticals**. Keep the camera level, so the
sensor stays parallel to the face, and vertical lines stay vertical. Point it straight
down at a table and the table's edges stay parallel. → [[Perspective-Correction]]

Height and distance work together. A low angle close to a person with a wide lens makes
the legs look long and the head small, because the feet are much closer to the lens
than the head. → [[Focal-Length-and-Perspective]]

## How to use it

- **Try at least three heights before you settle:** ground, waist and eye level, plus
  above if you can get there safely. Your first frame is usually at eye level, so the
  better frame is often somewhere else.
- **Match the subject's eye level** for children, pets and seated people. Kneel or sit.
- **Go low to clean up a background**, and to add size or drama. The jumble of signs in
  a hawker centre can disappear if you shoot up at a person against the ceiling.
- **Go high to show patterns, context and arrangements:** people crossing a plaza, a
  table of food, floor tiles. → [[Food-Photography]]
- **For a flat lay, keep the camera parallel to the table**, pointing straight down.
  Even a few degrees of tilt makes the table's edges converge.
- **Decide about tilt.** Level looks calm and correct. A strong upward tilt can look
  dramatic. A slight one just looks like a mistake.
- **Stay safe and polite.** Don't climb railings or lean over ledges, and don't point
  the camera into people's homes from a corridor or a staircase.

## On your kit

All four devices make unusual heights easy, because none of them needs your eye at the
camera:

- **Lumix L10:** the 3" vari-angle screen swings out and turns. Hold the camera at
  ground level or above your head and still see the frame. Use the viewfinder at eye
  level, where holding the camera to your face keeps it steadier.
- **iPhone 15 Pro Max:** screen only. To get lower, turn the phone so the end with the
  lenses is nearest the ground. The 13 mm (0.5×) lens at ground level gives a huge
  foreground, but it has no stabilisation, so brace it in dim light.
- **DJI Pocket 3:** rotate the 2" screen to switch between horizontal and vertical
  framing. The gimbal smooths your movement when you hold it low or high, and Tilt
  Locked holds the up-and-down angle steady. FPV mode rolls with your movement, so
  avoid it when you want a level horizon.
- **DJI Osmo Action 5 Pro:** small enough to rest on the ground or a ledge. Use the
  front screen when you can't see the rear one. Its 155° view makes a low foreground
  enormous; use Standard (Dewarp) for buildings, to remove the fisheye bend.

## Common mistakes

- **Shooting everything from standing eye level.** It's the view everyone already has.
  Change your height before you change anything else.
- **Shooting children and pets from above.** The viewer sees the tops of heads. Get
  down to their eyes.
- **A slight accidental tilt.** Lines that lean by a few degrees look like an error.
  Keep the camera level, or tilt clearly on purpose.
- **Forgetting that the background moved too.** When you go low, check what's behind
  the subject now. A sky is clean; a lamp post growing out of a head isn't.
- **A flat lay that isn't flat.** If the camera isn't pointing straight down, the table
  looks as if it's sliding away.

## Try it

Twenty minutes, on any device with a screen you can see from odd angles. One subject: a
bicycle at a void deck, a cup on a table, or a friend.

1. Keep the same distance. Shoot from the ground, knee height, waist height, eye level,
   and from above (a step, a bench or a staircase).
2. For each frame, note what's behind the subject and what feeling the angle gives.
3. Pick the best one and say why in one line.

These are exactly the frames that make up the "work" part of **A3.1** (frames 16–35).

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Your portrait of a friend has a messy car park behind them. Name two viewpoint changes that fix it without moving your friend.
> Go low and shoot slightly up, so sky or trees sit behind them. Or step sideways until
> a plain wall lines up behind them. Going higher and shooting down onto grass or
> paving also works.

> [!question]- Why does a child photographed from adult standing height look small and far away?
> The camera is above the child, looking down, the way adults look at small things. The
> viewer is placed above them and sees the top of the head instead of the eyes.

> [!question]- You shoot a tall block of flats from its base, tilting up, and the sides lean in. Why?
> Tilted up, the sensor no longer faces the block square-on, so the top is much further
> from the sensor than the bottom and is drawn smaller. Step back and keep the camera
> level, tilt strongly so it looks deliberate, or correct it in the edit.

> [!question]- What does a low viewpoint do to a subject, and why?
> It makes the subject look bigger and stronger, because the viewer is placed below,
> looking up, the way we look at things larger than us. It also tends to put sky or
> ceiling behind the subject, which simplifies the background.
