# Pattern Repetition and the Break

> [!summary] In one line
> Repeated shapes, colours or lines make a pattern, and the eye enjoys its rhythm. One
> element that breaks the pattern grabs the eye at once, so the break is often the real
> subject.

## What it is

- **Pattern:** a shape, line or colour repeated across the frame. Windows on a block of
  flats, floor tiles, a row of stools.
- **Rhythm:** how the repetition flows. It can be **regular** (the same gap every
  time), **alternating** (two elements taking turns) or **progressive** (each repeat
  smaller than the last, like pillars in a row going away from you).
- **The break:** the one element that differs in colour, shape, direction or state.
  One lit window, one open door, one person in red among black umbrellas.

## How it works

### The brain predicts, then stops checking

Your visual system is built to find regularity. After a few repeats, it predicts the
rest and stops checking each one. That's why a pattern feels calm and easy to look at,
and also why it can feel monotonous.

### The odd one out pops out

An element that breaks the prediction is found almost instantly. Psychologists call
this **pop-out**: in a field of identical items, one that differs in colour, size or
direction is seen at once, however many others there are. So the break carries far
more weight than its size suggests. → [[Visual-Weight-and-Balance]]

### Filling the frame makes it endless

If the pattern runs to all four edges, the viewer imagines it carrying on beyond the
frame: endless windows, an endless crowd. If the frame shows where the pattern stops,
the pattern becomes an object with edges instead. Both are valid; decide which you
want.

### Pattern alone is decoration

A pattern with no break and no change of rhythm can look like wallpaper. Sometimes that
is the point: sameness, monotony, the scale of a city. Usually, though, a break or a
change of rhythm is what gives the pattern a story.

## How to use it

### Where to look in Singapore

- **HDB blocks:** windows, air-con units, laundry poles, corridors floor after floor.
- **Void decks:** rows of pillars, banks of letterboxes, stone tables and stools.
- **Crowds:** umbrellas in the rain, a lunchtime crowd seen from above.
- **Tiles:** kopitiam floors, Peranakan tiles on shophouse fronts, pool tiles.
- **Nature:** palm fronds, leaves, ripples on water.

### Shooting it

1. **Square up to flat patterns.** Keep the camera parallel to the wall or floor. If
   you tilt, the grid converges and the regularity is lost. → [[Perspective-Correction]]
2. **Use a longer focal length from further away** for tall buildings: 75 mm on the
   L10 or 120 mm on the iPhone, from across the road. You need less tilt, and the
   pattern flattens.
3. **Fill the frame edge to edge** if you want the pattern to feel endless. Check the
   edges for a strip of sky or a stray object.
4. **Find the break, or wait for it:** a person walking past a row of pillars, a
   window opening, someone hanging out laundry.
5. **Place the break on purpose:** on a third, or dead centre in a symmetric pattern.
   → [[Symmetry-and-Centring]]
6. **Change the rhythm with the angle.** Shoot a row of pillars along its length, and
   the regular rhythm becomes progressive, each pillar smaller than the last.
7. **Use side light for texture patterns** such as tiles, bricks and ripples. Light from
   the side throws a small shadow from every bump.

## Common mistakes

- **A pattern with no point.** It looks like wallpaper. Find a break, a change of
  rhythm, or a clear reason to show sameness.
- **Almost filling the frame.** A sliver of sky or another building at one edge pulls
  the eye. Fill the frame completely, or show the pattern's edges clearly.
- **Lines that converge on a flat building.** You tilted the camera. Step back, use a
  longer focal length and keep the camera level.
- **A break too small to see.** On a phone screen a tiny difference vanishes. Get
  closer, or wait for a bigger break.
- **Too many breaks.** Three different odd ones out aren't a break; they're noise. Find
  a stretch of pattern with only one.

## Try it

Twenty minutes at a block of flats or a void deck:

1. Fill the frame with a pattern, so no edge shows where it ends.
2. Find a break that's already there: one open window, one missing tile, one stool out
   of line.
3. Set up a pattern frame and wait for a person to become the break.

If your one subject for **A3.1** is a block of flats, these three are a strong start.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why does one red umbrella among black ones grab the eye at once?
> Your brain predicts the pattern and stops checking each umbrella. The one that
> differs in colour pops out immediately, so it carries much more weight than its size.

> [!question]- Your photo of a block of flats shows the rows of windows leaning inward. What happened, and how do you fix it?
> You tilted the camera up, so the top of the block was further away and drawn smaller.
> Step back, use a longer focal length and keep the camera level, or correct it in the
> edit.

> [!question]- A pattern fills the frame except for a thin strip of sky at the top. What does the strip do?
> It's bright and different, so it pulls the eye away from the pattern, and it shows
> where the pattern ends. Crop it out, or move so the pattern fills the frame.

> [!question]- When is a pattern without a break enough?
> When sameness is the intent: the monotony of a housing block, the scale of a crowd,
> the rhythm or texture itself. Then a break would only distract.
