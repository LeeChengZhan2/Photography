# Series vs Singles

> [!summary] In one line
> A single photograph has to say everything inside one frame. A series spreads the
> meaning across many frames, so each picture can do less and the whole can say more.

## What it is

| | Single | Series |
|---|---|---|
| Where the meaning lives | Inside the frame | Between the frames |
| What each picture needs | Subject, light, composition and moment, all at once | To add something the others don't |
| Quiet pictures | Rarely survive | Can work as connecting frames |
| Typical home | A cover, a competition, a print on the wall, a news page | A book, an exhibition, a portfolio, your capstone |
| What it shows about you | You can make a strong picture | You can see, think and choose over time |

A **series** (or *body of work*) is a group of photographs made around one subject or
idea, in a consistent way. A **typology** is a strict kind of series: one type of thing,
photographed the same way every time, so that you can compare them.

## How it works

### A single works inside the frame

A strong single holds everything at once: a clear subject, light that suits it, a
composition that leads the eye, and the right moment. It must read without help,
because it usually appears alone. → [[Subject-and-Intent]]

### A series works by comparison

When viewers see several pictures, they compare them. Three things build the meaning:

1. **Repetition sets a rule.** What stays the same tells the viewer what to ignore.
2. **Variation carries the content.** Once the rule is clear, the differences stand
   out.
3. **Accumulation makes a claim.** One photo of a void deck is a snapshot. Twenty, made
   the same way, are a study of how a city shares space.

This is the syllabus's "one variable at a time" in another form. A series is like an
experiment: hold the constants, and let one thing change.
→ [[Pattern-Repetition-and-the-Break]]

### Consistency

The constants are what make a set read as one piece:

- the subject or idea
- distance and point of view
- light and time of day
- orientation and aspect ratio
- focal length
- colour treatment and contrast: one look → [[Developing-a-Look]]

Consistent doesn't mean identical. Inside the rules, vary the scale and the pace, so the
eye has something to compare.

### Typologies: Bernd and Hilla Becher

The Bechers, a German couple, photographed industrial structures for more than four
decades, from the late 1950s: water towers, blast furnaces, winding towers, gas tanks.
Every picture followed the same rules. Black and white. A straight-on view with the
structure in the centre. Flat, overcast light with no strong shadows. No people. They
showed the prints in grids, one type of structure per grid.

Alone, one water tower is a dull record. In a grid of nine, you start to compare the
tanks, the legs, the roofs. Small differences become the subject. The strict rules are
what make that possible.

Two more to look at:

- **August Sander** photographed German people of every class and job from the 1920s,
  and arranged the portraits by type.
- **Hiroshi Sugimoto's** *Seascapes*, from 1980: the horizon cuts each frame exactly in
  half, sea after sea. → [[Photographers-to-Study]]

## How to use it

Decide early which one you're making, because it changes how you shoot.

- **For a single,** work one scene hard. Move, wait and shoot many frames until one
  holds everything.
- **For a series,** write the rules before you start: the constants, and the one thing
  that changes. Then shoot many examples and keep to the rules.

To test a picture for a series, ask three questions:

1. Does it follow the rules? If not, does breaking them add something?
2. Does it add something that no other picture in the set has?
3. Would the set be weaker without it?

Lay the set out as a grid. A picture that breaks the pattern for no reason jumps out at
once. → [[Editing-a-Sequence]]

## Common mistakes

- **Calling a "best of" a series.** Ten favourite photos with nothing in common are a
  collection. A series needs a shared idea and shared rules.
- **Keeping a great single that breaks the rules.** A frame in different light or colour
  pulls attention to the inconsistency. Save it for another set.
- **Rules so strict that nothing changes.** Twenty identical frames give the eye nothing
  to compare. The variable has to vary.
- **A theme that's too broad.** "Singapore" is a topic. "Hawker stalls after closing
  time" is a series.
- **Thinking a series lets weak pictures in.** Quiet pictures can stay. Weak ones,
  technically poor or off-topic, still drag the set down.

## Try it

Thirty minutes near home: a mini-typology.

1. Pick one kind of everyday object: letterbox banks, bicycle racks, shophouse doors,
   playground slides.
2. Set the rules: the same distance, camera level at chest height, the object in the
   centre, flat light (overcast or open shade), one focal length.
3. Photograph nine examples.
4. Lay them out in a 3 × 3 grid. What do you notice that one picture alone wouldn't
   show?

This is a warm-up for the capstone, a series of 12–20 images.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- What can a series do that a single photograph can't?
> It makes meaning through comparison. Repeated rules make differences visible, and
> many pictures together can make a claim that one frame can't, such as "this is how
> the city uses this space".

> [!question]- Why did the Bechers photograph every structure the same way?
> So that the only thing changing was the structure. With light, angle and framing held
> constant, the viewer compares shapes, not photographic choices.

> [!question]- Your strongest photo is in warm evening light. The rest of the series is in flat morning light. Keep it?
> Usually cut it, or reshoot that scene in the series' light. It breaks the constants,
> so it draws attention to itself rather than to the subject. Keep it for another set.

> [!question]- Is a quiet picture the same as a weak one?
> No. A quiet picture is well made but calm or simple, and it can rest the eye in a
> series. A weak picture is badly made or off-topic, and it weakens any set it is in.
