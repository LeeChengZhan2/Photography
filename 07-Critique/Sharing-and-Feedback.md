# Sharing and Feedback

> [!summary] In one line
> Likes tell you a picture was seen. Criticism tells you why it works or doesn't. Find a
> few people who will give you the second, ask them good questions, and use what they
> say with care.

## What it is

Sharing is showing your work. Feedback is what comes back. They are not the same thing,
and most places built for sharing give very little real feedback.

| Where | What you get | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| A camera club or critique group | Spoken critique, often from experienced eyes | Regular, honest feedback, and deadlines |
| A photowalk group | Company and quick comments | Practice, and finding critique partners |
| One blunt person who shoots better than you | Specific criticism you trust | Worth more than every platform combined |
| An online critique forum | Written comments from strangers | Volume, if the forum expects real critique |
| Portfolio reviews and open calls | Expert eyes and a deadline | Later, once you have a finished set |
| Social media | Likes and short comments | Sharing and keeping in touch, not learning |

In Singapore, look for a camera club (the Photographic Society of Singapore is one of
the oldest), a photowalk group, or two or three friends who meet once a month to look
at each other's work. [[Resources]] lists more places to get real criticism.

## How it works

### Why likes aren't feedback

A like takes half a second, on a small screen, while someone scrolls. It gives no
reason. It often comes from people who like *you*, not the picture. And the platform
decided who saw the post in the first place.

### What the algorithm does

Platforms don't publish exactly how they rank posts, and they change it often. In
general, they show more of whatever they predict will keep people on the app: posts
that get quick likes, comments, shares, saves or longer viewing. Your numbers depend on
how many people follow you, when you post, and which format the platform is pushing
that month, as much as on the photo.

So what does well tends to be what reads instantly on a phone: bold colour, one clear
subject, a face. That pulls everyone towards the same kind of picture. Quiet, complex
or sequenced work often does worse, and that tells you nothing about its quality.

### What makes feedback useful

Useful feedback is **specific** (it points at the frame), **reasoned** (it says why)
and **repeated** (other people see the same thing). It is more useful still when the
person knows what you were trying to do. → [[Critique-Vocabulary]]

## How to use it

### Asking for feedback

- **Give your intent in a sentence or two.** "I wanted the set to feel calm and early."
- **Ask specific questions.** "Where does your eye go first?" "Which two would you
  cut?" "Does the order hold?" Never just "Thoughts?"
- **Show a few pictures:** 5–12, not 50.
- **Ask for the weakest, not the best.** Most people find it easier to be kind than
  useful. This question gives them permission to be honest.
- **Ask them to describe before they judge.** "What do you see?" comes first, as in
  [[Reading-a-Photograph]]. It tells you whether the picture reads at all.
- **Don't explain first.** Your words won't hang next to the print on a wall. Let them
  meet the picture cold.

### Using it

Sort each comment before you act on it:

| Comment | Type | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| "The horizon is tilted." | Fact | Fix it |
| "I'd crop the left side." | Suggestion | Try it, compare the two versions, then decide |
| "I don't get it." | Reaction | Ask where they got lost |
| "Not my kind of thing." | Taste | Note it and move on |
| Three people say the middle drags | Pattern | Act on it |

Write comments down word for word, and wait a day before changing anything. You don't
have to take every suggestion, but you do have to consider it.

### Using social media sensibly

Post if you enjoy it, but don't use the numbers to judge your progress. Some apps let
you hide like counts; try that during the course. Check how a platform crops and
compresses your pictures before you post a set there.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating likes as a grade.** They measure reach and speed, not quality.
- **Asking "Any thoughts?"** You'll get "nice". Ask a specific question.
- **Defending each picture.** The viewer can only respond to what is in the frame.
  Listen, ask, and write it down.
- **Only asking friends who love you.** Their kindness is real, but it isn't
  criticism.
- **Changing everything after one comment.** Wait for a pattern, or test the change
  first.
- **Dropping a project because a post flopped.** A quiet week online is not a verdict
  on the work.

## Try it

Twenty minutes to set up, then wait for the replies.

1. Choose 6–10 frames, for example your **A7.4** sequence.
2. Write three lines: your intent, and two specific questions.
3. Send it to one person whose eye you trust, or take it to a club or group.
4. Log the answers word for word, sorted with the table above.

**A5.6** asked you to show your film to someone who wasn't there. This is the same idea
for stills: a fresh pair of eyes.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why is a like not feedback?
> It gives no reason, comes in half a second on a small screen, and depends on
> followers, timing and the algorithm as much as on the picture. You can't act on it.

> [!question]- Write a better request than "Any thoughts?"
> For example: "These eight are about early mornings in my block. Which two would you
> cut, and where does the set lose you?" It states the intent and asks something
> specific.

> [!question]- Three people give you three different suggestions. What do you do?
> Look for what they agree on underneath; perhaps all three point at the same picture.
> Test the suggestions that fit your intent, compare the versions, then decide.

> [!question]- Why ask for the weakest picture rather than the best?
> People default to praise. Asking for the weakest gives them permission to be honest,
> and points you straight at what to change.

> [!question]- A post gets half its usual likes. What can you conclude about the photo?
> Almost nothing. Timing, the algorithm and that month's favoured format all move the
> number. Ask a person instead.
