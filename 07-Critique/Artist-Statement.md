# Artist Statement

> [!summary] In one line
> About 150 plain words that say what your pictures show, why you made them and how.
> Every sentence should be something a viewer can check against the photographs.

## What it is

An artist statement is a short text that goes with a body of work: on a wall, at the
front of a portfolio, or in an open call (an invitation to submit work for a show, a
book or a prize). It answers three questions:

- **What** are the photographs of?
- **Why** did you make them? What are they about, for you?
- **How** did you make them, where that changes how we read them?

It is not a biography, a CV or a list of camera settings. It is written in the first
person ("I"), in plain words.

## How it works

The statement is a contract between your words and your pictures. A viewer reads it,
then looks for what it promised. If they find it, they trust both. If the words promise
"loneliness" and the pictures show crowded, cheerful markets, they trust neither.

Writing it is also a test for you. If you can't say in plain words what the work is
about, the series may not have a centre yet. That's why Gate 5 asks that your statement
and your portfolio agree. When they don't, one of them has to change.

Art-speak fails the test because it can't be checked. "Explores notions of memory and
place" fits almost any photograph. A sentence that fits everything says nothing about
yours.

### A structure

| Part | The question | Length | Starts like |
|---|---|---|---|
| What | What are the pictures of? | 1–2 sentences | "These photographs show…" |
| Why | Why this subject? What drew you to it? | 2–3 sentences | "I started when…" |
| How | How did you make them? Only what changes the reading | 1–2 sentences | "I photographed…, always…" |
| Close (optional) | What should a viewer look for? | 1 sentence | "Look for…" |

### An example (invented)

> **Void Decks, 6 a.m.**
>
> Most HDB blocks in Singapore stand on a void deck: an open ground floor that belongs
> to everyone. By day it is a shortcut, a playground, and a place for weddings and
> funerals. These twelve photographs show it in the hour before the block wakes up.
>
> I walked through void decks for twenty years without seeing them. When a new job made
> me start early, I found they had a morning life of their own: a cleaner who sings
> while she mops, a man doing tai chi beside the letterboxes, a cat asleep on a stone
> table.
>
> I photographed eight blocks in Toa Payoh between 6 and 7 a.m., always from waist
> height and without flash. The fluorescent tubes and the first daylight do all the
> lighting.
>
> I wanted the pictures to stay as quiet as the place. Look for the people. They are
> small, but they are always there.

That's about 150 words, and every claim can be checked: twelve pictures, void decks,
early light, waist height, no flash, small figures. Now compare an art-speak version:

> This body of work interrogates liminal urban space, exploring the tension between the
> communal and the individual and inviting the viewer to reflect on the human condition.

Which space? Whose tension? What should the viewer look for? Nothing in it can be
checked against a picture.

## How to use it

Write a draft before you shoot, as a brief. Rewrite it after the edit, from what the
pictures actually show.

1. **Answer what, why and how** in one sentence each.
2. **Expand to about 150 words,** using the structure above.
3. **Cut every word you wouldn't say out loud to a friend.**
4. **Run the underline test.** Lay the statement next to the pictures. Underline each
   claim, and find a picture that proves it. Rewrite or delete claims with no proof.
5. **Read it aloud.** Where you stumble, the sentence is too long.
6. **Test it on a reader.** Let someone read it before they see the pictures. Ask what
   they expected, and what they found.

Words that often hide a missing idea: *explores, interrogates, liminal, the human
condition, notions of, a dialogue between*. If you catch one, write what you actually
mean instead.

## Common mistakes

- **Art-speak.** It sounds serious and says nothing. Use the words you'd use with a
  friend.
- **Claims the pictures don't support.** If the statement says "quiet" and three frames
  are loud, change the frames or change the words.
- **A list of settings.** f/1.7 and ISO 3200 don't belong, unless the method changes the
  reading, such as long exposures that remove people.
- **A biography.** Where you studied and what you've won belong in a separate bio.
- **Telling the viewer what to feel.** "You will feel the loneliness" is a promise.
  Describe what you saw, and let them feel it.
- **Going long.** Past 150 words, readers skim. Cut.

## Try it

Twenty minutes. Write a 150-word statement for a body of work that isn't yours: a series
by someone on [[Photographers-to-Study]], such as the Bechers' water towers. Use only
what you can see in the pictures. Then run the underline test on it.

Then write a 50-word what, why and how for your **A7.4** sequence. That is the start of
the draft statement for week 28, before **Gate 5**.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Your statement says the series is about "the loneliness of city life", but most frames show crowds. What now?
> The words and the pictures disagree, so one of them must change. Rewrite the statement
> to say what the pictures show (perhaps one person inside a crowd), or reshoot for the
> idea you meant.

> [!question]- What is wrong with "this work explores the human condition"?
> It fits any photograph ever made, so it says nothing about yours. A viewer can't check
> it against a single frame.

> [!question]- Should you mention that you shot at f/1.7?
> Only if it changes how we read the work. Most viewers don't need settings. "Long
> exposures that erase the crowds" earns its place; "f/1.7, ISO 800" doesn't.

> [!question]- When should you write the statement: before shooting, or after?
> Both. A draft before works as a brief and keeps the shoot focused. The final version
> comes after the edit, written from what the pictures actually show.
