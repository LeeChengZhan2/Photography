# Reading a Photograph

> [!summary] In one line
> A slow, four-step way to look at any photograph: describe it, analyse it, interpret
> it, then judge it. Doing the steps in that order stops a quick "I like it" from
> deciding everything.

## What it is

Edmund Feldman, an American art educator, set out four steps for talking about any work
of art. They work just as well for photographs, your own included.

| Step | The question | Allowed | Not allowed yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Describe | What is literally in the frame? | Things, people, places, positions, sizes | Any opinion or meaning |
| 2. Analyse | How is it built? | Light, composition, tone, colour, moment, likely settings | What it means |
| 3. Interpret | What is it about? | Meaning and mood, each backed by evidence | Whether it is good |
| 4. Judge | Does it work? | A verdict, with reasons | — |

The vault's Critique template uses the same four headings. Assignment A7.1 asks you to
fill it in twenty times.

## How it works

You decide whether you like a picture almost instantly. The reasons come later. If you
start with that verdict, you then look only for evidence that agrees with it. The four
steps hold the verdict back until you have looked properly.

Each step feeds the next:

- **Describing** makes you look at the whole frame: the edges, the background, the
  out-of-focus parts, the empty space. Most people only look at the subject.
- **Analysing** links each effect to a decision. "The face glows" becomes "the face is
  the only lit area, lit by a lamp on the left". A decision is something you can copy.
- **Interpreting** turns those decisions into meaning. Because you described and
  analysed first, your reading points to things in the frame, not just to a feeling.
- **Judging** comes last, when you know what the photograph is trying to do. You can
  then judge it on its own terms, not only against your taste.

The order also makes critique fair between people. Two people should agree on the
description, and mostly on the analysis. They may disagree on the meaning and the
verdict. That is fine, as long as each one points to evidence.

If you need a detail you didn't describe, go back and add it. The order is a way of
thinking, not a one-way door.

## How to use it

Give each step about the same time. For A7.1, that's 5–8 minutes per step, written down.

1. **Describe** in plain sentences. Use nouns, verbs and positions: "left third", "in
   the foreground", "out of focus". No words like *beautiful*, *sad* or *clever*.
2. **Analyse** four areas: light (quality, direction, colour, contrast), composition
   (where the eye goes first, second, third), tone and colour, and the moment. Guess the
   focal length and settings, then say what they *did* to the picture.
3. **Interpret** in one sentence: "This photograph is about…". Then add two or three
   "because…" lines that point to the frame.
4. **Judge** with three questions. What is it trying to do? Does it do it? What one
   change would make it stronger?

Stephen Shore's *The Nature of Photographs*, listed in [[Resources]], is a good next
read on how photographs carry meaning.

### Worked example

The photograph (invented for this note): a horizontal colour frame, at night, in a
hawker centre after closing time.

**Describe.** Most stalls have their shutters down. One stall on the right is still lit
by a bare fluorescent tube. Under it, an elderly man in a white singlet wipes a steel
counter with a cloth. His arm is stretched out, mid-wipe. Rows of empty tables and
stools fill the left half and fade into darkness. A green plastic stool lies on its side
close to the camera, out of focus, in the bottom-left corner. The man takes up about a
fifth of the frame's height.

**Analyse.**

- *Light:* one hard source from above, so the shadow under his arm has a sharp edge. It
  falls off fast, and the tables go dark within a few metres. → [[Inverse-Square-Law]]
- *Tone and colour:* the frame is low-key (mostly dark tones). The counter and his
  singlet are the only bright areas. The tube light is cool and slightly green.
- *Composition:* the bright stall pulls the eye first. The big green stool competes,
  because it is large, close and the only strong colour. The rows of tables lead from
  the stool to the stall across the dark half, which works as [[Negative-Space]].
- *Moment:* his arm is at full stretch, in the middle of a gesture.
- *Settings:* the blurred stool means a wide aperture; the dark scene means a high ISO.
  Perhaps f/2, 1/60 s, ISO 3200.

**Interpret.** This is about work that nobody sees: the last person in an empty place.
The single light makes him the only thing left. The stool on its side hints that a crowd
has just gone. The cold light makes the mood tired rather than cosy. Someone else might
read pride instead: he cleans carefully though no one is watching. Both readings use
evidence from the frame.

**Judge.** It mostly works. The light and the empty space carry the idea at a glance.
The stool adds meaning, but it is too big: it is the largest shape and the only strong
colour, so it fights the man for attention. Smaller in the frame, from a step back or a
step to the side, it would keep its meaning and stop competing. In a series about
hawker centres at night, this could be the closing frame.

## Common mistakes

- **Judging inside the description.** "A stunning sunset" is a verdict. Write what is
  there: "the sun is just above the sea; the sky is orange near it and deep blue above."
- **Describing only the subject.** Look at the corners, the edges, the background and
  the empty areas too. Problems and meaning often hide there.
- **Interpreting without evidence.** "It's about loneliness" needs a "because" that
  points to something in the frame.
- **Judging the subject, not the photograph.** Liking cats doesn't make a cat photo
  work. Ask whether the light, framing and timing serve it.
- **Stopping at the settings.** "f/2, ISO 3200" describes the camera. Say what those
  settings did to the picture.
- **Only critiquing photos you like.** A7.1 asks for half you dislike, because that is
  where you learn to separate taste from judgement.

## Try it

Twenty minutes, with one photograph by someone on [[Photographers-to-Study]]. Choose one
you don't like.

1. Set a timer for five minutes per step, and write each step under its own heading.
2. In the description, allow no adjective that judges.
3. When you finish, reread the first two steps. Underline any judgement that slipped in,
   and rewrite it as description.

This is the first of the twenty critiques in **A7.1**.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Your description says "a lonely old man sits at a table". Which word belongs to a later step, and why?
> "Lonely" is an interpretation. It is a reading of his mood, not something you can
> point at. Describe the evidence instead: "a man sits alone; the other four stools at
> his table are empty."

> [!question]- Why does judging come last rather than first?
> Once you've decided you like or dislike a photo, you look only for reasons that agree.
> Judging last means the verdict rests on what you saw and understood, and you can judge
> the photo against what it was trying to do.

> [!question]- Two people interpret the same photo differently. Can both be right?
> Yes, if each reading points to evidence in the frame. They should still agree on the
> description. If they don't, one of them hasn't looked carefully.

> [!question]- In your analysis you wrote "f/2, 1/60 s, ISO 3200". What is missing?
> The effect of each choice. For example: f/2 blurred the stool in the foreground,
> 1/60 s left his moving hand slightly soft, and ISO 3200 added grain in the dark half.
