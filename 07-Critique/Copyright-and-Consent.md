# Copyright and Consent

> [!summary] In one line
> Copyright decides who owns a photograph and who may use it. Consent decides whether
> the people and places in it agreed. A photo can pass one test and fail the other, so
> check both before you post, sell or reuse anything.

## What it is

> [!warning] General information, not legal advice
> Laws differ between countries and change over time. This note gives general
> principles, with some Singapore notes, as of late 2026. For a contract, a paid job or
> a dispute, ask a lawyer. For Singapore basics, see the guides published by the
> Intellectual Property Office of Singapore (IPOS).

Five separate questions come up with almost every photograph:

| Question | What decides it |
|---|---|
| May I take it here? | The place's owner, signs, and laws on harassment and protected places |
| Who owns it? | Copyright: normally the photographer |
| May I publish it? | Consent, privacy, honest context, and your own judgement |
| May someone else use it? | A licence from the copyright owner |
| May I use theirs? | Their permission, a licence such as Creative Commons, or a narrow legal exception |

## How it works

### Who owns a photo

**Copyright** is the right to copy, publish, adapt and license a work. It exists
automatically the moment you take the photo. In Singapore, as in most countries, you
don't register it, and you don't need a © notice. It lasts for decades: in many
countries, until 70 years after the creator dies. Owning a print or a file is not
owning the copyright.

Normally the photographer owns it. Two common exceptions:

- **Employment.** Photos you take as part of your job usually belong to your employer,
  unless your contract says otherwise.
- **Commissions.** Rules differ by country. In Singapore, the Copyright Act 2021 made
  the photographer the default owner of commissioned photos too, unless a contract says
  otherwise. The client can still use the photos for the purpose they paid for, such as
  sharing wedding photos with family. Under the older law the client usually owned
  them, so older jobs may follow the old rule. Put ownership in writing either way.

### Licences

A **licence** is permission to use a work on stated terms. You keep the copyright; the
user gets limited rights. A licence says which photos, what use, for how long and
where, whether it is exclusive (only they may use it), and the fee and credit. For
example, a café could license your hawker-centre photo for its menu: one year, print
only, non-exclusive, for a fee and a credit.

### Using other people's photos and music

The default is no. You need permission, or a licence that covers your use.

- **Found online doesn't mean free.** No watermark and no © notice change nothing.
- **Credit is not permission.** Naming the photographer doesn't make the use legal.
  In Singapore you usually need both: since 2021, creators have a right to be named
  whenever their work is used in public, separate from the copyright.
- **"Royalty-free" isn't "free".** It means you pay once, or nothing, under a licence,
  with no fee for each use. Read the licence.
- **Legal exceptions are narrow.** Most countries allow some uses, such as criticism,
  review, news or study, but judge them case by case. Don't rely on one without advice.
- **Music needs a licence too,** even for a few seconds, even with a credit. Platforms
  may mute or block videos with unlicensed music, and music from a platform's own
  library is often licensed for that platform only. → [[Sound-Mixing]]

### Creative Commons

Creative Commons (CC) licences give permission in advance, with conditions. They
combine four elements: **BY** (credit the creator), **SA** (ShareAlike: anything you
make from it carries the same licence), **NC** (NonCommercial: no commercial use) and
**ND** (NoDerivatives: no changed versions).

So "CC BY-NC" means: credit the creator, and no commercial use. For music, the licence
counts putting it to moving pictures as changing it: ND music can't go in your film,
and SA music makes your film carry the same licence. **CC0** gives up all rights. When
you credit, give the title, the author, the source and the licence. If you release your
own work under CC, you can't take that permission back from people who have already
used it. **Public domain** means the copyright has expired or been
given up. It doesn't mean "publicly visible".

### People and places

In Singapore, as in many countries, photographing people in public places is generally
lawful. But laws against harassment and voyeurism still apply, and a photo that was
legal to take can still be unkind or unwise to publish. Singapore's Personal Data
Protection Act (PDPA) mainly applies to organisations, not to individuals acting in a
personal or domestic capacity; if you photograph people for a business, it may apply
to you.

Since 29 June 2026, Singapore's Online Safety Commission can also order harmful posts
taken down. It starts with intimate images, abusive images of children, doxxing
(posting someone's personal details to harm them), harassment and stalking.

A **model release** is a signed form in which a person agrees to how their image will
be used: which shoot, which uses, for how long, any payment, signed and dated.

- **Commercial use** (advertising, promoting a product, stock) needs a release from
  every recognisable person. Clients and stock agencies will ask for one.
- **Editorial use** (news, education, commentary) and personal portfolios often don't,
  but asking is courteous and keeps later uses open.
- **Children:** a parent or guardian must agree, and schools and events often have
  their own rules. A school uniform or crest can reveal where a child studies.

Some places forbid photography. In Singapore, military areas and some other protected
places carry signs that forbid it: follow them. Private property, such as malls,
condos, offices, museums and places of worship, can set its own rules. Organised or
commercial shoots often need permission, even in public places. → [[Shoot-Planning]]

### Manipulation and AI

Adjusting tone and colour is normal. Adding, removing or generating content is not.

- **News and documentary** forbid adding or removing things. Many competitions do too,
  and may ask to see your raw file.
- **Elsewhere, disclose heavy manipulation** when a reasonable viewer would assume the
  picture is a straight record. Composites and generative fill count.
- **Platforms** increasingly ask you to label realistic AI-generated or altered images
  and video, and their rules change often. Who owns an AI-generated image is unsettled,
  because copyright generally expects a human author. → [[Retouching]]

### Metadata

Your files can carry your name, a copyright notice and contact details in their
metadata (the hidden information stored inside the file). Set this up once as an
import preset in your raw developer, and check the L10's manual for a setting that
writes your name into each file. Many social platforms strip metadata on upload, so it
helps honest people find you but doesn't stop copying. Some apps can also attach
Content Credentials: a record of how an image was made and edited. Since August 2026,
Adobe's apps add them automatically to images made or changed with their generative AI
tools.

## How to use it

Before you post, run five checks:

1. Do I own this, or have a licence for all of it, including the music?
2. Is anyone recognisable? Would they mind? Is any child identifiable?
3. Does the caption tell the truth about who, where and what?
4. Have I removed location data from photos of my home or family?
5. Have I disclosed heavy manipulation or AI?

## Common mistakes

- **"It was on the internet, so it's free."** Copyright is automatic. Find a licence,
  or ask.
- **Using a song because the clip is short.** There's no safe number of seconds. Use
  music you're licensed to use.
- **Assuming the client owns what they paid for.** In Singapore the default now
  normally favours the photographer. Put it in a contract.
- **Posting children without thinking.** Get a guardian's agreement, and leave out
  names, schools and locations.

## Try it

Twenty minutes.

1. Set up a metadata preset in your raw developer with your name, a copyright line
   (© year and your name) and an email address. Apply it on import.
2. Check your last ten posts against the five checks above. Fix or remove anything that
   fails.
3. For your **A5.6** film, write down where the music came from and its licence terms.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- In Singapore, you pay a photographer to shoot your family portraits. Who owns the copyright by default?
> Under the current law, normally the photographer, unless your contract says otherwise.
> You can still use them for what you paid for, such as sharing them with family. So
> the contract should say who owns the photos and how each side may use them.

> [!question]- A photo online has no watermark and no © notice. Can you use it in your video?
> No. Copyright exists without a notice. You need the owner's permission, a licence
> such as Creative Commons that covers your use, or a clear legal exception.

> [!question]- A photo is licensed CC BY-NC. Can you use it on a poster for a paid event?
> No. NC means no commercial use, and a paid event is commercial. Ask the owner for
> separate permission.

> [!question]- You photographed a stranger at a hawker centre. Now a brand wants the photo for an advert. What changes?
> The use becomes commercial. You still own the copyright, but the brand will need a
> signed model release from that person. Owning the photo doesn't settle consent.
