# Editing a Sequence

> [!summary] In one line
> Choosing which photographs to show, and putting them in order. The order changes what
> each picture means, so a good sequence says more than the same pictures shuffled.

## What it is

Here "editing" means what a picture editor or a curator does: **selecting** photographs
and **sequencing** them. It isn't moving sliders (that's post-processing), and it isn't
cutting video, though it works in a similar way.

A sequence has four working parts:

| Part | What it is |
|---|---|
| Pairs | Two pictures seen together, side by side or one after the other |
| Rhythm | The pattern of loud and quiet, close and far, dark and light |
| Flow | What carries the eye from one picture to the next |
| Opening and closing | The first and last frames, which set and settle the meaning |

## How it works

In a sequence, nobody sees a photograph on its own. Each one is read against the one
before. Film editors call this the Kuleshov effect, after an early Soviet film-maker. In
his famous experiment, the same face, cut after a bowl of soup or after a coffin, seemed
to show hunger or grief. Photographs work the same way.

### Pairs

When two pictures sit together, the eye looks for the link. The link makes a third
meaning that neither picture has alone.

| Pair by | Example | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Echo of shape | A round lamp, then a round face | Feels designed; the eye connects them |
| Echo of colour | A red door, then a red umbrella | Carries the eye across |
| Contrast of scale | A crowd, then one face | A jolt; puts weight on the face |
| Contrast of tone | A dark room, then a bright street | A change of beat |
| Look and answer | A man looking right, then what he might be seeing | Suggests a story |

An echo between two photographs works like a match cut in film: a cut that joins two
shots through a shared shape or movement. → [[Cuts-and-Transitions]]

### Rhythm

A sequence is read in time, like music. Twelve equally loud pictures tire the viewer, so
none of them lands. Quieter frames, such as a detail or an empty space, work as rests
that let the strong ones hit. Vary the scale, the density and the tone. Repeat a motif,
then break it. → [[Pacing-and-Rhythm]]

### Flow

Small things carry the eye from one picture to the next: a line that continues, a
movement in the same direction, a colour that repeats. People facing *into* the sequence
pull you forward; people facing out of it push you away. A deliberate jump, such as a
sudden change of colour or scale, marks the start of a new section.

### Opening and closing

People tend to remember the first and last items in a list best. Psychologists call this
the serial-position effect. So the first and last frames carry extra weight.

- **The opener** sets the terms: where we are, what kind of pictures these are, the
  mood. It should make the viewer want the next one. It isn't always your best picture.
- **The closer** settles the sequence, or leaves a question open. It often echoes the
  opener, or is quieter. It's the picture people walk away with.

### Photobooks as models

A photobook is a sequence you can hold. Page turns set the timing, and each spread (two
facing pages) is a pair. Borrow these from a library and read them for the order, not
for the single pictures:

- Walker Evans, *American Photographs* (1938).
- Robert Frank, *The Americans* (late 1950s). Flags, cars and jukeboxes keep returning.
- Stephen Shore, *Uncommon Places* (1982).
- Any of Rinko Kawauchi's photobooks: quiet, often square frames that echo each other
  across each spread.

## How to use it

For **A7.4** (take 30 keepers and sequence 12 of them), work on paper. A screen shows
you one picture at a time; a table shows you all of them at once.

1. **Print all 30 small.** Contact sheets on A4 paper, cut up, are enough.
2. **Spread them out and group them** by subject, colour or mood. Five minutes.
3. **Make pairs.** Find the five or six strongest links. Note which pictures fight.
4. **Pick two or three possible openers and closers.**
5. **Lay out a first twelve.** Read it fast, as a stranger would. Then read it slowly.
6. **Cut the weakest picture** and bring in the best one waiting. Repeat.
7. **Photograph the layout** with your phone, so you can rebuild it.
8. **Leave it overnight.** Reread it cold. Weak links show the next day.
9. **Write two sentences:** why this opener, and why this closer.

Rules of thumb:

- **Spread the strong pictures out,** because a sequence that peaks early sags at the
  end.
- **One weak photograph weakens the set,** because viewers assume you can't see that it
  is weak.
- **Order by meaning, not by date,** because the order you shot in rarely matches how
  the story reads.

## Common mistakes

- **Putting all the best pictures at the front.** The rest then reads as filler. Space
  them out.
- **Keeping a frame because it was hard to get.** The viewer can't see the effort. Judge
  only what is in the picture.
- **Too many similar frames in a row.** Two near-identical pictures cancel each other
  out. Keep the stronger one.
- **Sequencing on a phone.** You can't see the whole set at once. Print small, or use a
  grid view on a large screen.
- **Fixing a weak sequence by adding pictures.** Remove instead. Twelve good frames beat
  twenty mixed ones.

## Try it

Fifteen minutes, with twenty keepers from any module, as small prints or a grid on
screen.

1. Make three pairs: one echo, one contrast, one "look and answer".
2. Choose an opener and a closer, and write one line on each.
3. Swap the opener and the closer. What changes in how the set reads?

This is a small version of **A7.4**.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why might your single best photograph be the wrong opener?
> An opener has a job: set the place, the subject and the tone, and pull the viewer in.
> Your best frame may work harder later, where it lands after a build-up.

> [!question]- Two photographs look great alone but wrong side by side. What might be happening?
> They may compete: two strong centres, clashing colours, or subjects facing away from
> each other. Or they repeat each other, so the second adds nothing. Split them up.

> [!question]- What does a quiet picture do in a sequence?
> It works as a rest. It lowers the tempo so that the next strong frame lands. A
> sequence of only loud pictures goes numb.

> [!question]- How is sequencing photographs like cutting a film?
> Both are read in time, and each image is read against the one before. Echoes work
> like match cuts, quiet frames work like held shots, and rhythm matters in both.
