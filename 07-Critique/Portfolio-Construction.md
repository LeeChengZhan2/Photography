# Portfolio Construction

> [!summary] In one line
> A portfolio is a small, tightly edited set, usually 12–20 images, that shows what you
> do and how well. What you leave out says as much about you as what you put in.

## What it is

A portfolio is not an archive or a "best of everything". It's a selection made for one
purpose and one audience: Gate 5, a club critique, an open call, a client. Different
purposes can need different portfolios.

It shows two skills at once: making pictures, and choosing them.

| A good portfolio | A weak portfolio |
|---|---|
| 12–20 images | 40 images "to show range" |
| One subject, idea or way of seeing | Every genre you've tried |
| Every image strong | A few strong, many fine |
| One consistent finish | Each image edited differently |
| A deliberate order | Shot order, or random |

## How it works

**Viewers judge by the weakest image.** If you include a weak frame, they assume you
can't tell it's weak. That costs more than the frame was worth. So choosing is part of
the work.

**Coherence lets the content show.** In a consistent set, the viewer looks at the
subjects. In a mixed set, they notice the changes of style instead, and the work looks
undecided.

**Twelve to twenty is a range that works.** Fewer than about ten pictures can't show
consistency. Past about twenty, weaker frames creep in and attention drops. Your
capstone uses the same range.

**Good is not enough.** A strong photograph that doesn't fit the theme still hurts the
set, because it breaks the pattern. Cutting it doesn't mean it's bad. It means it
belongs somewhere else.

## How to use it

### Building it

1. **Decide the purpose and the audience.** For Gate 5, the audience is you, and the
   test is whether the portfolio and your [[Artist-Statement]] agree.
2. **Make a long list** of 60–100 candidates from the whole course.
3. **Cut technical faults:** missed focus, unintended blur, clipped highlights you
   didn't choose. → [[Culling-Discipline]]
4. **Cut off-topic work,** however good. Move it to an "orphans" folder. It may start
   another portfolio.
5. **Check coherence.** Lay the rest out as a grid. Anything that jumps out for the
   wrong reason (a different colour, ratio or distance) goes, or gets re-edited.
6. **Get down to 12–20.**
7. **Sequence it,** choosing the opener and the closer with care.
   → [[Editing-a-Sequence]]
8. **Finish consistently:** one look, one export size, one caption format.
9. **Test it.** Show it to one person and ask which two pictures they would cut.

### Formats

| Format | Use it for | Practical points |
|---|---|---|
| Web page | Sharing a link | sRGB JPEGs, about 2000–2500 px on the long edge. One large image at a time, on a plain background. Check it on a phone. → [[Export-Settings]] |
| Prints | Reviews in person; the capstone print | One size and one paper for all. Check prints in daylight; the screen hides problems. → [[Printing]] |
| PDF | Open calls, applications, email | One image per page, plus a title page with your name and statement. Stay under the call's file-size limit. Name the file clearly, such as `Name-Project-2027.pdf` |

Social media is not a portfolio. A feed mixes everything, crops your frames and buries
older work. Use it to point people to the portfolio.

### Keeping it current

- **Review it every three months,** or after each project.
- **One in, one out.** A new picture has to beat the weakest one already there.
- **Keep dated copies** of old versions. They are your record of progress.
- **Update the statement** when the pictures change.

## Common mistakes

- **Too many images.** Forty pictures "to show range" show that you can't choose. Cut
  to the best 12–20.
- **Mixing genres.** Street, birds and weddings in one set read as three
  half-portfolios. Make separate ones.
- **Keeping a picture because it got likes.** Likes measure a quick reaction on a small
  screen, not whether a picture fits the set.
- **Inconsistent editing.** One warm, one cold, one black and white looks accidental.
  Re-edit to one look.
- **Near-duplicates.** Two frames of the same moment cancel each other out. Keep one.
- **Building it once and forgetting it.** A portfolio from two years ago shows who you
  were. Review it on a schedule.

## Try it

Thirty minutes, with your keepers from Modules 4–6.

1. Pull 40 candidates.
2. Cut to 15 in two passes: faults first, then fit.
3. For each picture you cut in the second pass, write one line on why.
4. Put the 15 in a grid. Which one would a stranger point to as the odd one out?

This is a first pass at the portfolio that has to agree with your statement at
**Gate 5**.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why can one weak picture hurt a whole portfolio?
> Viewers judge your eye as well as your pictures. A weak frame suggests you can't tell
> the difference, which makes them doubt the strong ones too.

> [!question]- Your technically best photo is a landscape. Your portfolio is portraits. What do you do with it?
> Leave it out. It's good, but it breaks the pattern and makes the set look undecided.
> Keep it for a landscape portfolio.

> [!question]- Why aim for 12–20 images rather than 50?
> Fewer than about ten can't show consistency. Past about twenty, weaker frames creep in
> and viewers' attention fades. A tight set shows judgement.

> [!question]- Why export a web portfolio in sRGB?
> Most screens and browsers expect sRGB. A file in a wider colour space, such as Adobe
> RGB, can look dull or wrong where its profile isn't read. → [[Colour-Spaces]]

> [!question]- How do you keep a portfolio current without losing the record of your progress?
> Review it on a schedule and swap one in, one out. Save each old version with a date,
> so you can see how your work has changed.
