# Critique Vocabulary

> [!summary] In one line
> Precise words for light, composition, tone, colour, moment and meaning. They replace
> "nice" with something you can check, repeat or change, and that is what makes
> feedback useful.

## What it is

"Nice", "interesting" and "powerful" tell you that someone reacted. They don't say to
what. Critique vocabulary is the set of words that names the cause.

| Instead of | Say something like | It names |
|---|---|---|
| "Nice light" | "Soft window light from the left; the shadow side is a stop or two darker" | Quality, direction, contrast |
| "Good composition" | "The rail is a diagonal that leads from the bottom-left corner to her face" | Structure, eye path |
| "Great colours" | "A limited palette of teal and orange, with one red accent" | Palette, contrast |
| "Moody" | "Low-key: most of the frame is deep shadow; only the face is lit" | Tone |
| "Cool moment" | "Caught at the top of the jump, feet off the ground, shadow below" | Timing |
| "Busy" | "Three bright areas compete, so the eye has nowhere to settle" | Visual weight |
| "Powerful" | "The empty chair beside her makes the absence the subject" | Meaning |

## How it works

A precise word points at something in the frame, so anyone can check it. If you say
"hard light", the shadow edges should be sharp. If they aren't, the word is wrong, and
you have both learned something. "Nice" can't be wrong, so it can't teach anything.

Words also train your eye. Once you know the word *merger*, you start to notice poles
growing out of heads in the viewfinder, before you press the shutter.

### A word bank

| Area | Words | What to look at in the frame |
|---|---|---|
| Light | hard, soft, front, side, back, rim, top, warm, cool, mixed, fall-off, contrast ratio | Shadow edges, where shadows fall, the colour of highlights and shadows |
| Composition | balance, visual weight, eye path, leading line, diagonal, layers, negative space, framing device, merger, point of view | Where the eye lands first, second, third; what touches the edges |
| Tone | high-key, low-key, tonal range, clipped highlights, blocked shadows, separation, local contrast | The brightest and darkest areas; the subject against its background |
| Colour | palette, harmony, complementary, saturation, colour cast, accent, muted | How many colours; which one dominates; whether whites look neutral |
| Moment | peak, gesture, anticipation, aftermath, frozen, motion blur, stillness | Hands, faces, feet; what happens just before and just after |
| Meaning | subject, theme, juxtaposition, ambiguity, tension, irony, sense of place, intimacy, distance | What it is *of*, what it is *about*, how close the photographer feels |

The words that most often need explaining:

- **Rim light** — light from behind that draws a bright edge around the subject.
- **Merger** — two things overlap so they read as one: a pole growing out of a head, a
  dark coat lost against a dark wall.
- **Separation** — the subject stands apart from the background, by tone, colour, focus
  or light.
- **High-key / low-key** — mostly light tones / mostly dark tones, on purpose. Not the
  same as over- or underexposed. → [[Tonal-Composition]]
- **Clipped highlights / blocked shadows** — areas pushed to pure white or pure black,
  with no detail left.
- **Visual weight** — how strongly an area pulls the eye. Brightness, contrast,
  saturation, faces and text all add weight. → [[Visual-Weight-and-Balance]]
- **Complementary colours** — colours opposite each other on the colour wheel, such as
  orange and blue. Side by side, each makes the other look stronger.
- **Juxtaposition** — two things placed together so each changes how you read the
  other. → [[Juxtaposition]]
- **Peak** — the top of an action, when movement pauses for an instant: the top of a
  jump, the end of a swing. → [[Gesture-and-Moment]]

## How to use it

### Giving feedback

Use three parts: **what you see → what it does → what to try.**

> "The red sign in the top-left corner is the strongest colour in the frame. It pulls
> my eye away from her face. Try cropping it out, or darkening it."

- **Ask what they were going for first.** Feedback is only useful against an intent.
- **Pick the one or two biggest points.** Ten small notes bury the important one.
- **Point at the frame.** "Top-left corner", "the man's hand", "the third photo".
- **Say what works, specifically.** "The gap between the two faces holds the tension" is
  as useful as a fault, because it tells them what to keep.
- **Be kind to the person and honest about the picture.**

### Taking feedback

- **Listen; don't defend.** Explaining what you meant can't change the photo. Viewers
  only have the picture.
- **Ask a follow-up.** "Where did your eye go first?" turns a vague reaction into
  something you can use.
- **Write it down** word for word, and decide later.
- **Look for patterns.** One person's comment is an opinion. The same comment from three
  people is worth acting on.
- **You may disagree.** Test the suggestion, compare the two versions, then choose.

## Common mistakes

- **Using praise words alone.** "Stunning" ends the conversation. Add what made it so.
- **Using jargon to sound clever.** "Nice bokeh" is still "nice". Say what the blur
  does: "the soft background hides the crowd."
- **Critiquing the subject, not the picture.** "Cute dog" is about the dog. "The dog's
  eyes are the only sharp thing in the frame" is about the photograph.
- **Giving a fix with no reason.** "Crop tighter" doesn't teach. "Crop tighter, because
  the empty floor at the bottom takes weight from the face" does.
- **Hearing "I don't like it" as a verdict.** It is a taste. Ask what they would change,
  and why.

## Try it

Twenty minutes with five of your own photographs. For each, write one sentence each on
light, composition, tone or colour, moment and meaning. Ban these words: *nice, good,
great, beautiful, interesting, stunning, cool*. If you can't say anything without them,
look longer. Add any new word you needed to the [[Glossary]].

This is practice for the critiques in **A7.1** and for your cold self-critique in
**A7.2**.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- A friend says "the light is nice". What two questions turn that into useful feedback?
> "Where is it coming from, and is it hard or soft?" and "What does it do to the
> subject?" The answers name direction, quality and effect, which you can repeat.

> [!question]- What is the difference between "high-key" and "overexposed"?
> High-key is a choice: mostly light tones, with detail kept where it matters.
> Overexposed means highlights are clipped and detail is lost, usually by accident.

> [!question]- Why is "the horizon cuts through her head" more useful than "it feels off"?
> It names a specific merger in a specific place, so the photographer knows what to fix:
> lower the camera, move, or wait. "It feels off" gives them nothing to change.

> [!question]- Three people say the middle of your sequence drags. One says it's perfect. What do you do?
> Treat the three as a pattern and look hard at the middle. The one who disagrees may
> simply like slow sequences. Test a tighter version and compare.
