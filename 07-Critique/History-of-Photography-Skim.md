# History of Photography Skim

> [!summary] In one line
> A quick tour of how photography changed, from the first public processes in 1839 to
> today. Each movement answered a new technology or the movement before it, and each one
> left a tool you can still use.

## What it is

A skim, not a course: the moments that changed what photographs look like and what
people thought they were for.

| When | Movement | Names to know | What changed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1839 | The first public processes | Louis Daguerre, William Henry Fox Talbot | Photography went public, in two rival forms |
| 1880s–1910s | Pictorialism | Alfred Stieglitz (early work), Edward Steichen, Gertrude Käsebier | Soft focus and hand-worked prints, to prove photography could be art |
| 1910s–1930s | Straight photography; Group f/64 (1932) | Paul Strand, Edward Weston, Ansel Adams, Imogen Cunningham | Sharp focus, full tonal range: photographs that look like photographs |
| 1930s–1950s | The decisive moment | Henri Cartier-Bresson | Small 35 mm cameras; geometry and timing caught in a split second |
| 1890s onwards | Documentary traditions | Jacob Riis, Lewis Hine, Dorothea Lange, Walker Evans, Robert Frank | Photographs as evidence and argument; later, as a personal view |
| 1970s | New Topographics (1975) | Robert Adams, Lewis Baltz, Bernd and Hilla Becher, Stephen Shore | Ordinary man-made landscapes, shown flat and neutral |
| 1970s | New Colour | William Eggleston, Stephen Shore, Joel Meyerowitz | Colour accepted as art; the everyday as subject |
| Late 1970s–now | Contemporary practice | Cindy Sherman, Jeff Wall, Andreas Gursky, Rinko Kawauchi | Staged pictures, huge prints, series and photobooks, digital and phones |

New Topographics and New Colour ran side by side in the same decade, and Stephen Shore
belongs to both.

## How it works

Two forces drive the story. New technology makes new pictures possible. Then
photographers react against whatever has become the normal way to work.

### 1839: the first public processes

In 1839 two processes were made public within months of each other. Louis Daguerre's
*daguerreotype*, announced in Paris, was a very sharp image on a silvered copper plate.
Each one was unique: there was no negative, so no copies. In England, William Henry Fox
Talbot made a *negative* on paper, from which he could print many *positives*. His
pictures were softer, but they could be copied. The negative–positive idea ruled
photography until digital. (The oldest surviving camera photograph is earlier, from the
1820s, by Nicéphore Niépce.)

Early exposures were long, often minutes. In one of Daguerre's Paris street views, the
busy boulevard looks empty, because moving people didn't stay still long enough to
record. Only a man standing still to have his boots polished appears.

### Pictorialism: photography wants to be art

In 1888 Kodak sold a small camera already loaded with film. When it was full, you sent
the whole camera back for developing. Suddenly anyone could take a photograph.
Pictorialists answered by making pictures that looked hand-made: soft focus, misty
scenes, prints worked by hand. In 1902 Alfred Stieglitz founded the Photo-Secession in
New York to promote photography as art.

### Straight photography and Group f/64

From the 1910s, photographers, Stieglitz among them, turned against the painterly look.
Paul Strand made sharp, direct pictures of the city and of everyday objects. In 1932 a
group in California, including Edward Weston, Ansel Adams and Imogen Cunningham, called
itself **Group f/64**. f/64 was one of the smallest apertures on their large-format
cameras. On big sheets of film it gave great depth of field: sharp from front to back,
with a full range of tones.

You can't copy the number, because depth of field depends on sensor size. On 8×10-inch
film, f/64 gives about the same depth of field as f/4 on your L10's Four Thirds sensor,
for the same framing. → [[Sensor-Size-and-Crop-Factor]]

### The decisive moment

Small 35 mm cameras, led by the Leica in the 1920s, made photography fast and quiet.
Henri Cartier-Bresson used one to catch the instant when shapes and action line up. His
1952 book was published in English as *The Decisive Moment*, and the phrase stuck. In
1947 he co-founded the Magnum Photos agency.

### Documentary traditions

Jacob Riis photographed New York's slums for *How the Other Half Lives* (1890). Lewis
Hine photographed child workers in the 1900s and 1910s, and his pictures helped the
campaign for child labour laws. In the 1930s, US government projects sent Dorothea Lange
and Walker Evans to record the Great Depression. Picture magazines such as *Life*, from
1936, brought photo stories to millions. Then Robert Frank's *The Americans* (late
1950s) showed that documentary could be personal, loose and critical.

In Japan in the late 1960s, the magazine *Provoke*, with Daido Moriyama, went the other
way from f/64: grainy, blurred and out-of-focus pictures, on purpose.

### The 1970s: New Topographics and New Colour

By the 1970s, Ansel Adams' grand, untouched wilderness had become the standard
landscape. In 1975 an exhibition in Rochester, New York, *New Topographics: Photographs
of a Man-Altered Landscape*, showed the opposite: new housing, car parks, industrial
buildings. The pictures were flat, level and calm, with no drama.

At the same time, serious art photography was still mostly black and white. Colour meant
adverts and holiday snaps. In 1976 the Museum of Modern Art in New York gave William
Eggleston a solo show of colour photographs of ordinary scenes. Many critics disliked
it, but it changed the rules. Stephen Shore's *Uncommon Places* (1982) and Joel
Meyerowitz's colour work made the everyday, in colour, a serious subject.

### Contemporary practice

From the late 1970s, Cindy Sherman and Jeff Wall staged their pictures like film scenes.
Students of the Bechers in Düsseldorf, such as Andreas Gursky, made huge, detailed
colour prints. Digital replaced film for most people in the 2000s, and phones then made
everyone a photographer. Many photographers now work in long projects: series,
photobooks, research and archives. The Singapore names on [[Photographers-to-Study]]
show this locally: research-led projects, archives, a consistent palette.

## How to use it

Each movement left a tool. Take the tool, not the costume.

| Movement | The tool you can use now |
|---|---|
| Early processes | Exposure time shapes what a photo can show. Long exposures erase moving people. → [[Long-Exposure]] |
| Pictorialism | Softness and heavy editing are allowed if they add something. Ask what your preset adds. |
| Group f/64 | Previsualise: decide how the finished picture should look before you press the shutter. |
| The decisive moment | Compose the frame first, then wait for the subject to walk into it. → [[Gesture-and-Moment]] |
| Documentary | Get close, earn access, caption honestly. → [[Documentary-and-Photojournalism]] |
| New Topographics | The ordinary built world is a subject: car parks, void decks, MRT viaducts. Shoot it level and calm. |
| New Colour | Colour can be the subject, not decoration. → [[Colour-Theory-for-Photographers]] |
| Contemporary | Think in series and books, not only single frames. → [[Series-vs-Singles]] |

When you critique, ask which tradition a photograph belongs to. A frame that looks
"boring" may be New Topographics doing exactly what it set out to do.

## Common mistakes

- **Memorising names and dates.** Learn what each movement changed, then look through
  one book from it.
- **Looking only at famous single images.** Most of these photographers worked in series
  and books. The one famous picture hides the method.
- **Copying the look without the reason.** Soft focus without an idea is just blur. A
  flat, level frame without a system is just dull.
- **Treating the history as only American and European.** Look at Fan Ho in Hong Kong,
  Daido Moriyama and Rinko Kawauchi in Japan, and the Singapore names on the list.

## Try it

Thirty minutes, any device, somewhere ordinary near home.

1. **Fifteen minutes as New Topographics.** Flat light (overcast or open shade), camera
   level at eye height, no person as the subject. Photograph a car park, a void deck or
   a bus interchange. Ten frames.
2. **Fifteen minutes as the decisive moment.** Find a frame with strong lines, then wait
   for someone to walk into the right spot. Ten frames.
3. Compare the two sets. Which approach suited the place better, and why?

This is a warm-up for **A7.3**, where you copy a photographer on purpose.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why did the pictorialists make photographs look like paintings?
> Cameras had become cheap and easy to use, so a photograph alone didn't look like art.
> Soft, hand-worked prints set their pictures apart from snapshots and borrowed some of
> painting's status.

> [!question]- What does "f/64" refer to, and why would f/64 be a bad idea on your L10?
> It was one of the smallest apertures on large-format cameras, used for great depth of
> field. The L10 stops at f/16, and diffraction softens it from about f/5.6–f/8. Its
> small sensor gives deep focus at much wider apertures anyway.

> [!question]- Ansel Adams and several New Topographics photographers both worked in the American West. What changed?
> The subject and the attitude. Adams showed grand, untouched wilderness. New
> Topographics showed the land people had built over, in flat, neutral pictures without
> drama.

> [!question]- Why did William Eggleston's 1976 show at the Museum of Modern Art matter?
> Art photography was then mostly black and white. A major museum showing colour
> pictures of everyday scenes helped make colour a serious choice.
