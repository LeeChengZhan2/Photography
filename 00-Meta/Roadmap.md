# Roadmap — 32 Weeks

The [[Syllabus]] is *what*. This is *when*, and *how long*. Dates assume a start of
**Mon 26 Oct 2026** and about **5 hours per week**. Shift the dates freely; keep the
sequence.

> [!tip] The sequence is not arbitrary
> Exposure before light, light before composition, composition before post, post before
> video. Each module's assignments assume the previous module's control. Reordering is
> the most common way self-taught photographers stall.

- **One assignment** takes about one week's shoot and edit time (about 3 hours). In
  weeks with two or three, split that time between them.
- **One note** takes about 10–15 minutes, including its "Check yourself" questions.
  Each week's reading is planned to fit in about an hour.
- **Two editing notes come early** — [[Editing-Workflow]] in week 1 and
  [[Basic-Panel-Order]] in week 2 — because you edit from the first week. Module 4
  re-reads them. The [[LUTs]] note is read in week 20, with the video notes that use it.
- **The capstone** is one project spread over four weeks: plan, shoot, shoot again,
  finish.

## Weekly rhythm (~5 h)

| Slot | Time | What |
|---|---|---|
| **Shoot** | 2 h | The week's assignment. To brief, not to mood. |
| **Edit & cull** | 1 h | Ingest, cull, develop the keepers. |
| **Review** | 30 min | A shoot log (template: [[Shoot-Log]]): what you tried, what failed, what's next. |
| **Read** | 1 h | *After* the shoot: the week's notes, below, and their "Check yourself" questions. |
| **Look at others' work** | 30 min | One critique (template: [[Critique]]). Non-negotiable — this is the input side. |

The shoot slot is the one that gets skipped. Protect it: put it in the calendar with a
location, not just a time. If a week lists more notes than fit in an hour, read the ones
that week's assignment uses first and finish the rest the following week.

**Optional, daily (about 10 minutes):** one deliberate photo a day, usually on the
iPhone, using that week's idea. A little every day trains your eye faster than one long
session a week.

## Phase 1 — Control the machine

### Week 0 · Module 0 reading, before you start

On the weekend before 26 Oct, read [[My-Kit]], [[Camera-Menu-Baseline]], [[Camera-Care]],
[[RAW-vs-JPEG]] and [[Ingest-and-Backup-Workflow]] (about 65 minutes). Set up your
cameras, and choose your editing app (the options are in [[Resources]]). Week 1 is then
free for shooting.

> read [[Subject-and-Intent]] and [[Simplification]] early. They're short and need no

**Exit test (14 Mar):** 30 minutes of unedited footage → a finished one-minute film in
under three hours. Not a gate: nothing later depends on video, so a miss carries
forward instead of blocking Module 6.

> [!note] No ND filter?
> At 25 fps the 180° rule asks for 1/50 s. In daylight that's several stops too much
> light for the iPhone and the DJIs, whose apertures are fixed. On the L10, even its
> smallest aperture, f/16, lets in about a stop too much in full sun at base ISO, and
> f/16 is deep into diffraction. Without ND filters, shoot A5.1 at dusk or indoors.
> For the rest, film in low light or accept a faster shutter in daylight and note what
> it does to the motion.

## After week 32 — from good to expert

Thirty-two weeks gives you strong foundations. You control the camera, read light,
compose on purpose, finish files, cut a short film, and know how a body of work is
built. That makes you a capable photographer — not yet an expert. Expertise comes from
years of volume, depth and outside feedback. A realistic path:

| Stage | Roughly | Focus | You're there when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Foundations | Weeks 1–32 | This syllabus | Your capstone series is finished |
| Depth | Year 2 | One genre, one long project, the advanced skills that genre needs | People understand a 20–30 image project of yours without you explaining it |
| Mastery | Year 3 onward | Exhibiting, publishing, teaching, your own voice | People recognise your work and ask you for critique |

**In year 2:**

- **Choose one genre** from Module 6 and stay with it for six months. Re-read its note,
  and the concept notes it links to, every few weeks.
- **Start one long project:** a single subject, photographed for six to twelve months.
  Plan it with [[Shoot-Planning]]; edit it with [[Editing-a-Sequence]].
- **Learn the advanced skills your genre needs.** For example: two- and three-light
  setups for portraits (the next step after [[Off-Camera-Flash]]), focus stacking and
  filters for landscape ([[Photo-Merges]], [[Lens-Filters]]), or sound and story for
  documentary video ([[Sound-Mixing]], [[Story-Structure]]).
- **Get regular critique** from someone better than you — a club, a mentor, a critique
  group. → [[Sharing-and-Feedback]]
- **Rebuild your portfolio every six months** ([[Portfolio-Construction]]), and each
  year enter one open call or make one small zine or photobook.

The habits that matter most never change: shoot every week, write the shoot log, look
hard at other people's work, and go back to the notes when something fails.

## Variants

**Fast track (16 weeks, ~10 h/week).** Halve every module, keep every gate, drop
Module 6 to one genre. Do not compress Module 1 — double the shooting instead.

**Slow burn (~60 weeks, ~2–3 h/week).** One assignment per week rather than per
module-week. Gates stay in the same order; dates roughly double.

**Trip-driven.** If a trip lands mid-syllabus, pull the relevant genre note forward
from Module 6 and treat the trip as that module's ten-frame set. Then return to
sequence. Don't try to learn exposure *and* a new place at the same time.

---

## When you fall behind

You will. The failure mode isn't slipping a week — it's abandoning the sequence
because you slipped. Two rules:

1. **Never skip a shoot slot to catch up on reading.** Notes without frames are
   trivia. Frames without notes still teach you something.
2. **Slip the dates, not the gates.** Move the whole calendar right. The gates exist
   because the later modules genuinely don't work without the earlier ones.

Log the slip in the week's shoot log with the reason. After eight weeks you'll see
the real pattern — usually a time of day or a location that never works — and can
design around it.
