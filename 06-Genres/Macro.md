# Macro

> [!summary] In one line
> Photographs of small things, close up, often near life-size. Getting close is the easy
> part; the hard part is that depth of field shrinks to millimetres, and light, wind and
> your own shadow fight you.

## What makes a good one

- **It shows what the eye misses:** the texture of a leaf, the eyes of a fly, a drop of
  water holding a reflection.
- **The thin sharp zone is placed on purpose,** on the insect's eye or the edge of a
  petal, not wherever it happened to land.
- **A clean background:** smooth blur, with no bright spots or clutter.
- **Soft, even light** that shows texture without harsh glare.

## The hard part

### Magnification

**Magnification** is the size of the subject on the sensor divided by its real size.
At 1:1 (1×, "life-size"), a 10 mm beetle covers 10 mm of the sensor. At 1:2 (0.5×) it
covers 5 mm. "True macro" usually means 1:1 or closer; anything less is close-up work.

The L10's 4:3 frame is about 15.6 mm wide on the sensor (5200 pixels × about 3 µm). So:

| Magnification | Real width the L10 frame shows | Depth of field at f/5.6 |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1× | about 16 cm | about 2 cm |
| 0.25× | about 6 cm | about 3 mm |
| 0.5× | about 3.1 cm | about 1 mm |
| 1× | about 1.6 cm | about 0.3 mm |

Measure yours: photograph a ruler at the closest focus, and read how many millimetres
fill the frame width. 31 mm across means 0.5×.

### Working distance

**Working distance** is the gap between the front of the lens and the subject. When it
is tiny, the lens blocks your light, insects flee, and the lens can touch the subject.

- The L10 in AF Macro focuses down to 3 cm at 24 mm, but only to 30 cm at 75 mm. The
  75 mm setting gives you about ten times the room, which matters for anything alive.
  Measure the magnification at both settings and compare.
- The iPhone's macro focuses at about 2 cm, on the ultra-wide. That close, the phone's
  own shadow falls on the subject, so let the light come from the side.

### Depth of field collapses

At macro distances, depth of field depends mainly on magnification and aperture. It
falls roughly with the square of magnification: double the magnification and the sharp
zone shrinks to between a quarter and a third, as the table shows. → [[Depth-of-Field]]

Stopping down helps less than you'd hope. On the L10, diffraction softens everything
from about f/5.6–f/8. At close focus the lens also behaves like a smaller aperture than
the number shows, so diffraction arrives sooner. Instead:

- **Turn the subject's main plane parallel to the sensor.** Shoot a butterfly's wings
  side-on, or a flower face-on, so more of it falls inside the thin slice.
- **Choose what must be sharp,** usually the eye, and let the rest go.
- **Focus by moving.** At this scale, rocking your body forward and back is faster than
  turning a focus ring. Use MF and take several frames as you sway. → [[Manual-Focus]]

### Diffusion

Softness depends on the light's size compared with its distance from the subject.
→ [[Quality-of-Light]] At macro scale, small things become big lights: a 20 cm sheet of
baking paper 10 cm from a flower is enormous next to the flower, so its light is very
soft.

- Diffuse hard sun with baking paper, a white umbrella or a thin white cloth, and
  bounce light into shadows with a white card.
- Shiny subjects (beetles, water drops) reflect the light source. A big diffuser gives a
  smooth glow instead of a hot spot.
- Overcast days are free diffusion, and Singapore has many bright cloudy ones.

### Focus stacking

When one frame can't hold enough depth, **focus stack**: take several frames, moving the
focus a little each time from front to back so the sharp zones overlap. Software then
keeps the sharp part of each. → [[Photo-Merges]]

- Use a support, and a subject that can't move: a leaf, a shell, a flower indoors.
- Keep the exposure identical; change only the focus. 5–30 frames is common.
- Check the L10's manual for a focus-bracketing feature that automates the steps.
- Living insects rarely allow it. Try two or three frames, or accept one sharp plane.

## Settings to start with

- **L10, handheld:** focus selector to AF Macro (or MF), A mode, f/4–f/5.6, ISO
  200–800, 1/250 s or faster. At 0.5×, a 1 mm sway moves you out of focus, and the
  2-axis lens stabiliser can't correct forward-and-back movement.
- **L10 on a support** (still subjects and stacks): ISO 100–200, f/5.6, any shutter
  speed, and the self-timer so your touch doesn't shake it.
- **iPhone:** move in close; the camera switches to the ultra-wide for macro at about
  2 cm. Macro shots save 12 MP, and the ultra-wide has no stabilisation, so brace your
  hands. Press and hold to lock focus and exposure.
- **Pocket 3 and Action 5 Pro:** not for macro. They focus no closer than 20 cm and
  35 cm.

## Gear reality

Forums say you need a dedicated 1:1 macro lens, a twin flash and a focusing rail. To
learn, you need close focus (you have two: the L10's AF Macro and the iPhone's macro),
a steady hold, soft light, and patience.

- Measure what your devices reach and work at that scale. Much good close-up work
  happens below 1:1. → [[My-Kit]]
- Four Thirds helps: at the same framing and f-number, it gives about twice the depth
  of field of full frame.
- Free kit: baking paper, white card, a clothes peg on a stick to steady a stem without
  damaging it, and a bag of rice to rest the camera on.

## Ethics and the law

- **Don't harm the subject.** Don't chill insects in a fridge to slow them, and don't
  pin or glue them. If a subject was captive or posed, say so.
- **Move yourself, not the plant.** Don't break leaves to clear a background.
- **Parks and reserves:** don't pick flowers or damage plants, and stay on marked trails
  in nature reserves. Rules change, so check the current ones; this is not legal advice.
- **Your safety:** keep away from wasp and bee nests, and look before you kneel.

## Who to study

- **Karl Blossfeldt** — *Urformen der Kunst* (1928): enlarged black-and-white studies of
  buds, stems and seed heads on plain backgrounds. Look at the even light and the empty
  background, and how isolation turns a plant into sculpture.
- **Levon Biss** — *Microsculpture*: huge, sharp portraits of insects from a museum
  collection, each built from thousands of stacked frames and lit section by section.
  Look at how soft, even light makes shiny shells readable.
- **Rinko Kawauchi** — soft, pale pictures of small things: insects, water, light. Look
  at how shallow focus and gentle light make close-ups feel personal rather than
  scientific.

## A project you can finish in a week

**One square metre.** Choose one small patch: a park flower bed, the edge of a pond, or
the plants in your corridor. Visit it on five mornings, when the air is often calmer.
Make ten frames: three at your closest focus (write down the measured magnification),
two with and two without a baking-paper diffuser, one focus stack of a still subject,
and two of living things such as ants or a dragonfly. That's your A6.2 set.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- You move from 0.25× to 0.5× at f/5.6. What happens to the depth of field, and why?
> It falls from about 3 mm to about 1 mm. At macro distances depth of field shrinks
> roughly with the square of magnification, so doubling it leaves a third or less.

> [!question]- Why not stop down to f/16 to get a whole beetle sharp on the L10?
> Diffraction softens everything from about f/5.6–f/8, and sooner at close focus, so
> the whole frame goes soft. Focus stack instead.

> [!question]- Why is a sheet of baking paper very soft light for a flower, but not for a person?
> Softness depends on the light's size compared with its distance. Ten centimetres from
> a small flower, the sheet is huge. A metre from a person, it is small.

> [!question]- Which suits a nervous dragonfly better: the L10 at 24 mm from 3 cm, or at 75 mm from 30 cm? Why?
> 75 mm from 30 cm. The longer working distance doesn't scare it or shade it. If the
> magnification you measured there is lower, crop a little.
