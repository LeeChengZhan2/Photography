# Family and Events

> [!summary] In one line
> Photographs of the people closest to you and the days that matter to them: ordinary
> evenings, birthdays, festivals, weddings. The best pictures come from being part of
> the day without taking it over, and from respecting what your family wants to share.

## What makes a good one

- **Real moments over posed smiles:** your grandmother laughing, a child asleep in the
  car, faces lit and eyes sharp. These are the frames you'll treasure in twenty years.
- **Everyday life, not just occasions.** Meals, homework, cooking, the flat as it looks
  now. Rooms and routines change, and nobody photographs them. → [[Subject-and-Intent]]
- **A story, not a pile:** a set that shows the day from start to finish.
- **Pictures that get printed and seen.**

## The hard part

### Being unobtrusive

The camera changes behaviour: people stop what they're doing and pose. The fix is time.
Keep the camera out and use it often, and after a while people stop noticing it.

- **Small and quiet:** the iPhone, or the L10's silent electronic shutter. If LED
  lights show stripes, switch to the mechanical shutter.
- **No flash in faces.** If you must add light, bounce it off a ceiling or wall.
  → [[Bounce-Flash]]
- **Don't direct all day.** Make one group photo, then shoot from the edge, at your
  subject's eye level. Kneel for children.

### Candid and posed

Candid frames tell the truth of the day; posed frames record that everyone was there.
You need both, but most frames should be candid. For candids, anticipate: the child
before the cake, the elders before a toast, the reunion at the door. Be in position
before it happens. → [[Gesture-and-Moment]] For posed frames, be quick: two minutes,
because patience is short, especially children's.

### Group shots

- **Light:** open shade or even indoor light. Direct sun makes people squint.
- **Arrange:** tallest at the back, children and elders seated in front. If you can't
  see someone's face, the camera can't either.
- **Depth of field:** two rows need some depth. On the L10 at 35 mm and f/4, focused at
  3 m, everything from about 1.8 m to 9 m is sharp.
- **Edges stretch.** On a wide lens (the iPhone's 0.5×, or 24 mm), faces near the
  corners get stretched. Keep people away from the edges, or step back.
- **Take five to ten frames.** In a big group, someone blinks in every frame. Keep
  talking, and after the "last one", shoot one more: people relax when they think it's
  over. Swapping in a blink-free face from another frame is fine for family pictures,
  never for documentary.

### Low indoor light

Homes and restaurants are far darker than they look, often 6–10 stops darker than
daylight outside. Your eyes adapt; the camera doesn't. Work in this order:

1. **Open the aperture.** The L10 at 24 mm opens to f/1.7. Zooming to 75 mm drops you
   to f/2.8, about 1½ stops less light.
2. **Keep the shutter fast enough:** 1/125 s for children, 1/60 s for adults sitting
   still.
3. **Raise the ISO** to 3200–6400. A sharp, noisy frame beats a clean, blurred one.
   → [[ISO]]
4. **Move people to the light:** a window by day, the brightest lamp at night.

The iPhone's Night mode suits still scenes, but it blends several frames, so moving
children can blur or ghost. For birthday candles, expose for the faces lit by the
flames, shoot from across the cake at the child's eye level, and use a short burst as
they blow.

### Weddings as a guest

The hired photographer has the job; you have the guest's view. Stay out of the aisle
and out of their line of sight, and never shoot over their shoulder, because you'll
appear in their frames. No flash during the ceremony. If the couple asks for an
"unplugged" ceremony (no phones or cameras), respect it completely. Your strengths are
the moments the professional misses: relatives at your table, children, the quiet
between formal moments. Give the couple your photos, and don't post before they do.

### Telling the story of a day

The same shot list works for a birthday, a wedding or Chinese New Year: the **setting**
(the home, decorations, food laid out); **preparation** (cooking, dressing, hands at
work); **arrivals and greetings**; **people**, with a portrait of each generation; the
**main moment** (blowing out candles, lo hei at Chinese New Year, visiting relatives at
Hari Raya, lighting oil lamps at Deepavali, a wedding tea ceremony); **interactions**
(hugs, laughter, children playing); and **the quiet after** (leftovers, sleeping
children, goodbyes). → [[Story-Structure]]

### Prints and albums

Files sit unseen; prints get looked at. After each event, print a few and give them to
grandparents, and make one album or photobook a year. → [[Printing]] Back up family
photos as the irreplaceable things they are: three copies, on two kinds of storage,
one kept somewhere else. → [[Archive-Strategy]]

## Settings to start with

| Situation | Device | Settings |
|---|---|---|
| Indoors, children moving | L10 at 24 mm | A or M; f/1.7; 1/125 s; Auto ISO up to 6400; continuous AF |
| Group of ten | L10 at 35 mm | f/4; 1/125 s; Auto ISO; focus on the front row; five or more frames |
| Birthday candles | L10 at 24 mm | f/1.7; 1/60–1/125 s; ISO 3200–6400; expose for the faces |
| Quick candid | iPhone 1× or 2× | AE/AF lock on the face; Night mode only for still subjects |
| Event video | Pocket 3 | Your best low-light video camera; Follow mode for walking |
| You in the picture | L10 or Action 5 Pro | L10 on a table with the self-timer; or the Action 5 Pro's front screen |

## Gear reality

Forums say events need full frame, an f/1.4 lens, a flash and a second body. You need a
camera that's out of the bag, settings that work in dim light, and permission. The
iPhone is the camera you'll actually have; the L10's f/1.7 at 24 mm makes indoor light
manageable; the Pocket 3 is your best low-light video camera; the Action 5 Pro covers
the beach and the pool. No flash? Window light by day, lamps by night. → [[My-Kit]]

## Ethics and the law

### Consent within the family

Family photos feel private, but posting one makes it public and permanent: copies,
screenshots and search results outlive any "delete".

- **Children can't fully consent,** and they'll grow up with whatever is online about
  them. Would they be happy to see this at sixteen? Avoid bath, nappy, tantrum and
  toilet pictures. Ask older children before you post them; their "no" counts.
- **Don't publish identifying details together.** A school uniform with the school's
  name, plus your block or estate, can lead a stranger to a child.
- **Share privately.** A shared family album beats a public post. Remove location data
  from anything you do post publicly.
- **Relatives:** some elders don't want to be online, for personal or religious reasons.
  Ask, and respect it. Ask other parents before posting their children.

Laws on privacy and children's images differ by country and change; this is general
guidance, not legal advice. → [[Copyright-and-Consent]]

## Who to study

- **Rinko Kawauchi** — *Cui Cui*: thirteen years of her own family's life, including a
  birth and a death. Look at how small, quiet moments (meals, hands, light in a room)
  add up to a family story.
- **Larry Sultan** — *Pictures from Home* (1992): his parents photographed over about
  ten years, alongside old family snapshots and their own comments, including where
  they disagreed with how he showed them. Look at how he lets his subjects answer back.
- **Sally Mann** — *Immediate Family* (1992): large-format black-and-white pictures of
  her three children growing up at the family's summer place in Virginia. Look at how
  ordinary play becomes lasting and strange. Then read about the public debate the book
  caused over photographing children, and ask what your own family would agree to.

## A project you can finish in a week

**One family meal, start to finish.** Choose a family dinner or weekend lunch this
week. Tell everyone what you're doing, and that nothing goes online without their OK.
Shoot it as a story with the shot list above, from cooking to washing up, with at most
one posed group shot. Choose ten frames for your A6.2 set, then print the best three
and give them to the people in them.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Indoors at night, your L10 is at 75 mm and your photos of your nephew are blurred. What do you change first, and why?
> Zoom out to 24 mm and step closer. There the lens opens to f/1.7, about 1½ stops more
> light than f/2.8, so you can keep 1/125 s. Then raise the ISO if needed.

> [!question]- Why use f/4 rather than f/1.7 for a two-row group?
> The rows are at different distances, and f/1.7 may leave the back row soft. At 35 mm
> and f/4, focused at 3 m, the L10 keeps about 1.8–9 m sharp.

> [!question]- Your sister posts a photo of your niece in school uniform, with the school's name and your block visible. What's the risk, and what would you suggest?
> Together they tell strangers where the child is every day, and the post may never
> fully disappear. Suggest deleting it, cropping out the details, or sharing it in a
> private family album instead.
