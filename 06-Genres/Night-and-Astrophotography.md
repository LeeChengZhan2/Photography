# Night and Astrophotography

> [!summary] In one line
> Photographs made after dark: lit cities, light trails and stars. Light is scarce, so
> you trade time for it, with long exposures from a steady camera, careful manual
> focus, and planning around the moon and light pollution.

## What makes a good one

- **A balanced night city:** the sky still holds some colour, the lights keep their
  detail instead of blowing out into white blobs, and everything is sharp.
- **Stars as points,** not dashes (unless trails are the idea), above a foreground that
  says where you are.
- **Mood from what's hidden.** Night hides clutter. One lit window can carry a picture.

## The hard part

### Light pollution: Singapore's limit

A 2016 world atlas of light pollution ranked Singapore the most light-polluted country
on Earth: its sky never gets dark enough for your eyes to fully adjust. City light
scatters in the air and lights up the sky itself (sky glow), hiding all but the
brightest stars and planets. The Bortle scale rates sky darkness from 1 (truly dark)
to 9 (inner city); most of Singapore sits at the bright end.

So the genre splits in two. **Night cityscapes are your local strength:** skylines, HDB
blocks with lit windows, light trails, neon shophouses. **Real astrophotography, such
as the Milky Way, needs travel** to a dark place far from towns, in the week around a
new moon. The bright centre of the Milky Way is best placed roughly from February to
October; an astronomy app shows when it rises on your date. → [[Shoot-Planning]]

### Stars move: the 500 rule and the NPF rule

The Earth turns 15° an hour, so in a long exposure each star draws a short line. How
long can you expose before you see it?

- **The old 500 rule:** longest exposure in seconds = 500 ÷ the 35 mm-equivalent focal
  length. At 24 mm that's about 20 s. It was made for film and small prints. Modern
  sensors have tiny pixels, so a star that drifts a few pixels shows as a dash when you
  zoom in. That's why 500-rule photos often show short trails.
- **The NPF rule** adds what the 500 rule ignores: aperture and pixel size. Simple
  version: longest exposure in seconds = (35 × f-number + 30 × pixel size in µm) ÷ real
  focal length in mm. In plain words: smaller pixels, a wider aperture and a longer
  lens each make a star's drift easier to see, so each one shortens the time.

Worked example: the L10 at 24 mm (a real focal length of 10.9 mm, since the crop
factor is about 2.2×), f/1.7, with pixels about 3.0 µm wide.

(35 × 1.7 + 30 × 3.0) ÷ 10.9 = (59.5 + 90) ÷ 10.9 ≈ **14 s**

The 500 rule would say 20 s, about half as long again, and the stars would trail. At
75 mm (34 mm real) and f/2.8, the NPF answer drops to about 5 s. For stars,
stay wide.

### Focus in the dark

Autofocus fails on stars. Set the L10's focus selector to MF, magnify the live view on
a bright star or a distant light, and turn the focus until the point is smallest. Check
the manual for focus magnification. → [[Manual-Focus]] The Action 5 Pro needs none of
this: its focus is fixed from 35 cm to infinity, so stars are always sharp.

### Noise and stacking

Short exposures at high ISO are noisy. **Stacking** helps: take 10–20 identical frames,
then align and average them. Random noise partly cancels out while the real signal adds
up. Averaging *N* frames improves the signal-to-noise ratio by √*N*: four frames make it
twice as clean, like gathering two stops more light; sixteen make it four times as
clean (four stops). Stars move between frames, so the software must align sky and
ground separately; free tools such as Sequator (Windows) do this. → [[Noise-Reduction]]

### Star trails and light painting

For trails, don't make one hour-long exposure: sky glow keeps building and a city sky
turns flat orange-grey. Shoot many 20–30 s frames back to back and blend them with a
"lighten" stack, which keeps the brightest version of each pixel. Free tools such as
StarStaX do this. The L10's **Live View Composite** does it in camera: it adds only new
bright areas, so moving stars, planes and car lights build up while the scene stays as
bright as one short frame.

Near the equator, stars rise and set almost vertically. Face east or west and trails
are near-straight lines climbing out of the horizon. Face north or south for arcs
around the sky's poles, which sit right on the horizon here.

**Light painting:** during a long exposure, move a torch or phone light through the
frame, or sweep it over an object. The camera records only where the light shines; you
stay invisible if you keep moving and never point the light at the lens. Live View
Composite suits it, because the background doesn't over-expose while you paint.

## Settings to start with

| Scene | Device | Starting settings |
|---|---|---|
| Blue-hour skyline | L10 on a support | A or M, f/5.6, ISO 100, about 2–15 s, MF on the buildings |
| Light trails | L10 on a support | M, f/8, ISO 100, 10–30 s, or Live View Composite |
| City, handheld | iPhone | Night mode; hold still while it works |
| Stars, dark site | L10 on a support | M, 24 mm, f/1.7, ISO 3200, 14 s, MF on a bright star |
| Stars or trails | Action 5 Pro, mounted | PRO mode, 20–30 s, ISO 1600–3200; check at 100% |
| Night video | Pocket 3 | Your best low-light video camera |

Blue hour starts soon after sunset and lasts only about 20 minutes here, so be set up
before the sun goes down. Shoot RAW, and use the self-timer (check the manual) so
pressing the button doesn't shake the frame.

## Gear reality

Forums say astrophotography needs a full-frame camera, an f/1.4 lens and a star
tracker. For Singapore's nights, what you need most is something steady.

- **A tripod helps most.** Workarounds: a wall, a railing, a bag of rice. On a
  footbridge, traffic shakes the deck; wait for a gap and shoot several frames.
- **The L10** is fast at f/1.7 and 24 mm, gives exposures up to 60 s, and has Live View
  Composite. Four Thirds is noisier than full frame; stacking closes much of the gap.
  → [[My-Kit]]
- **The iPhone's** Night mode combines frames for a clean handheld city shot, but it
  chooses the exposure, and manual apps stop at about 1 s: too short for stars. The
  Pocket 3 also stops at 1 s for stills.
- **The Action 5 Pro** reaches 30 s and ISO 25600 in PRO mode. Stills have no
  stabilisation, so mount it, and expect more noise from its smaller sensor.

## Ethics and the law

- **Safety first.** Never stand in a road for light trails, and keep tripod legs out of
  walkways. When light painting, never shine lights at drivers, into homes, or at
  animals.
- **Privacy:** a long lens on lit HDB windows can show people at home. Treat windows
  as patterns, never the people inside.
- **Access:** parks and nature reserves have opening hours and rules, and dark sites
  abroad may need permits. Laws change; this is not legal advice.
- **Composites:** a Milky Way from a dark site over Singapore's skyline is an art
  choice. Label it as a composite; don't present it as one moment.

## Who to study

- **Todd Hido** — *House Hunting*: suburban houses at night, lit only by their windows
  and streetlights. Look at how one lit window carries the story, and how the mood comes
  from available light alone. Try the same with HDB blocks.
- **Hiroshi Sugimoto** — *Theaters*: each picture is one exposure lasting a whole film,
  so the screen burns to pure white and lights the room. Look at how time, not one
  instant, becomes the subject: the idea behind Live View Composite.
- **Thierry Cohen** — *Villes éteintes* (*Darkened Cities*): big cities combined with
  starry skies photographed in dark places at the same latitude. Look at what city
  light hides, and notice that the work is openly a composite.

## A project you can finish in a week

**One place, three nights.** Choose a spot with a view and traffic: a footbridge over a
main road, a field facing an HDB block, the Marina Bay waterfront. Night 1: blue hour,
balancing sky against lights. Night 2: light trails, with 20–30 s frames or Live View
Composite. Night 3: light painting, or a star test at the NPF time to see what
Singapore's sky allows. Ten frames make your A6.2 set. Note every setting as you go;
at night, memory fails.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- The 500 rule says 20 s at 24 mm, but your stars are short dashes at 100%. Why?
> The 500 rule ignores pixel size and aperture, and the L10's small pixels show even
> tiny drift. The NPF rule gives about 14 s at 24 mm and f/1.7.

> [!question]- You stack 16 frames of the same sky. How much cleaner is the result, and why?
> About four times as clean, like gathering four stops more light. Noise is random, so
> averaging partly cancels it, while the stars and sky add up. The gain grows with the
> square root of the frame count.

> [!question]- Why does Live View Composite suit Singapore's sky better than one 10-minute exposure?
> In one long exposure, sky glow and lit buildings keep brightening until they blow
> out. Live View Composite adds only new bright areas, so the scene stays at one short
> frame's exposure while the moving lights build up.

> [!question]- Facing east from Singapore, why do star trails look like near-straight lines?
> At about 1° north, stars rise almost vertically out of the eastern horizon. The sky's
> poles sit on the north and south horizons, so you only see arcs facing those ways.
