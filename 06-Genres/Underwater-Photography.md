# Underwater Photography

> [!summary] In one line
> Photographs taken in and under water: pools, beaches, reefs. Water steals colour,
> contrast and light with every metre, so the craft is staying shallow, close and
> steady, near the sun, while keeping yourself and the camera safe.

## What makes a good one

- **A close subject.** Less water in between means more colour, contrast and sharpness.
- **Light you can see:** sun rays, and the moving net of light (caustics) on sand or
  pool tiles; or a swimmer seen from below, dark against the bright surface.
- **Colour that looks true, or chosen,** not a flat blue-green cast over everything.
- **Two worlds at once** in an over-under (split) frame.

## The hard part

### Colour loss with depth

Water absorbs light, and it absorbs red first. Red fades within the first few metres
and is mostly gone by about 5 m; orange follows by about 8 m, and yellow by 10–15 m.
Deeper still, almost everything looks blue-green. What counts is the **total path
through water**: from the surface down to the subject, then from the subject to the
camera. A red fish 3 m down and 2 m away has about 5 m of water on its light's path, so
it already looks brown-grey. So:

1. **Shoot shallow.** The top one to three metres hold the most colour.
2. **Get close,** under a metre when you can.
3. **Correct white balance in editing.** The Action 5 Pro's raw DNG helps, but you can't
   restore red that never reached the sensor; pushing it hard brings noise. Divers use
   underwater lights to put red back; without one, closeness is your tool.
   → [[White-Balance]]

### Clarity, and shooting near the sun

Particles in the water scatter light like haze in air, so contrast and sharpness fall
with distance. Singapore's sea is usually murky; clear pools, and clearer water on
trips abroad, give far better results. The 155° lens lets you fill the frame from very
close, but it focuses only from about 35 cm in air. Under water, things look about a
quarter closer, and a third bigger, through the flat front glass, so keep subjects
about 50 cm away.

Underwater, unlike on land, the middle of the day is often best. When the sun is high,
less light bounces off the surface, more enters the water, and it takes a shorter path
down. In Singapore that means roughly 10:00–16:00. Keep the sun behind you for colour,
or shoot up towards it for silhouettes and sun rays. Calm, sunny water gives rays and
caustics; choppy water and cloud give flat light.

### Over-under (split) shots

Half the lens above the water, half below: a swimmer's legs underwater, the sky above.

- **Exposure:** the sky is far brighter than the water. Shoot with the sun high, in
  shallow water over a pale floor, which brightens the underwater half. Expose for that
  half and let the sky go bright.
- **The waterline** must cross the small lens cleanly. Find calm water, hold still, and
  shoot many frames, or record video and pick one.
- **Drops on the lens** blur the top half. Dip the camera fully, then lift it to the
  line just before you shoot, and check the lens often.
- **The halves won't match in scale.** With most small cameras, things underwater look
  larger and closer than in air, because water bends light at the front glass.

### Staying steady in water

The Action 5 Pro stabilises only video; for stills, water moves you all the time.

- **Use a fast shutter,** 1/250 s or faster in bright, shallow water. Hold the camera
  with both hands, elbows tucked in, and kick slowly. Hold the pool edge where allowed;
  never hold or stand on coral.
- **Snorkelling or in a pool,** breathe out slowly before the shot: you sink a little
  and steady. If you scuba dive, never hold your breath; follow your training.
- **Or shoot video,** which is stabilised, and take a still from the best moment.

### Pools and beaches

Pools are the best classroom: clear, shallow water, sunlit floors, tiles for straight
lines, friends as subjects. Ask about the pool's rules first. Singapore's beaches are
usually too murky for clear underwater frames, but good for waterline and splash shots,
and for waves seen from water level. Clear water on holidays gives coral and fish;
still shoot shallow and close.

### Rinse after salt water

- **After the sea or a chlorinated pool,** rinse the Action 5 Pro in fresh water, and
  let it dry before you open any cover. Salt or sand left in a seal lets water in.
- **Before each swim,** check every cover is fully closed and free of sand and hair.
  It is waterproof to 20 m on its own, and to 60 m in its case.
- **Keep the others dry.** The L10 isn't splash-resistant and the Pocket 3 has no
  rating. The iPhone's IP68 rating protects it against accidents; underwater work is
  the Action 5 Pro's job. → [[Camera-Care]]

### Safety first

The camera is never a reason to break a safety rule.

- **Never swim or dive alone,** and stay within your ability. Watch for currents, boats
  and jellyfish, and obey lifeguards and flags.
- **Never hyperventilate before a breath-hold dive,** and never practise breath-holding
  alone. Both can cause a blackout without warning.
- **Scuba divers:** never hold your breath, never chase a shot into a fast ascent or
  past your depth limit, and watch your air.

## Settings to start with

- **Action 5 Pro, stills:** PRO mode, 1/250–1/500 s, ISO 100–800 in bright shallow
  water, raw DNG. Wide or Ultra Wide for close subjects; Standard (Dewarp) for people
  and straight lines.
- **Action 5 Pro, video:** stabilised. Use 10-bit D-Log M if you will grade it. If the
  camera lets you fix the white balance, do it, so the clips match.
  → [[Video-Stabilisation]]
- **L10 and iPhone:** stay on the dry side, for the beach and poolside.

## Gear reality

Forums say you need a housing, strobes, a dome port and a dive certificate. To start,
you need the Action 5 Pro in a pool or shallow sunlit sea, and the skill to stay still.
→ [[My-Kit]]

What it can't do: put red back (it has no light), focus closer than about 50 cm under
water, or steady its stills. Its small sensor gets noisy when the water is deep or dim.
A wrist strap or tether, if you have one, stops it sinking or floating away.

## Ethics and the law

- **Pools and beaches:** many pools restrict cameras to protect swimmers' privacy, so
  ask first. People in swimwear have even more reason to object: don't photograph
  strangers, and never photograph other people's children without their parents' OK.
- **Marine life:** don't touch, chase, feed or move animals. Don't stand on coral or
  collect shells. Follow marine park rules, which differ by place and change; this is
  general guidance, not legal advice.
- **Honesty:** colour correction is normal. Adding a fish isn't.

## Who to study

- **David Doubilet** — decades of underwater work for *National Geographic*, known for
  his over-under frames. Look at how he balances the bright world above with the dim
  world below in one picture.
- **Brian Skerry** — marine wildlife for *National Geographic*: sharks, whales, seals.
  Look at how close he gets with wide lenses, and how his pictures tell conservation
  stories.
- **Laurent Ballesta** — French underwater photographer and biologist. His Gombessa
  expeditions include *700 Sharks*, about grey reef sharks hunting at night. Look at how
  planning, science and patience make pictures no one has seen before.

## A project you can finish in a week

**One pool, three sessions.** Find a pool where cameras are allowed, and a friend or
relative to swim for you. Session 1, around midday: stills of the swimmer, close and
shallow, sun behind you; compare 1/125, 1/250 and 1/500 s. Session 2: over-unders at
the pool edge in calm water. Session 3: video of a swim towards the camera; pull stills
from it. Ten colour-corrected frames, including two over-unders and one silhouette
against the surface, make your A6.2 set. Rinse the camera after each session.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- A red swimsuit 4 m down looks brown-grey. Why, and what are your two best fixes without lights?
> Water absorbs red first, and the light has travelled through the depth plus your
> distance from the swimmer. Go shallower and get closer, then correct the white balance
> from raw, knowing it can't fully restore red that never arrived.

> [!question]- Why is midday often best underwater, when it's the worst time on land?
> A high sun bounces less light off the surface, so more enters the water, and it takes
> the shortest path down. On land, the same overhead sun gives harsh, flat light.

> [!question]- Your over-under has a sharp bottom half and a blurry top. What happened?
> Water drops on the lens above the waterline. Dip the camera fully, lift it to the line
> just before you shoot, and check the lens between attempts.

> [!question]- Why are your underwater stills soft when video from the same swim looks smooth?
> The Action 5 Pro stabilises only video. Stills need a fast shutter and a steady hold,
> or you can take stills from the stabilised video.
