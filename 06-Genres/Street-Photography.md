# Street Photography

> [!summary] In one line
> Candid photographs of everyday life in public places. The skill is seeing a picture a
> moment before it happens, being ready when it does, and treating strangers fairly.

## What makes a good one

A competent street photo shows that people were in a street. A good one catches
something you could never arrange: a gesture, a coincidence, a moment when light, place
and people line up for a fraction of a second. → [[Gesture-and-Moment]]

- **One clear moment.** A raised hand, a glance, a stride caught mid-step. A busy street
  is not yet a photograph.
- **A strong stage.** Many great street photos are a good background first: a shaft of
  light, a coloured wall, a doorway. The person completes the picture.
- **Layers.** Something readable near, in the middle and far away. This is what makes
  people look twice. → [[Layers-and-Depth]]
- **Something unexpected,** such as a face on a poster above a real face, two things
  that don't belong together side by side. → [[Juxtaposition]]

## The hard part

### Anticipation

Most "lucky" street photos are not luck. The photographer found a stage and waited for
someone to walk onto it. You have to predict, because reacting to something you see
takes about a quarter of a second. A person walking at about 1.4 m/s moves 30–35 cm in
that time: most of a stride. Press as the moment *builds*, not when it arrives.

- **People follow the shade.** In Singapore's midday sun, people keep to covered
  walkways. A patch of sun crossing a five-foot way (the covered walkway in front of
  shophouses) is a spot everyone must pass through.
- **Streets repeat.** The bus comes every few minutes; the crossing turns green on a
  cycle. Someone reaching for a phone will look down next. Learn the rhythm.

### Layers

A layered frame has something happening in the foreground, midground and background,
each readable on its own. Every layer must be right at the same instant: if each of
three layers is "right" one moment in five, all three line up about one moment in 125.
So layered photographers stay in one place and shoot a lot.

Let the foreground be big and partly cut off by the frame edge; it shows depth. Watch
for merges, such as a pole growing out of a head. A step sideways separates them.

### Zone focusing

Autofocus can hunt, or pick the wrong person. Zone focusing means setting focus to a
fixed distance in advance and letting depth of field cover a range in front of and
behind it. You never wait for the camera. → [[Hyperfocal-Distance]]

On the L10, use back-button focus: set [Shutter AF] to off, press [AF ON] once on
something at the right distance (a pavement tile 3 m away), then let go. Focus stays
there until you press [AF ON] again. → [[Back-Button-Focus]]

| L10 zoom (equiv.) | Aperture | Focused at | Roughly sharp from |
|---|---|---|---|
| 24 mm | f/5.6 | 2 m | 0.9 m to infinity |
| 35 mm | f/5.6 | 3 m | 1.6 m to about 40 m |
| 35 mm | f/8 | 3 m | 1.3 m to infinity |
| 75 mm | f/5.6 | 3 m | 2.5 m to 3.8 m |

These use the usual "sharp in a normal print" standard; at 100% the zone is narrower.

## Settings to start with

**L10 (main camera):**

- **Mode and aperture:** A at f/5.6 with Auto ISO, capped at your ceiling from A1.3.
  f/5.6 is deep enough for zone focusing and stops before diffraction softens the L10's
  files (from about f/5.6–f/8).
- **Shutter:** 1/250 s for walking people, 1/500 s for children and cyclists. If A mode
  picks slower, switch to M with Auto ISO and set the shutter yourself.
- **Focus:** zone focus at 2–3 m, set once with [AF ON].
- **Zoom:** 24 or 35 mm, and stay there, as in A3.2's prime jail. When you know the
  frame by heart, you can compose before you raise the camera.
- **Shutter type:** the electronic shutter is silent, but it's a rolling shutter: fast
  movement leans and LED signs can band. Use the mechanical one for fast movement.
- **Viewpoint:** flip the vari-angle screen up and shoot from the waist. People react
  less, and the low viewpoint changes the picture.

**iPhone (always with you):** 1× (24 mm). Press and hold on the pavement 2–3 m ahead to
lock focus and exposure (AE/AF LOCK). That is zone focusing on a phone, and its small
sensor already gives deep focus.

## Gear reality

Forums say you need a small rangefinder, a fast prime lens and silent autofocus. You
need a camera you actually carry, that people don't fear, and that is ready before the
moment arrives.

- The L10 is close to ideal: small, silent with the electronic shutter, nothing to
  change. Its deep depth of field, which frustrates portrait photographers, makes zone
  focusing forgiving. → [[My-Kit]]
- The iPhone is the least noticed camera on any street, because everyone holds one.
- An Action 5 Pro worn on the chest records people who can't tell they're being
  photographed. That is covert shooting, and it fails the fairness test below.
- Stepping out of an air-conditioned mall, the lens fogs. Let the camera warm up in a
  closed bag for a few minutes first.

## Ethics and the law

**The law (general guidance, not legal advice).** In Singapore, as in many countries,
photographing people in public places is not in itself generally illegal. Other laws
still apply:

- Harassment laws cover following someone or photographing them to cause distress.
- Photographing under clothing, or in toilets or changing rooms, is a serious crime.
- Some sites, such as military camps and some government and utility buildings, are
  protected under the Infrastructure Protection Act. A "No Unauthorised Photography"
  sign marks them, and photographing them, even from outside, needs the owner's
  permission. Malls and condos set their own rules. Obey the signs, and if staff ask
  you to stop, stop.
- Using a recognisable person in an advert usually needs their signed permission.
  Personal and editorial use is treated differently.

Laws differ by country and change over time. → [[Copyright-and-Consent]]

**Fairness, which asks more than the law.** Would you mind being photographed like
this? Would they mind seeing it online? Don't make fun of people, and don't turn the
homeless, the ill or the distressed into scenery. Take extra care with children. Shoot
openly; if someone objects, show them the frame and delete it if they ask. Most harm
happens at publishing, not shooting: a frame in your archive is very different from
one shared with a mocking caption.

## Who to study

Start from [[Photographers-to-Study]], and do A6.1 first: 20 images in critique notes
before you shoot.

- **Henri Cartier-Bresson** — *Hyères, France* (1932): a spiral staircase seen from
  above, with a cyclist blurred as he passes below. Look at how the geometry is complete
  before the person arrives. He chose the stage, then waited.
- **Alex Webb** — dense colour from places such as Haiti and the Mexico–US border.
  Look at how each layer holds its own small event, and how deep shadow in hard
  tropical light hides the clutter.
- **Nguan** — Singapore in soft pastel light: HDB estates, the city, lone figures. Look
  at how one consistent palette makes ordinary Singapore feel dreamlike, and how small
  the people often are in the frame.

## A project you can finish in a week

**One stage, five visits.** Choose one spot with good light and a reason for people to
pass: a sunlit gap in a five-foot way, a covered walkway at an MRT exit, a hawker
centre entrance. Go five times at the same time of day, for example 17:00–18:00, when
the sun is low. Shoot only there, zone-focused, and wait for people to enter the frame
you built. After each visit, note what repeats: when the light arrives, who passes,
where they pause. Choose ten frames that show the place, its light, and the people who
move through it.

This can be your A6.2 set. For A6.3, shoot one visit on the iPhone's 120 mm from across
the street and compare how the distance changes the pictures.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- On the L10 at 35 mm and f/5.6, focused at 3 m, someone steps in 1 m away. Sharp or soft, and what would you change?
> Soft. The sharp zone runs from about 1.6 m to 40 m, so 1 m is outside it. Zoom out to
> 24 mm and focus at 2 m: the zone then runs from about 0.9 m to infinity.

> [!question]- Why press the shutter before the moment you want, rather than when you see it?
> Reacting takes about a quarter of a second. A walking person moves 30–35 cm in that
> time, so by the time you react, the gesture has changed. You have to predict it.

> [!question]- Why do many street photographers choose the background first and then wait?
> The background and light don't move, so you can compose them with care. People are
> the only variable. Waiting turns a lucky accident into something you can repeat.

> [!question]- A stranger asks you to delete a photo you took legally. What do you do, and why?
> Delete it. Legal doesn't mean fair, and street photography depends on people's
> goodwill. One frame is not worth someone's distress.
