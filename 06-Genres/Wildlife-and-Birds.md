# Wildlife and Birds

> [!summary] In one line
> Photographs of wild animals behaving naturally. Long lenses help, but the real tools
> are fieldcraft (knowing the animal and getting close without disturbing it) and the
> patience to wait.

## What makes a good one

- **Natural behaviour:** feeding, preening, hunting, playing. A bird sitting still on a
  branch is a record; a bird doing something is a photograph.
- **A sharp eye** with a catchlight, ideally shot from the animal's eye level.
- **A clean background, or a meaningful habitat.** A heron in a concrete canal under HDB
  blocks tells a Singapore story a frame-filling portrait can't.
- **An undisturbed animal.** If it is staring at you, it is reacting to you, and the
  picture shows that.

Singapore's parks, park connectors and nature reserves hold more than people expect:
herons and kingfishers at ponds and canals, monitor lizards, otters in city waterways,
and migratory shorebirds at wetland reserves during the northern winter.

## The hard part

### Reach

How big an animal looks depends on focal length and distance. Roughly: frame width ≈
distance × 35 ÷ equivalent focal length. For an animal 10 m away:

| Lens (equiv.) | Frame width at 10 m | A 25 cm kingfisher fills |
|---|---|---|
| L10 at 75 mm | about 4.7 m | about 1/20 of the width |
| iPhone 5× (120 mm) | about 2.9 m | about 1/12 |
| A birder's 600 mm | about 0.6 m | nearly half |

Your longest lens is the iPhone's 120 mm. To match a 600 mm frame you would need to be
five times closer: 2 m instead of 10 m. Most wild birds fly long before that. Cropping
is limited too: the 5× saves 12 MP, so cropping to half the width leaves 3 MP, and
beyond 5× the phone only zooms digitally.

So don't fight the gap. Work with it:

1. **Show the animal in its habitat.** Small in the frame, but in a place that explains
   its life.
2. **Choose tolerant animals.** Herons at park ponds, monitor lizards, otters, mynas and
   squirrels are used to people and let you closer, without you chasing them.
3. **Let animals come to you.** Sit by a flowering tree or the edge of a pond, and stay.

### Fieldcraft

- **Learn the animal:** when it feeds, where it perches, what it does next. Birds are
  most active in the early morning, from about 7:00; midday heat quietens them.
- **Move slowly and quietly.** Approach at an angle, not straight on. Stop whenever it
  looks at you, and wait until it goes back to what it was doing.
- **Read the warning signs:** head up and fixed on you, feeding stopped, body crouched,
  or alarm calls. You are too close. Step back.
- **Sit and wait.** Twenty minutes still in one place, and animals return to normal.
- **Wear dull colours,** and use cover such as trees and pillars.

### AF tracking

A moving animal needs **continuous AF**, where the camera keeps refocusing as the
distance changes, with an area mode that follows the subject. → [[Autofocus-Modes]]

- Use back-button focus: hold [AF ON] to track, release it to hold focus while the
  animal pauses. → [[Back-Button-Focus]]
- Through branches, AF grabs the twig. Switch to a single small AF point, or fine-tune
  in MF.
- Check the L10's manual for its tracking and subject-detection options. Practise on
  pigeons before the kingfisher arrives.

## Settings to start with

- **L10 at 75 mm:** S mode, or M with Auto ISO. 1/500 s for perched birds (their heads
  move constantly), 1/1000–1/2000 s for flight or playing otters. f/2.8, the widest at
  75 mm. Continuous AF with [AF ON], short bursts, RAW. Use the mechanical shutter: the
  electronic one is a rolling shutter and bends fast wingbeats.
- **iPhone 5× (120 mm, f/2.8):** the Camera app picks the shutter itself. To set
  1/500 s or faster, use Halide or Lightroom, and raise the ISO to match.
  → [[Shutter-Speed]]
- **Action 5 Pro as a remote camera:** leave it recording on a low support near a spot
  animals visit, such as a log by a pond, and walk away. Its focus is fixed from 35 cm,
  and the 155° view shows the habitat. Never place it near a nest, and check park rules
  before leaving equipment.

## Gear reality

Forums say you need a 600 mm lens and a full-frame camera. In this genre, reach really
does matter, and it is the one genre where your kit is genuinely short. → [[My-Kit]]
Don't let that stop you; change the kind of picture you make. Habitat pictures, tolerant
urban animals, patient sitting, and a remote Action 5 Pro all work with what you own.

Big lenses don't fix ethics either: many disturbance problems come from people with
long lenses crowding nests.

The L10 isn't splash-resistant. In the rain, use a bag or wait it out, and store gear in
a dry box afterwards.

## Ethics and the law

- **The animal comes first.** If your presence changes its behaviour, you are too close.
- **No baiting, no recorded calls.** Playing a bird's call makes it think a rival is
  near; it stresses the bird and can pull it away from its nest or young.
- **Don't flush birds** to get flight shots, and stay away from nests. Disturbed parents
  may abandon them, and cutting branches for a clear view exposes chicks to predators
  and heat.
- **In Singapore, feeding wild animals is against the law,** and nature reserves ask you
  to stay on marked trails. Rules change, so check NParks' current rules; this is not
  legal advice.
- **Label captive animals.** A picture from a zoo or bird park must say so. Passing it
  off as wild is dishonest, and contests disqualify it.
- **Don't share exact locations** of rare or nesting animals online; crowds follow.
- **Keep your distance** from monitor lizards and crocodiles at wetlands. Near monkeys,
  don't carry food openly.

## Who to study

- **Sebastião Salgado** — *Genesis*: a long black-and-white project on wild places,
  animals and people living traditional lives. Look at how often animals appear within
  their landscape, and how tone does the work of colour.
- **Tim Laman** — a years-long project on the birds-of-paradise of New Guinea, made with
  an ornithologist. Look at how much of each picture is fieldcraft: knowing the display
  perch, building hides, climbing into the canopy, and waiting.
- **Robert Zhao Renhui** — Singaporean; projects on animals and nature that mix fact and
  fiction. Look at how he questions what a nature photograph proves, and at his
  attention to Singapore's man-made, in-between nature.

## A project you can finish in a week

**One pond, five mornings.** Choose a pond or canal in a park. Arrive at about 7:15
each morning for five days. Sit in the same place for 20 minutes before you shoot.
Photograph whatever lives there with the iPhone's 5× and the L10 at 75 mm, and leave the
Action 5 Pro recording near the water's edge, well away from any nest. Keep a log of
what arrives and when. Ten frames, half close portraits of tolerant animals and half
"animal in its habitat", make your A6.2 set.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- With the iPhone at 120 mm, how close must you be to fill the frame like a birder's 600 mm lens at 10 m, and why is that a problem?
> About 2 m, five times closer. Most wild birds fly off before you get there, and
> chasing them is disturbance. So choose tolerant animals, or show the animal in its
> habitat.

> [!question]- A bird stops feeding and stares at you. What does that mean, and what do you do?
> It is alert to you: you are too close. Stop, back off slowly, and wait until it goes
> back to feeding.

> [!question]- Why use continuous AF with back-button focus for a heron that stalks, then strikes?
> Continuous AF keeps refocusing as the distance changes. Back-button focus lets you
> track with [AF ON] and fire at the strike without the shutter button refocusing.

> [!question]- Your best kingfisher frame was taken at a bird park. How do you caption it, and why?
> Say it was taken at a bird park, with a captive bird. Calling it wild would mislead
> viewers, and in a contest it would be disqualified.
