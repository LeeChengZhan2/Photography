# Sports and Action

> [!summary] In one line
> Photographs of people moving fast, caught at the moment that matters. Most of the work
> happens before the action: knowing where it will happen, standing in the right place,
> and having focus and shutter speed ready.

## What makes a good one

- **Peak action:** the top of a jump, the ball leaving the foot, the racket at contact.
- **Emotion:** the celebration, the effort, the frustration on a face.
- **The ball in the frame,** or whatever the game is played with, against a background
  that doesn't fight the athlete. Without the ball, many sports pictures don't read.
- **Sharp where it matters, or blurred on purpose,** as in a pan. → [[Shutter-Speed]]

## The hard part

### Reading the game to pre-position

Action happens in predictable places: the goalmouth, the net, the finish line, the
hurdle, the bend of a track. Learn the sport's rhythm, then choose a position where:

1. **The action comes towards you,** so you get faces, not backs.
2. **The light is behind you or to the side.**
3. **The background is plain:** dark trees or an empty wall, not a car park.

Once there, stay. Moving around costs you moments.

Direction also changes the shutter speed you need. Blur depends on how far the subject
moves *across the frame* during the exposure, not on its real speed. A runner coming
straight at you mostly grows larger, so 1/500 s is often enough. Crossing in front of
you is harder: at 5 m/s and 1/500 s, a runner moves 1 cm. With the L10 at 75 mm and
10 m away, the frame is about 5 m wide, so that is about 10 pixels of blur. At 1/2000 s
it is under 3 pixels. Arms and legs move faster than the body, so they blur first.

### Pre-focus

Autofocus can lag behind a sudden movement. **Pre-focus** on the spot where the action
will happen (the bar of a high jump, the finish line, the net), then hold that focus by
releasing [AF ON] or switching to MF. When the athlete arrives, you only press the
shutter. → [[Back-Button-Focus]]

For steady movement towards you, track instead: continuous AF, holding [AF ON].
→ [[Autofocus-Modes]] At 24–35 mm and f/4 the L10 keeps a zone of several metres
sharp, which forgives small errors.

### Burst discipline

Burst mode fires many frames per second, but the peak can still fall between them. At
10 frames per second, frames are 100 ms apart, while a ball stays on a foot or racket
for only a few milliseconds. Speed alone won't catch it; timing will. Press just before
the peak.

Shoot short bursts of three to five frames around the peak, not three-second bursts.
Long bursts fill the camera's buffer, so it stops to save at the wrong moment, and they
leave you hundreds of near-identical frames to sort. Check the manual for the L10's
burst speeds. → [[Culling-Discipline]]

### Peak action

Many movements have a peak where motion pauses: the top of a jump, the end of a
backswing, a basketball at the top of a shot. At the top of a jump, vertical speed
passes through zero. So the peak is both the easiest moment to freeze and the most
expressive. Learn each sport's peaks, and press half a beat early.

### Point of view: the Action 5 Pro

The Action 5 Pro shows what the athlete sees. Mount it on a helmet, handlebars, a chest
strap, the goal net, or the ground at a take-off spot. Its 155° view puts the viewer
inside the action.

- **No focus lag at all:** focus is fixed from 35 cm to infinity.
- **Stills are not stabilised.** In PRO mode set a fast shutter (1/1000 s or faster),
  or record video, which is stabilised, and pull frames from it.
- **Standard (Dewarp)** removes the fisheye bend when straight lines matter; Ultra Wide
  keeps it for an immersive look.

## Settings to start with

| Situation | Device | Settings |
|---|---|---|
| Outdoor football, runners | L10 at 75 mm | S, or M with Auto ISO; 1/1000 s, f/2.8, continuous AF with [AF ON] |
| Indoor badminton, basketball | L10 | M with Auto ISO; 1/500–1/1000 s, widest aperture, ISO 3200–6400 |
| Panning a cyclist | L10 | S mode, 1/30–1/60 s; follow the rider through the shot |
| Point of view | Action 5 Pro | PRO mode, 1/1000 s or faster for stills; or video |
| Phone | iPhone 2× or 5× | Halide or Lightroom for 1/1000 s; lock focus on the spot |

Use the L10's mechanical shutter for anything fast. **Indoor flicker:** on 50 Hz mains,
many indoor lights flicker 100 times a second. At 1/1000 s each frame catches a
different part of that flicker, so brightness and colour vary within a burst. Shoot
extra frames and pick, or check the manual for a flicker-reduction setting.

## Gear reality

Forums say sport needs a 400 mm f/2.8 lens and 20 frames per second. Professionals in
stadiums do use them. At community matches, park runs, skate parks and void-deck
courts, you can stand close.

- **The L10 at 75 mm** covers a lot from the touchline. Its mechanical shutter, up to
  1/2000 s, freezes almost any body movement, and it is the only one of your shutters
  that doesn't make fast swings lean and bend. → [[My-Kit]]
- **The iPhone's 120 mm** reaches further, but the Camera app chooses its own shutter
  speed. Use Halide or Lightroom to set it.
- **The Pocket 3** tracks smoothly in Follow mode alongside a runner: great for video,
  but its stills are only about 9 MP.
- **The Action 5 Pro** gives angles that a big lens never can.

## Ethics and the law

- **Event rules come first.** Many events limit cameras or control where you can stand.
  Follow the marshals, and never step onto the field of play. Don't use flash: it can
  distract athletes or put them at risk, such as a high jumper in mid-air.
- **Children's sport:** ask the coach and parents. Schools often have rules about
  photographing students. Don't post identifiable children without consent.
- **Safety:** stay out of the line of play, because balls, bikes and skateboards hurt.
  Secure any mount so it can't fall onto people, and ask before mounting a camera on
  someone else's gear.
- **Commercial use** of an athlete's image needs their permission; personal and
  editorial use differ. Laws differ and change; this is not legal advice.
  → [[Copyright-and-Consent]]

## Who to study

- **Henri Cartier-Bresson** — *Behind the Gare Saint-Lazare* (1932): a man leaping over
  a puddle, caught in the air above his own reflection. Look at how the whole picture is
  about the instant before contact.
- **Ernst Haas** — his 1950s colour pictures of bullfights and rodeos, made with slow
  shutter speeds. Look at how blur turns motion into colour and shape, the opposite of
  freezing it.
- **Neil Leifer** — boxing and American football for *Sports Illustrated* from the
  1960s, including a famous view of a boxing ring from high above it. Look at how his
  positions were chosen before the action began.

## A project you can finish in a week

**One sport, one position.** Pick a public game you can return to: football on a
community field, basketball at a void-deck court, a weekend park run, skaters at a
skate park. Ask the players or organisers first. Session 1: watch for 15 minutes
without shooting and note where the peaks happen, then shoot from one position only.
Session 2: pre-focus on that spot and use short bursts. Session 3: add one
point-of-view angle with the Action 5 Pro, with permission, and one panning series. Ten
frames (four peak action, two pans, two point-of-view, two emotion) make your A6.2 set.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- A runner coming straight at you is sharp at 1/500 s, but the same runner crossing in front of you is blurred. Why?
> Blur depends on movement across the frame. Coming towards you, the runner mostly grows
> larger and barely moves across it. Crossing, they sweep across many pixels during the
> exposure.

> [!question]- Your burst shows the ball before and after the racket, never touching it. Why, and what fixes it?
> Contact lasts a few milliseconds, but burst frames are much further apart, so the
> peak falls between frames. Fix it with timing: pre-focus, and press just before the
> moment.

> [!question]- Indoors at 1/1000 s, frames in one burst vary in brightness and colour. What is happening?
> The lights flicker 100 times a second on 50 Hz mains, and each fast frame catches a
> different part of the flicker. Shoot extra frames and pick, or use a flicker-reduction
> setting if the camera has one.

> [!question]- Why is the peak of a jump the best moment to press the shutter?
> At the top, vertical speed passes through zero, so the athlete is moving slowest and
> is easiest to freeze. It is also usually the most expressive pose.
