# Landscape

> [!summary] In one line
> Photographs of places: land, water and sky. You can't move the mountain, so the work
> is choosing where to stand and when to be there, then keeping the scene sharp from
> front to back.

## What makes a good one

- **Light and weather that make the place look like itself,** at its best or its most
  dramatic. The same view at noon and after a storm are two different pictures.
- **A foreground that leads in.** A rock, a path or a line of waves carries the eye
  from near to far. → [[Leading-Lines]]
- **One main shape** for the eye to land on, sharp from the near foreground to the
  horizon when the scene needs it.

## The hard part

### Planning

You can only choose your position and your time, so planning is most of the work.
→ [[Shoot-Planning]]

- **Scout in daylight.** Find the foreground and the view before the good light comes.
  For coastal scenes, check the tide; before any trip, check the rain radar.
- **Know where the sun will be.** Near the equator it rises in the east and sets in the
  west all year, swinging up to about 23° north or south of due east and west with the
  seasons. A sun-position app such as PhotoPills shows the direction for any date.
- **Arrive early.** The sun rises and sets steeply here, so golden hour is short. Be
  set up 30 minutes before sunrise (about 7:00) or sunset (about 19:00).

### Weather: working with Singapore's conditions

Singapore is flat (Bukit Timah Hill, the highest point, is about 164 m) and humid, so
the classic grand view is rare. Work with what is here.

- **Humid, hazy air.** Water vapour and particles scatter light. The further away
  something is, the more scattered light sits between you and it, so distant things go
  pale and bluish. Use it: on a long lens, rows of buildings or trees fade in steps,
  each paler than the one in front.
- **Flat midday light.** From late morning to mid-afternoon the sun is nearly overhead,
  shadows are short and shapes look flat. Shoot early or late. Under thick cloud, try
  forests and reservoirs, where soft light suits the detail.
- **Storms.** Afternoon and evening thunderstorms are common, especially between the
  monsoons (roughly April–May and October–November). The clouds before and after a
  storm are your drama. A rainbow appears opposite the sun, and only when the sun is
  lower than 42°: roughly before 10:00 or after 16:00.
- **Smoke haze.** In some years, smoke from regional fires blurs distant views. Check
  NEA's haze readings (the PSI) before planning a long view.

### Filters

- **Polariser:** cuts reflections on water and leaves, and darkens blue sky most at 90°
  from the sun. At midday here, with the sun almost overhead, that is the whole sky
  near the horizon. It cuts some blue haze, but does little for smoke.
- **ND (neutral density):** a dark filter that makes exposures longer, to smooth water
  and streak clouds. → [[ND-Filters]]
- **Graduated ND:** darker at the top, so a bright sky doesn't blow out above dark land.

Check whether your lens takes filters before planning around them. → [[Lens-Filters]]
Free workarounds: bracket and merge for bright skies; for smooth water, shoot 10–30
frames and average them in editing, which blurs the water like one long exposure.
→ [[Photo-Merges]]

### Hyperfocal distance and focus stacking

You can't just stop down to f/16 for depth: on the L10, diffraction softens the whole
image from about f/5.6–f/8. Place the focus instead. → [[Hyperfocal-Distance]]

The **hyperfocal distance** is the focus distance that makes everything from half that
distance to infinity acceptably sharp. On the L10, by the usual "sharp in a normal
print" standard:

| Zoom (equiv.) | f/4 | f/5.6 | f/8 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 24 mm | 2.1 m | 1.5 m | 1.1 m |
| 50 mm | 9 m | 6.5 m | 4.5 m |
| 75 mm | 21 m | 15 m | 10 m |

So at 24 mm and f/5.6, focus at 1.5 m and everything from about 0.8 m to infinity is
sharp. Hence the rule of thumb: focus at about twice the distance of the nearest thing
that must be sharp, as long as that is at least the table's distance. If unsure, focus
a little further, never closer: a soft horizon looks worse than a soft foreground edge.

When the foreground is very close, such as a rock 30 cm from the lens, no single focus
distance covers everything. **Focus stack:** on a support, take three to five frames
focused near, middle and far, changing nothing else, then merge the sharp parts in
editing.

## Settings to start with

- **Device:** the L10 for most scenes. The iPhone's 0.5× (13 mm) for a dramatic close
  foreground (12 MP and no stabilisation, so brace it); its 5× for layers in the haze.
- **Support:** a tripod, a wall, a railing, or a bag of rice on a ledge. Use the
  self-timer (check the manual) so pressing the button doesn't shake the camera.
- **Mode and aperture:** A at f/5.6. On the L10 that gives about the depth of field of
  f/11 on full frame, without going past the diffraction point.
- **ISO:** 100 on a support. Handheld, Auto ISO with 1/60 s or faster at 24 mm.
- **Focus:** manual, at the hyperfocal distance; or AF once on the right spot, then
  switch to MF so it stays there.
- **File and bright skies:** RAW, always. If the sky is far brighter than the land,
  bracket three frames at −2, 0 and +2 EV and merge them later. → [[Dynamic-Range]]

## Gear reality

Forums say landscape needs full frame, an ultra-wide lens, a heavy tripod and a filter
kit. You need to be there in the right light, something steady, and patience.

- **The L10's 24 mm** covers most views, and the iPhone's 13 mm goes wider. Four
  Thirds gives deep focus at f/4–f/5.6, before diffraction sets in. → [[My-Kit]]
- **No tripod?** Walls, railings, a bag of rice, and the vari-angle screen for low
  angles. **No ND?** Average a stack of frames, or shoot at dusk.
- **Rain and spray:** the L10 isn't splash-resistant. In sea spray or drizzle use the
  Action 5 Pro (waterproof) or the iPhone (IP68). Afterwards, store gear in a dry box:
  fungus grows on lenses in Singapore's humidity.

## Ethics and the law

- **Leave no trace.** Stay on paths; nature reserves ask you to keep to marked trails.
  Don't trample plants for a foreground, or collect shells and animals from the shore.
- **Signs mean it.** Military areas and some infrastructure sites ban photography or
  entry. Rules change, and this is not legal advice.
- **Be honest about edits.** Sky replacement and composites are fine as personal art.
  Don't present them as one real moment where honesty is expected, such as contests
  with strict rules.
- **Stay safe.** In a thunderstorm, leave open ground, beaches and lone trees and go
  indoors. Carry water, and use insect repellent: dengue mosquitoes bite mainly in the
  daytime, especially early morning and late afternoon.

## Who to study

- **Ansel Adams** — *Clearing Winter Storm*, Yosemite. Look at how the weather is the
  subject, and how cliffs, cloud and valley floor each sit in a different tone. He
  planned the final print before he made the exposure.
- **Michael Kenna** — minimal black-and-white landscapes, often long exposures at dawn,
  many made in Hokkaido. Look at how mist and long exposure remove detail until one
  shape is left: the lesson for Singapore's hazy days.
- **Joel Meyerowitz** — *Cape Light* (1978), colour made with a large-format camera on
  Cape Cod. Look at how the same few places change with the light at dusk and in
  different weather.

## A project you can finish in a week

**One view, five lights.** Choose one view you can reach easily: a reservoir edge, a
stretch of coast, a quarry lake, a park hill. Visit it five times: sunrise, midday, late
afternoon, after a storm, and blue hour. Stand in the same spot each time, keep the
same framing, and focus with the hyperfocal table. Two frames from each visit make a
ten-frame set for A6.2; it is a longer version of A2.4. For A6.3, shoot the view on the
iPhone's 120 mm and see how the haze layers change.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- On the L10 at 24 mm and f/5.6, where do you focus to keep the most in focus, and what will be sharp?
> At about 1.7 m, the hyperfocal distance. Everything from about 0.9 m to infinity is
> then acceptably sharp.

> [!question]- Why do distant towers look pale and blue on a humid day, and how can you use it?
> Water vapour and particles scatter light, and the further away something is, the more
> scattered light lies in between, so contrast and colour fade with distance. A long
> lens turns that into layers, each paler than the one in front.

> [!question]- Why not shoot at f/16 for maximum depth of field on the L10?
> Diffraction softens the whole frame from about f/5.6–f/8. You would gain depth but
> lose sharpness everywhere. Use the hyperfocal distance, or focus stack.

> [!question]- When can you see a rainbow in Singapore, and where should you look?
> Only when the sun is lower than 42°: roughly before 10:00 or after 16:00. Stand with
> your back to the sun after rain; the bow is centred on the point opposite the sun.
