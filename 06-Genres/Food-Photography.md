# Food Photography

> [!summary] In one line
> Photographs that make food look as good as it tastes, and in Singapore, often taken
> fast at a hawker table. The tools are simple: window light from the side or behind,
> the right angle for the dish, a little styling, and speed.

## What makes a good one

- **Appetite appeal:** visible texture (crisp skin, glossy sauce, rising steam) and
  fresh colour, in light from the side or behind, never flat flash from the camera.
- **The right angle for the dish's shape.**
- **One hero.** One dish leads and the others support it.
- **Context when it helps:** hands, chopsticks, the hawker table, the stall. Food is
  also about people and place.

## The hard part

### Light: side and back

Window light is the classic food light. Put the food near a window, with the light
coming from the side of the dish or from behind it, as seen from the camera.
→ [[Direction-of-Light]]

- **Side light** makes every grain, crumb and crust cast a tiny shadow. That is what
  texture looks like in a photograph.
- **Backlight** passes through translucent things (soup, drinks, lettuce, sauces) so
  they glow. Glossy surfaces catch highlights, which read as fresh and juicy. Steam
  lights up against a darker background.
- **Front light,** including on-camera flash, fills every shadow, and food looks flat
  and greasy.
- **Fill** too-dark shadows with a white card, a menu or a napkin on the camera side.
  Soften harsh direct sun with a thin curtain, or move into shade. → [[Natural-Light]]

### Angles

| Angle | Best for | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Overhead (90°) | Bowls, flat dishes, a table of many dishes: laksa, a spread of hawker food | Shows shapes and patterns; the table becomes a graphic layout |
| 45° (the diner's view) | Most plates: chicken rice, nasi lemak | Shows the top and the side, as you see food when you sit down |
| Eye level | Tall food: burgers, layered kueh, drinks | Shows the layers and the height |

At 45° and at eye level, a longer focal length keeps round plates round: the L10 at
50–75 mm, or the iPhone's 2×. Close up with a wide lens, the near edge of the plate
looks huge. → [[Focal-Length-and-Perspective]] Overhead, keep the camera parallel to
the table, or square trays lean, for the same reason a tilted camera makes buildings
lean.

### Styling and props

- **Clean the rim** of the plate, and wipe drips.
- **Garnish for colour contrast:** a few fresh herbs, sliced chilli, a lime wedge. Green
  on brown curry, red on white rice. → [[Colour-Theory-for-Photographers]]
- **Props support, never compete:** a spoon, chopsticks, a napkin, a hand reaching in.
  Plain, matte and small; nothing brighter than the food. Uneven numbers often look
  more natural: three dumplings, not four in a row.
- **Leave a little life:** a crumb, a drip, a bite taken. It says "real food".

### Steam and freshness

Food fades fast on camera: herbs wilt within minutes, sauces form a skin, ice melts,
noodles clump, fried food goes limp.

- **Set up before the food arrives.** Light, angle, props and exposure, with an empty
  plate as a stand-in. Then shoot the real dish in the first minute or two.
- **Steam** needs backlight and a dark background behind it. It rises and vanishes
  fast, so shoot a short burst as the dish lands.
- **At home,** a quick brush of oil or water refreshes the shine. Commercial stylists
  fake steam and gloss; for a review or a menu, the picture must still show what the
  customer gets.

### Shooting quickly and politely in hawker centres

- **Your own food, at your own table, in under a minute.** Then eat.
- **Find the light:** sit near the open side of the hawker centre for daylight, away
  from the tables under the brightest tubes.
- **Don't block the queue,** stand on chairs, or move other people's things. Leave
  chope tissue packets where they are. No flash: it bothers everyone and flattens food.
- **Other diners** didn't agree to be in your post. Frame them out, or keep them
  unrecognisable.
- **Ask the stall-holder** before photographing them or their stall. Most are happy;
  some aren't. Avoid the lunch rush (about 12:00–13:30).
- **Mixed light:** daylight plus fluorescent or LED tubes gives colour casts and
  flicker. Shoot RAW or fix the colour in editing, and on the L10 use the mechanical
  shutter. → [[White-Balance]]

### Phone-friendly technique

- **Clean the lens first.** Oily fingerprints make hazy, glowing photos.
- **1× for overhead, 2× (48 mm) for 45°,** which keeps plates round.
- **Press and hold on the food** to lock focus and exposure (AE/AF LOCK), then drag the
  sun icon down a little, so white plates and shiny sauces keep their highlights.
- **Macro for texture:** move to about 2 cm and the phone switches to its ultra-wide
  macro. Watch your own shadow.
- **Portrait mode** blurs the background with software. Check glass rims, steam and
  chopsticks at 100%, where it guesses wrong. Keep the flash off, and leave
  Photographic Styles on Standard if you will edit: on this model they bake in at
  capture.

## Settings to start with

| Device | Settings |
|---|---|
| iPhone | 1× overhead or 2× at 45°; AE/AF lock on the food; exposure slightly down; flash off; ProRAW if you will edit (48 MP on 1× only) |
| L10 | A mode; 50–75 mm; f/2.8 at 45° for a soft background, f/5.6 overhead; Auto ISO; 1/125 s or faster; one AF point on the front of the hero; mechanical shutter under tubes |
| Pocket 3 | Short video of steam, pouring, or flames from a wok |

## Gear reality

Forums say you need a macro lens, a light tent and a styling kit. You need a window, a
white card (a menu or a napkin works), your phone, and speed.

- **The iPhone may be the best hawker-centre camera you own:** fast, quiet, and nobody
  looks twice. → [[My-Kit]]
- **The L10 shines at home,** with window light and 75 mm for a softer background at
  45°. At f/2.8 the focus falls off across the plate, so choose where it lands: usually
  the front edge of the hero.

## Ethics and the law

- **Restaurants:** some ask diners not to photograph, or not to use flash. Respect it,
  and ask before photographing staff or kitchens.
- **Honest reviews:** a heavily styled or edited picture misleads readers about portion,
  colour and freshness. If a meal was free or paid for, say so: advertising rules in
  many places, including Singapore, expect sponsored posts to be labelled. This is
  general guidance, not legal advice.
- **Don't waste food for a picture.** Eat what you photograph.

## Who to study

- **Edward Weston** — *Pepper No. 30* (1930): a single pepper inside a tin funnel, which
  served as its background and bounced light around it. Look at how a vegetable becomes
  pure form under soft, wrapping light.
- **Stephen Shore** — *American Surfaces*: plain snapshots from an early-1970s road
  trip, including many of the meals he ate. Look at how unstyled food on diner tables
  describes a time and a place.
- **Martin Parr** — close, flash-lit, saturated pictures of everyday food. Look at how
  hard flash and bright colour, the opposite of soft window light, say something about
  culture and taste.

## A project you can finish in a week

**Ten dishes, one hawker centre, one angle.** Choose a hawker centre near you. Eat there
five times this week and photograph two dishes each visit: your own food, at a table on
the daylight side. One rule: the same angle for all ten (45° or overhead), with the
light from the same side every time, so they read as a set. Under 60 seconds per dish.
That's your A6.2 set. Then, at home, reshoot one takeaway dish by a window with side
and back light, and compare.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why does backlight make soup and sauces look fresher than front light?
> Light passing through translucent liquid makes it glow, and highlights on glossy
> surfaces read as moisture. Front light fills the shadows and kills both, so the food
> looks flat.

> [!question]- Which angle suits a stack of kueh lapis, and why?
> Eye level. Its whole appeal is the layers, which show only from the side. From
> overhead you would see just the top layer.

> [!question]- Your chicken rice shot at 45° on the iPhone's 0.5× looks distorted. Why, and what fixes it?
> A wide lens used close up exaggerates the nearest parts, so the front of the plate
> swells. Step back and use the 2×, or the L10 at 50–75 mm.

> [!question]- Why set up the shot before the food arrives?
> Freshness fades within minutes: herbs wilt, steam stops, sauces skin over. With light,
> angle and exposure ready, the first minute goes on the food itself.
