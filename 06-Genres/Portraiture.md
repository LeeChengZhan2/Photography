# Portraiture

> [!summary] In one line
> Photographs that show what a person is like, not just how they look. The settings are
> easy; directing, distance and the light in the eyes are the work.

## What makes a good one

- **A real expression,** not the stiff "camera face" people make when a lens points at
  them, and a sharp nearer eye with a small reflection of the light in it.
- **Light that shapes the face.** Soft light from the side or at 45° gives the face
  depth; flat light from the camera position flattens it. Moving one light from in
  front of the face round to its side gives the four classic portrait patterns:
  butterfly, loop, Rembrandt and split. → [[Direction-of-Light]]
- **A decision about context.** A tight portrait is about the face. An environmental
  portrait shows the person in their place: a hawker at the wok, a grandmother in her
  kitchen. Both work; choose one on purpose.
- **Clean edges on the body.** When you crop a person, crop between joints (mid-thigh,
  waist, mid-upper-arm), never through the neck, an elbow, wrist, knee or ankle: a cut
  at a joint looks like an amputation. Leave a little space above the head, or crop
  into the hair on purpose in a tight portrait, but not something in between.

## The hard part

The hard part is the person in front of you, not the camera. Most people tense up when
photographed: shoulders rise, smiles go stiff. That needs rapport and direction, not
settings.

### Rapport

Talk before you shoot: say what you're making, how long it takes, and where the
pictures will go. Expect the first frames to be stiff, and treat the first ten minutes
as a warm-up. Show a good frame on the screen early; it builds trust. Keep talking while
you shoot, because silence behind a camera feels like something is wrong. Correct the
pose, never the person: "let's try something" beats "that looks bad".

### Directing and posing

Give actions, not instructions about feelings. "Relax" makes people more aware of
themselves; a physical task gives their attention somewhere to go.

| Instead of | Try |
|---|---|
| "Relax." | "Drop your shoulders and breathe out." |
| "Smile." | Ask about something they love, and shoot as they answer. |
| "Look natural." | "Look out of the window. Now back at me." |

- **Turn the body 30–45° from the camera, then the face back towards you.** A body
  square to the lens looks wide and static; the turn adds shape.
- **Chin slightly forward and down.** It defines the jawline and stops the camera
  looking up the nose.
- **Give the hands a job:** a cup, a pocket, the edge of a table. Idle hands look
  awkward. Leave a small gap between arm and body, or the arm looks wider.
- **Show, don't explain.** Do the pose yourself and let them copy it.

### Lens choice and distance

Perspective comes from distance, not from the lens. → [[Focal-Length-and-Perspective]]
The tip of the nose is about 10 cm closer to you than the ears. At 50 cm that is 20%
closer, so the nose looks clearly bigger. At 2 m it is only 5% closer, and the face
looks natural. The lens then only decides how much fits in the frame.

| Frame | Distance | L10 | iPhone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Head and shoulders | 1.5–2 m | 75 mm, f/2.8 | 5× (120 mm) |
| Waist up | 2–3 m | about 50 mm | 2× (48 mm) |
| Environmental | 2–4 m | 24–35 mm | 1× (24 mm) |

Background blur on the L10 is modest: f/2.8 blurs about as much as f/5.6 would on full
frame. So separate with distance. Put the background far behind your subject (5–10 m),
and choose one that is plain and darker than the face.

### Catchlights and eye light

A catchlight is the small reflection of the light source in the eye. The eye is a
curved mirror, so it shows whatever bright thing is in front of it: a window becomes a
soft rectangle, a bare bulb a hard pinpoint. Without one, eyes look flat and lifeless.

- **Aim for about 10 or 2 o'clock** in the iris. It says "light from above and to the
  side", which reads as natural.
- **Midday is the enemy.** From late morning to mid-afternoon, Singapore's sun is nearly
  overhead. The brow shades the eyes into dark pits with no catchlight. Move into open
  shade, such as the edge of a void deck, with the person facing the open sky. The
  bright sky becomes a huge soft light and a catchlight. → [[Natural-Light]]
- **Bounce light up** with a white card held below the face, out of frame. It fills the
  eye sockets and adds a second, lower catchlight.
- **Focus on the nearer eye.** If that eye is soft, the portrait fails. If glasses
  reflect the light, tilt them slightly down.

## Settings to start with

- **Device and zoom:** the L10 at 75 mm for head and shoulders; 24–35 mm for
  environmental portraits.
- **Mode and ISO:** A with Auto ISO, capped at your A1.3 ceiling.
- **Aperture:** f/2.8 at 75 mm (the widest it allows there). For two people at
  different distances from you, f/4.
- **Shutter:** 1/250 s or faster. People move, talk and blink, and stabilisation only
  steadies your hands. Under LED lights, use the mechanical shutter to avoid banding.
- **Focus:** single AF, with one small AF point moved onto the nearer eye. Don't focus
  and recompose: at close range, turning the camera shifts focus off the eye.
- **iPhone:** 2× or 5×. Press and hold on the eye to lock focus and exposure. Portrait
  mode adds blur with software, so check hair, glasses and ears at 100%, where it
  guesses wrong. → [[Computational-Photography]]

## Gear reality

Forums say a "real" portrait needs a full-frame camera and an 85 mm f/1.4 lens. You need
a face in good light, a natural distance, and a relaxed person.

- **The L10 at 75 mm** is a classic portrait view. Choose far, plain backgrounds rather
  than chasing a blur the sensor can't give. → [[My-Kit]]
- **The iPhone's 2× (48 mm, f/1.78) and 5× (120 mm)** are both good portrait lengths.
  The 2× is a 12 MP crop: fine for screens and small prints.
- **Your best modifier is free:** a window for light, and a white wall, sheet or card to
  bounce it back. → [[Light-Modifiers]]
- **The Pocket 3 and Action 5 Pro** are wide and deep-focused, so they're poor for tight
  portraits. Standard (Dewarp) removes the Action 5 Pro's fisheye bend, but not the big
  nose that comes from standing close.

## Ethics and the law

- **Portraits are made with people, not of them.** Say how you'll use the pictures, and
  ask before you post them.
- **Releases.** Using a recognisable person in an advert or for stock usually needs a
  signed model release; personal and editorial use is treated differently. Laws differ
  by country and change, and this is not legal advice. → [[Copyright-and-Consent]]
- **Children** need a parent's or guardian's consent.
- **Retouching.** Removing a passing spot is normal. Reshaping a face or body without
  asking is not. → [[Retouching]]
- **Money.** If someone pays you, agree in writing who may use the pictures, and how.
  Either way, give the photos back: it's the simplest thank-you.

## Who to study

- **Richard Avedon** — *In the American West* (1979–1984): drifters, miners and other
  working people against plain white paper, photographed outdoors in open shade. Look at
  how removing the background makes posture, hands and clothing tell the story.
- **Irving Penn** — the "corner" portraits of the late 1940s: sitters squeezed into a
  narrow corner made by two walls. Look at how the tight space pushes each person into
  a different pose, and how soft light shapes every face.
- **Dorothea Lange** — *Migrant Mother* (1936) and the frames she made before it. Look
  at how she moved closer with each exposure, and how hands and gaze carry the feeling.
  Then read what the woman in it, Florence Owens Thompson, later said about it.

## A project you can finish in a week

**Five people, one patch of shade.** Find open shade near home, such as the edge of a
void deck, with open sky in front. Photograph five people you know, twenty minutes each,
at about the same time of day (17:00–18:00 works well). Same frame for all: head and
shoulders, L10 at 75 mm, f/2.8. Name the lighting pattern on each face (the table is in
the Direction of Light note), and check each catchlight on the screen. Then photograph
each person once more in their own space at 24–35 mm. One tight and one environmental
frame per person makes a ten-frame set for A6.2. For A6.3, shoot one person at 24 mm
from 50 cm and compare the nose.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- A face fills the frame equally at 50 cm with 24 mm and at 2 m with 75 mm. Why does it look different?
> Perspective comes from distance. The nose is about 10 cm closer than the ears: 20%
> closer at 50 cm, only 5% at 2 m. Up close the nose looks bigger. The lens only
> changes how much fits.

> [!question]- At 13:00 your subject's eyes are dark pits. Name two fixes and say why each works.
> Move into open shade facing the open sky: the light then comes from a big source in
> front, which fills the eye sockets and makes a catchlight. Or bounce light up with a
> white card below the face. Or wait for lower sun after about 17:00.

> [!question]- On the L10 at 75 mm and f/2.8, the background isn't blurred enough. What can you change without new gear?
> Put more distance between the subject and the background, and choose a plainer one.
> Or move closer and frame tighter: more magnification means more blur. You can't open
> wider than f/2.8 at 75 mm.

> [!question]- Your subject freezes every time you say "relax". What do you say instead, and why does it work?
> Give a physical task: "drop your shoulders and breathe out", or "look at the window,
> now back at me". An action gives their attention a job, so their face stops posing.
