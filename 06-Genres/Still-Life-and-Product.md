# Still Life and Product

> [!summary] In one line
> Photographs of objects you choose, arrange and light yourself. Nothing moves and
> nothing is left to chance, so every flaw in the frame is a decision you made, or
> forgot to make.

## What makes a good one

- **One idea:** a shape, a texture, a material, or a small story between two objects.
  → [[Simplification]]
- **Light that describes the material.** Glass glows, metal shows clean highlights,
  wood shows its grain, fabric shows its weave.
- **A deliberate arrangement:** spacing, overlaps and angles all chosen, nothing
  accidental. → [[Visual-Weight-and-Balance]]
- **A background that supports the object** rather than competing with it.
- **For products, truth.** The buyer must recognise the colour, size and finish when the
  parcel arrives.

## The hard part

### Total control means no excuses

In street photography, luck helps. Here, everything is yours: the dust, the fingerprint,
the crooked label, the stray reflection. At 100% you will see all of them. The skill is
noticing. Before each shot, check the frame edge by edge and corner to corner. Clean
the object, handle shiny things by their edges, and change one thing at a time so you
know what each change did.

### Tabletop lighting with one light and reflectors

The basic set: a table beside a window (or one lamp), the object, a background, and a
few cards.

1. **Key light:** the window, from the side or slightly behind. Side light shows
   texture; light from behind makes glass and liquids glow. → [[Direction-of-Light]]
2. **Fill:** a white card (foam board, paper, a white T-shirt) on the shadow side
   bounces light back in. Move it closer for more fill.
3. **Negative fill:** a black card on the shadow side absorbs stray light, so shadows
   get deeper and edges sharper.
4. **Diffuser:** baking paper or a thin white curtain over the window softens the light.
5. **Small mirrors or foil:** add a small highlight exactly where you want one.

Turn off the room lights. Warm lamps mixed with daylight give two colour casts that no
single white balance can fix. → [[White-Balance]]

**Shiny objects are mirrors.** What you see on a bottle's surface is a reflection of
whatever is in front of it. For a clean, smooth highlight, put a large, soft, bright
surface where the bottle "sees" it: a diffused window, or a white card lit by the
window. A small bare bulb gives a hard little hot spot instead. Matte surfaces scatter
light in every direction, so their brightness depends on the light's angle, not on your
viewpoint. → [[Quality-of-Light]]

**Glass** shows up only by its edges:

- **Bright-field:** glass in front of a bright white background. Edges show as dark
  lines.
- **Dark-field:** glass in front of black, lit from behind at both sides. Edges show as
  bright lines.

### Backgrounds

- **A sweep** (paper curving from the wall down onto the table) has no horizon line, so
  the background seems to go on forever.
- **Distance controls the tone.** Keep the object near the light and the background far
  behind it. The background gets less light than the object, so it records darker, and
  softer too. → [[Inverse-Square-Law]]
- **Surfaces:** a wooden board, a slate tile, linen, a marble table. Matte, unless you
  want reflections. Choose a colour that harmonises with the object, or contrasts on
  purpose.

### Compositing, in brief

When one light can't do everything, shoot several frames with the light in different
places, then combine them. Keep the camera on a support and every setting fixed, so the
frames line up. In editing, stack them as layers and paint in the best part of each
with masks, or blend them with a "lighten" mode. The same method removes a stand that
held the object up: shoot one frame without the object, and mask the stand away.
→ [[Retouching]]

## Settings to start with

- **Device:** the L10 on a support, at 50–75 mm. A wide angle close up bends boxes and
  bottles.
- **Mode:** M. ISO 100, f/5.6 (about where diffraction starts), and whatever shutter
  speed the light needs. 1/15 s or even 1 s is fine, because nothing moves.
- **Focus:** a single AF point on the front label, or MF with magnified live view.
- **Self-timer** (check the manual), so your touch doesn't shake the camera.
- **White balance:** include a grey card or white paper in the first frame, and set the
  balance from it in editing. Shoot RAW.
- **Flicker:** on Singapore's 50 Hz mains, many indoor lights flicker 100 times a
  second. A shutter of 1/25 s or slower spans several cycles and averages it out; the
  mechanical shutter also avoids banding.
- **iPhone:** 2× (48 mm) on a stand. Press and hold to lock focus and exposure, then
  drag the sun icon down a little to keep the highlights.

## Gear reality

Forums say you need a light tent, studio flashes, a macro lens and a tethered laptop.
You need one window, a table, white and black card, baking paper, tape, and something
steady.

- On a support, the L10 can use slow shutter speeds at ISO 100, which beats any high
  ISO for clean files. Its hot shoe can take a flash if you have one, but a window is
  enough to learn. → [[My-Kit]]
- For small products sold online, the iPhone is fully capable.

## Ethics and the law

- **Represent products honestly.** Colour, size and condition must match what the buyer
  receives. Misleading product photos can break consumer protection rules in many
  countries, and they always cost trust. This is general guidance, not legal advice.
- **Cleaning is fine; changing is not.** Removing dust and stands is normal. Changing
  colour, size or features misleads.
- **Brands and designs:** a logo or someone else's design in commercial work can raise
  trademark or copyright questions. Check before you sell. → [[Copyright-and-Consent]]
- **Artworks as props:** credit the maker, and ask before using them commercially.

## Who to study

- **Irving Penn** — still lifes for *Vogue* and for advertising, and personal series
  such as cigarette ends picked up from the street. Look at the simplicity: soft light,
  a plain background, and objects placed with great care.
- **Josef Sudek** — still lifes in his Prague studio, many on the windowsill: glasses,
  eggs, fruit, often with mist on the window. Look at how one window is the only light,
  and how glass and water catch it.
- **Paul Outerbridge** — *Ide Collar* (1922): an advert for a shirt collar, set on a
  checkerboard. Look at how angle, shape and shadow turn a dull product into a graphic
  design.

## A project you can finish in a week

**One object, one window.** Choose an object with an interesting material: a glass, a
kopi cup, a metal spoon, a piece of fruit. Use one window as the only light. Make eight
frames, changing one thing each time: side or back light, white or black card, a sweep
or a textured surface, bright-field or dark-field for glass, and one composite of two
light positions. Label each. Then make two "catalogue" frames of something you own: one
on white, one in context. Ten frames make your A6.2 set.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why does a large diffused light give a bottle a clean highlight, while a bare bulb gives a hot spot?
> The bottle's surface is a mirror, so it shows the light source's shape and size. A
> large soft source makes a large smooth reflection; a small bulb makes a tiny bright
> point.

> [!question]- You want a darker background without changing the light. What do you move, and why does it work?
> Move the background further back, keeping the object near the light. The background
> is now much further from the light than the object, so it receives much less light,
> and it blurs more too.

> [!question]- Why is a 1 s exposure fine here when it would be wrong for street photography?
> Nothing moves and the camera is on a support, so a long exposure causes no blur. It
> lets you keep ISO 100 for the cleanest file, and it averages out light flicker.

> [!question]- What does a black card do that a white card doesn't?
> It absorbs light that would otherwise bounce back from walls and the table, so shadows
> get deeper and edges more defined. A white card does the opposite: it fills shadows.
