# Documentary and Photojournalism

> [!summary] In one line
> Photographs that tell true stories about real people and events. Every step must be
> honest: how you got access, what you photographed, how you edited it, and what the
> caption says.

## What makes a good one

**Photojournalism** reports news, often in single images made to a deadline.
**Documentary** goes slower and deeper: a project over weeks, months or years. Both
share the same standards.

- **Truth with a point of view.** It shows what happened, framed so the viewer
  understands why it matters. → [[Subject-and-Intent]]
- **A story, not a pile of pictures:** a set with a beginning, a middle and an end.
- **Intimacy that only time and trust can give.** You can see in the pictures whether
  the photographer was accepted.
- **Captions that inform:** who, what, where, when, and why if you know it.

## The hard part

### Narrative arc

A shot list long used by magazine photographers for photo essays:

| Shot | Its job |
|---|---|
| Establishing (wide) | Where are we? |
| Medium, interaction | Who is involved, and what are they doing together? |
| Close-up, detail | Hands, tools, objects that carry the story |
| Portrait | One face that holds attention |
| Sequence | Several frames of one action unfolding |
| Signature image | The single frame that sums it all up |
| Closing image | An ending: the work stops, the light goes, people leave |

Viewers read a set like sentences. Changes of distance and pace keep them moving;
twelve medium shots in a row lose them. A set also needs change: something at the end
should be different from the start. → [[Story-Structure]] · [[Editing-a-Sequence]]

### Access and consent

- **Access is earned.** Explain what you're doing, why, and where the pictures will
  appear. Spend time without shooting. Come back. Share pictures with the people in
  them.
- **Consent must be informed.** People should know whether they're agreeing to a
  personal project, a social media post or a newspaper. If someone withdraws consent,
  respect it wherever you can.
- **Vulnerable people** (children, the sick, the grieving, people in crisis) need extra
  care. Ask whether the story really needs their face. Children need a guardian's
  consent. Never trade someone's dignity for a stronger picture.
- **Private places** such as homes, workplaces and places of worship need the owner's or
  organiser's permission.

### Honest captioning

The same image under two captions tells two different stories, so the caption is part
of the evidence.

- Answer **who, what, where, when**, and **why** only if you know it.
- Write captions the same day. Ask people to spell their names, and check dates and
  places.
- Write what you saw, not what you assume: "A man eats alone at a hawker table at
  23:00", not "A lonely man...". Don't guess feelings, relationships or causes.
- Say if a picture was posed, re-enacted or combined from several frames.

### No manipulation

Most press codes allow the kind of changes a darkroom could make, and nothing more.

| Usually acceptable | Not acceptable |
|---|---|
| Exposure, contrast, white balance | Adding, removing, moving or cloning anything |
| Cropping that doesn't change the meaning | Combining frames, or generative fill |
| Light dodging and burning | Toning so heavy that it hides part of the scene |

Even a stray plastic bag stays in: once anything can be removed, nothing can be
trusted. Contests such as World Press Photo ask for original files and disqualify
altered entries, so always keep your raws. And never stage a moment and present it as
candid.

## Settings to start with

- **L10 at 24–35 mm.** Close and wide puts the viewer inside the scene. A mode at f/4
  (wider indoors), Auto ISO capped at your A1.3 ceiling, and a shutter of at least
  1/125–1/250 s.
- **Focus:** single AF with [AF ON], or zone focus at 2–3 m for fast scenes.
- **Shutter type:** the silent electronic shutter in quiet places such as ceremonies and
  interviews, but the mechanical one under LED lights, which can band.
- **No flash** in most situations. It changes the scene and draws attention to you.
- **iPhone** as a quiet second camera. **Pocket 3** for interviews and moving shots,
  when the story needs sound. → [[Recording-Sound]]
- **A notebook** (or your phone's notes) for names, times and places, every time.

## Gear reality

Forums say you need two professional bodies, fast primes and a press pass. You need
time with people, a camera they stop noticing, a notebook, and honesty.

- The L10's small size helps: it looks far less threatening than a big camera with a
  long lens. → [[My-Kit]]
- Phones are accepted documentary tools. What matters is access and honesty, not the
  logo on the camera.

## Ethics and the law

- **Codes of ethics.** Press photographers' groups publish codes covering accuracy, no
  manipulation, care for vulnerable people, and not paying subjects, because payment
  can change what people do and say. Read one, such as the US NPPA's code.
- **The law, in general terms.** Publishing pictures of identifiable people can raise
  privacy, harassment and defamation issues. Some places, such as courts, ban
  photography. Rules on showing minors and victims of crime are strict in many
  countries. Laws differ and change; this is not legal advice.
  → [[Copyright-and-Consent]]
- **Religious events and processions:** follow the organisers' guidance, never block
  the people taking part, and ask before close portraits.
- **Show your subjects the work** before you publish it, when you can. It is fair, and
  it catches caption errors.

## Who to study

- **W. Eugene Smith** — *Country Doctor* (*LIFE*, 1948): a country doctor's days and
  nights in a small Colorado town. Look at the arc: the setting, the work, the exhausted
  ending. It is a template for the photo essay.
- **Gordon Parks** — *American Gothic* (1942), a government cleaner photographed with
  her mop and broom in front of the US flag, and his *LIFE* stories such as
  *Segregation Story* (1956). Look at how his relationships with people gave him access,
  and how his framing gives them dignity.
- **Sim Chi Yin** — Singapore-born; long, research-led projects that combine
  photographs with archives, film and installation. Look at how she stays with one
  subject for years, and how much research sits behind each frame.

## A project you can finish in a week

**A day in one working life.** Ask one person who works with their hands if you can
follow their working day: a hawker, a cobbler, a market stall-holder, a relative. Visit
two or three times: opening, the rush, closing. Shoot with the shot list above. Write a
caption for every frame, with names spelled and facts checked with them. Edit to ten
frames with a clear beginning and end, and show them the set before you share it
anywhere. That's your A6.2 set.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- You removed a plastic bag from the corner of a documentary frame. Why is that a problem, when the bag didn't matter?
> Once anything can be removed, nothing in the picture can be trusted. The rule has to
> be absolute: nothing added, removed or moved, however small.

> [!question]- What's wrong with the caption "A sad old man at a hawker centre", and how would you fix it?
> "Sad" is your guess, and "old man" names no one. Give his name if he agreed, the
> place and the date, and describe only what you saw him doing.

> [!question]- Why spend time with people before you photograph them?
> They get used to you, so they behave normally. Trust gives you access to moments they
> wouldn't show a stranger, and you learn what is worth photographing.

> [!question]- Your set has ten strong frames, all medium shots. Why does it feel flat?
> There's no change of distance or pace: no wide shot to set the scene, no detail, no
> ending. A story needs variety and an arc, not just good single frames.
