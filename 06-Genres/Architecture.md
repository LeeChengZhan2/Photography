# Architecture

> [!summary] In one line
> Photographs of buildings and spaces that show their design: form, rhythm, material
> and use. The craft is keeping vertical lines straight, choosing the angle that
> explains the building, and waiting for the light that shows its shape.

## What makes a good one

- **Straight verticals,** unless a lean is strong and clearly deliberate. Slightly
  leaning walls just look like a mistake.
- **An angle that explains the design.** A flat-on front view shows symmetry and
  pattern. A corner view shows two sides and the building's volume. A detail shows
  material and rhythm. → [[Symmetry-and-Centring]]
- **Light that reveals form:** sun raking across a textured wall, one face lit and the
  other in shade.
- **Scale and use.** One person in the frame shows how big a space is and what it's for.

Singapore is full of subjects: HDB blocks with repeating windows and corridors,
shophouse façades, modernist complexes, places of worship, glass towers.
→ [[Pattern-Repetition-and-the-Break]]

## The hard part

### Verticals

When you tilt the camera up to fit a tall building, the sensor is no longer parallel to
the façade. The top of the building is now further from the sensor's plane than the
bottom, so it is drawn smaller, and parallel walls lean towards each other. Keep the
camera level, so the sensor stands upright, and the verticals stay parallel however far
away the top is.

Ways to keep the camera level and still fit the building in:

1. **Move back or go up.** A footbridge, a car park deck or a building opposite lets
   you shoot level.
2. **Shoot wider, then crop.** Frame the building level at 24 mm (or 0.5× on the
   iPhone), with empty ground in the bottom half, then crop that ground away. You lose
   pixels, but the lines stay straight with no stretching.
3. **Correct in editing,** as below.

Turn on the camera's grid or level display, if it has one (check the manual).

### Perspective correction

Editing software can straighten converging verticals with a "vertical" or "upright"
transform. → [[Perspective-Correction]]

- **Fix lens distortion first.** Wide lenses bend straight lines outwards (barrel
  distortion). Apply the lens profile, then correct perspective. → [[Lens-Corrections]]
- **Leave room.** Correction stretches the top of the frame and crops the edges, so
  frame looser than you need.
- **Expect softness at the top.** The top is stretched wider, so its detail is spread
  thinner. A strong correction costs a large part of the frame.
- **Don't always correct fully.** For very tall buildings, many photographers leave a
  slight lean, because full correction can make the top look too heavy.

### Tilt-shift, and why you correct in editing instead

A **tilt-shift lens** does two things. *Shift* slides the lens up, parallel to the
sensor, so the top of a building fits while the camera stays level. *Tilt* angles the
lens so the plane of focus tilts, which can make a whole façade sharp, or create the
"miniature" look.

They are specialist lenses for interchangeable-lens cameras, and the L10's lens is
fixed, so you can't fit one. You don't need one: shooting wide and cropping is the same
geometry as a shift, and editing does the rest. The cost is some resolution and edge
quality. The "miniature" look can be faked with a graduated blur in editing.

### Timing for light

- **East faces get morning sun; west faces get afternoon sun.** Plan the side you want
  lit.
- **North and south faces depend on the month.** At about 1° north, the sun stays in
  the northern half of the sky all day from about late March to late September. Then
  north faces are sunlit and south faces in shade; the rest of the year it's reversed.
  Around midday, that light grazes the wall steeply from above. Check a sun app.
- **Midday:** with the sun nearly overhead, balconies and ledges throw deep shadows
  straight down: strong patterns on HDB blocks, dark five-foot ways.
- **Overcast** gives even light, good for colour (painted shophouses) and interiors.
- **Blue hour** balances lit windows with the sky, the classic architecture moment.
  After sunset the sky dims fast, and for a few minutes it matches the interior lights.
  Here that window is short, so set up before sunset. → [[Direction-of-Light]]

## Settings to start with

- **Device:** the L10 at 24 mm for most buildings. The iPhone's 0.5× (13 mm) for tight
  interiors (12 MP, no stabilisation, strong distortion at the edges); its 5× for
  details and stacked façades seen from far away.
- **Mode, aperture, ISO:** A at f/5.6, ISO 100 on a support. Handheld, Auto ISO with
  1/60 s or faster at 24 mm.
- **Focus:** on the façade. At 24 mm and f/5.6, everything beyond a couple of metres is
  sharp.
- **Interiors:** windows are far brighter than the room, so bracket three to five
  frames (−2 to +2 EV) and merge them. → [[Photo-Merges]]
- **Shutter type:** mechanical indoors, because LED lights can band with the electronic
  shutter.
- **Video:** on the Pocket 3, Tilt Locked holds the pitch steady, so verticals stay put
  as you walk. On the Action 5 Pro, use Standard (Dewarp) to remove the fisheye bend.

## Gear reality

Forums say you need a tilt-shift lens and a full-frame camera. You need a level camera,
room to step back, a wide enough lens, and editing.

- **The L10's 24 mm** handles most exteriors; the iPhone's 13 mm covers tight interiors.
  → [[My-Kit]]
- **A tripod** helps for interiors and blue hour. Workarounds: a table, a wall, a
  railing or a bag of rice, with the self-timer.

## Ethics and the law

- **From public places,** photographing buildings is generally allowed in Singapore and
  many other countries. Rules differ abroad: some limit publishing pictures of certain
  buildings or artworks, and the Eiffel Tower's night lighting is a well-known example.
  This is not legal advice, and laws change.
- **Private property** (condos, malls, offices) sets its own rules: ask first, and if
  security asks you to leave, leave politely. Military and some infrastructure sites
  ban photography; obey the signs.
- **Homes are private.** HDB blocks stand close together. Don't point a long lens into
  people's windows.
- **Places of worship:** ask first, dress appropriately, use no flash, and don't
  photograph people at prayer without permission.
- **Honest editing.** Removing a crane is fine in art. In a property listing, hiding the
  building site next door misleads.

## Who to study

- **Darren Soh** — Singapore's buildings and changing city, including modernist
  landmarks and HDB estates, often photographed before redevelopment. Look at how
  plainly and consistently he frames them, so a set of buildings reads as a record.
- **Julius Shulman** — *Case Study House #22* (Los Angeles, 1960): a glass house at
  dusk, two women seated inside, the city lights below. Look at the timing, with
  interior light balanced against the dusk sky, and at the perfectly straight verticals.
- **Andreas Gursky** — *Paris, Montparnasse* (1993): a huge apartment block shown
  flat-on as a grid of windows, joined digitally from more than one frame. Look at how
  frontal framing turns a building into a pattern.

## A project you can finish in a week

**One estate, straight lines only.** Choose one HDB estate or one street of shophouses.
Make ten frames: three full façades (level, then corrected), three details or patterns,
two at blue hour, one with a person for scale, and one interior or void deck. Every
vertical must be vertical. Keep a before-and-after of one correction. That's your A6.2
set. For A6.3, shoot the same block on the iPhone's 120 mm from far away, and compare.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why do vertical lines lean in when you tilt the camera up, and why does stepping back help?
> Tilting puts the top of the building further from the sensor's plane than the bottom,
> so the top is drawn smaller. Stepping back, or going higher, lets the whole building
> fit with the camera level, so the lines stay parallel.

> [!question]- You shot an HDB block at 24 mm, camera level, with empty road filling the bottom half. Why is that better than tilting up?
> The verticals are already straight. Cropping off the road gives the same geometry as
> a shift lens, with no stretching. You only lose pixels.

> [!question]- Why does a strongly corrected photo look soft near the top?
> The correction stretches the top to make it as wide as the bottom. The same detail is
> spread over a bigger area, so it looks thinner there.

> [!question]- In Singapore, in which months does a north-facing façade get direct sun, and what is that light like at midday?
> From about late March to late September, when the sun stays in the northern half of
> the sky. Around midday it grazes the wall steeply from above, so texture and ledges
> throw long shadows downwards. The rest of the year that face stays in shade.
