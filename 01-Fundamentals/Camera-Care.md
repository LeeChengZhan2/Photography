# Camera Care

> [!summary] In one line
> Most camera damage is slow and preventable: humidity, grit on glass, salt, heat, and
> careless handling of batteries and cards. A few habits keep all four devices, and
> your files, safe.

## What it is

Camera care is the routine that keeps batteries, memory cards, glass and bodies
working. None of it is difficult, and most of it happens *between* shoots.

In Singapore the biggest threat is humidity. It lets fungus grow on lenses, and it fogs
cold gear the moment you step outside. Salt water, sand and heat come next.

## How it works

### Batteries

Camera and phone batteries are lithium-ion. Heat speeds up the chemistry that wears
them out, and a damaged one can swell or, rarely, catch fire.

- Charge the day before a shoot, and carry a charged spare. For a device with a
  built-in battery, a power bank is the spare.
- Keep batteries out of heat: not in a parked car, not on a sunny dashboard.
- For storage of a month or more, leave them about half charged, not full or empty.
- Video, bright screens and wireless connections drain a battery fastest.
- A swollen battery is finished. Stop using it, don't charge it, and take it to
  battery recycling.
- Avoid unbranded, unusually cheap batteries and chargers. A badly made lithium battery
  is a fire risk.

### Memory cards

Cards come in two sizes: **SD**, about the size of a postage stamp, and **microSD**,
about the size of a fingernail. Inside, they work the same way. Check which one each
camera takes.

Speed matters for video. Video writes a constant stream of data, and if the card can't
keep up, recording stops. The markings promise a *minimum sustained* writing speed:

| Marking | Minimum sustained writing speed |
|---|---|
| C10 (Class 10), U1, V10 | 10 MB/s |
| U3, V30 | 30 MB/s |
| V60 | 60 MB/s |
| V90 | 90 MB/s |
| "Up to 170 MB/s" | Nothing guaranteed; usually the peak *reading* speed |

Video bit rates are given in megabits per second (Mbps). Divide by 8 for megabytes:
150 Mbps is about 19 MB/s, so a V30 card keeps up. 4K and 10-bit modes usually use the
highest bit rates, so they need the fastest cards. Each device's manual gives the
minimum card speed it needs. → [[Codecs-and-Bitrate]]

Three more rules:

- **Beware fake cards.** They report more space than they really have. Once the real
  space runs out, new files overwrite old ones or come out corrupt, with no warning.
  Buy from a trustworthy shop, and test a new card with a free program that fills the
  whole card and reads it back.
- **Format in the camera**, and only after the files are backed up.
  → [[Ingest-and-Backup-Workflow]]
- **Don't run a card completely full**, and don't remove it while the camera is still
  writing. Either can cut the last file short or damage the card's file system. Swap
  cards before they fill.

### Glass and screens

The danger is grit. Wiping a dusty lens drags grit across the coating and scratches it.
So remove the grit first, then wipe.

1. **Blow** loose dust off with a hand blower. Not with your mouth (spit), and not with
   canned air (it can spray cold liquid).
2. **Brush** off anything left with a soft, clean lens brush.
3. **Wipe** smears with a clean microfibre cloth, in small circles from the centre out.
   For stubborn marks, put a drop of lens-cleaning fluid on the cloth, never on the
   lens.

Never use a shirt, tissue or paper towel. They carry grit and fibres, and paper can
scratch coatings. Clean off fingerprints soon, because skin oil left on a coating can
damage it over time. Screens and the viewfinder eyepiece get the same microfibre
treatment.

### Humidity and lens fungus

Fungus spores are everywhere. On camera gear they grow where it's humid, dark and
still, and a closed camera bag in a cupboard is ideal for them. They feed on dust and
oils on the glass, and their threads can etch the lens coating. The damage is often
permanent.

Fungus thrives when humidity stays above roughly 60–70% for long periods, and
Singapore's air is often above 80%. So store your gear dry:

- A **dry box** is a sealed plastic box with silica gel (drying beads) and a small
  hygrometer (humidity meter). When the beads change colour, dry them out again as
  their instructions say. It's the cheap option.
- A **dry cabinet** is an electric cabinet that holds a set humidity by itself.
- Aim for around 40–50% relative humidity.

Gear that's used often suffers less, because light and moving air discourage fungus.
After a humid day, let the gear and the bag air out before the gear goes back in the
box.

### Condensation

Warm air holds more water than cool air. When warm, humid air touches something cold,
it cools and leaves its water on the surface, like a cold drink "sweating". Carry a
camera from air-conditioning into Singapore's heat and the same thing happens on the
lens, the screen, and sometimes inside the camera.

The fix is to let the gear warm up slowly:

1. Before you leave the air-conditioning, put the camera in a closed bag. A zip bag
   inside your camera bag is ideal.
2. Outside, leave the bag closed for about 20–30 minutes, until the gear reaches the
   outside temperature.
3. If a lens has fogged anyway, switch the camera off and wait. Don't wipe inside, and
   don't open the battery or card doors until the fog has cleared.

Going the other way, from heat into air-conditioning, is safe: warm gear doesn't fog in
cool air.

### Salt water, sand and rain

Salt water dries into crystals that corrode metal and damage rubber seals. Sand gets
into seals, buttons and moving parts.

- After the sea or a pool, rinse a waterproof camera in fresh water, then let it dry
  before you open any door.
- Before it goes in the water, check that the door seals are clean and closed. One
  grain of sand or a hair can let water in.
- At the beach, keep gear in a bag between shots. Blow or brush sand off; don't wipe.
- Afternoon thunderstorms arrive fast. Carry a plastic bag big enough for your camera.

### Travel

- **Spare lithium batteries and power banks go in carry-on luggage, never in checked
  bags.** A battery fire in the cabin can be seen and put out; in the hold nobody can
  reach it.
- Keep each spare in a case, or tape over its contacts, so it can't short-circuit
  against keys or coins.
- Airline rules differ and change, so check yours before you fly. Camera batteries are
  usually well inside the limits; large power banks may not be.
- Back up while you travel, as at home: two copies before you format a card.

## How to use it

| When | What to do |
|---|---|
| After every shoot | Blow and wipe the glass, charge the batteries, back up and format the cards, put the gear back in the dry box |
| Once a month | Read the dry box's hygrometer and dry the silica gel if needed; check the front glass for haze or spots |
| Before water or the beach | Clean and check the seals; plan where you'll rinse in fresh water |
| Before a flight | Spare batteries and power banks in the carry-on, contacts covered |

## On your kit

- **Lumix L10:** not splash-resistant, so keep it out of rain and spray. Its lens is
  fixed, so fungus or dust inside it can't be cleaned at home. Prevention is
  everything: it lives in the dry box between shoots. It takes full-size SD cards.
- **iPhone 15 Pro Max:** rated IP68, which covers an accidental dunk in fresh water. It
  isn't a dive camera: the rating isn't tested in salt or chlorinated water, and seals
  wear with age. Wipe its lenses before you shoot. A fingerprint is the usual cause of
  hazy, flaring phone photos.
- **DJI Pocket 3:** no weather rating, and it takes microSD cards. Its gimbal is three
  small motors holding the camera, so treat it gently:
  - Keep it in a cover or case when you're not using it, never loose with keys.
  - Switch it off before you pack it.
  - Don't force the camera head by hand while it's on, and give the head room to move
    when it switches on.
  - DJI guarantees software updates only until 31 Dec 2026. Install the last one it
    releases.
- **DJI Osmo Action 5 Pro:** waterproof to 20 m bare, and to 60 m in its case. Rinse it
  in fresh water after the sea, and check its door seals before every swim. Wipe the
  lens and both screens with microfibre. It takes microSD cards, and also has 47 GB of
  built-in storage.

## Common mistakes

- **Wiping a lens with your shirt.** Blow off the grit first, then use a clean
  microfibre cloth.
- **Leaving gear in a closed bag after a humid day.** That's how fungus starts. Air it
  out, then store it in the dry box.
- **Unpacking cold gear straight into the heat.** Bag it before you leave the
  air-conditioning, and give it 20–30 minutes.
- **Trusting the biggest number on a card.** That's usually the peak reading speed.
  For video, look at the U or V mark.
- **Skipping the rinse because the camera is waterproof.** Waterproofing keeps water
  out; it doesn't stop salt drying in the seals.
- **Packing spare batteries in checked luggage.** They travel in the cabin, with their
  contacts covered.

## Try it

A 20-minute care audit, done alongside **A0.1**:

1. For each device, find which card it takes and the minimum speed its manual asks
   for. Compare that with the U or V mark on your cards.
2. Check every battery: charged, and not swollen.
3. Clean each lens and screen in the right order: blow, brush, wipe.
4. Check where your gear sleeps. If it's a closed bag in a cupboard, plan a dry box:
   any sealed plastic box with silica gel works, and a hygrometer tells you whether it's
   working.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Why does your lens fog when you walk out of a mall, and why does a closed bag help?
> The lens is cold from the air-conditioning. Warm, humid air touching it cools and
> leaves its water there. In a closed bag the gear warms up slowly and very little humid
> air reaches it, so nothing condenses.

> [!question]- A card says "U3, V30, up to 170 MB/s". Which marks matter for 4K video, and why?
> U3 and V30. Both promise at least 30 MB/s of sustained writing, which is what a
> constant video stream needs. The 170 MB/s is a peak, usually for reading, and
> promises nothing about recording.

> [!question]- Why does fungus grow on a camera kept in a closed bag, but rarely on one used every day?
> Fungus needs humidity, darkness and still air for long periods. A closed bag in a
> cupboard gives it all three. Daily use brings light, moving air and a chance to dry.

> [!question]- The Action 5 Pro is waterproof. Why rinse it after the sea?
> Waterproofing keeps water out, but salt dries into crystals on the seals and buttons.
> The crystals corrode metal and stop seals closing properly, so the next swim can leak.

> [!question]- Why must spare lithium batteries go in your carry-on bag?
> A damaged lithium battery can catch fire. In the cabin, the crew can see it and put it
> out. In the hold, nobody can reach it.
