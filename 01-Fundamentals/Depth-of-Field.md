# Depth of Field

> [!summary] In one line
> Depth of field is how much of the scene, from near to far, looks sharp. It decides
> whether the background tells part of the story or melts away.

## What it is

A lens focuses at one distance only. Things at exactly that distance are as sharp as the
lens can make them. Things a little nearer or further are slightly blurred. If the blur
is small enough, your eye can't see it, and they look sharp too.

**Depth of field** (DOF) is that zone of acceptable sharpness, from a near limit to a far
limit. It has no hard edge: sharpness fades gradually on both sides. The limits are
where the fading starts to show.

| | Shallow depth of field | Deep depth of field |
|---|---|---|
| What you see | A thin slice is sharp; the rest melts away | Near and far are both sharp |
| Good for | One subject against a messy background | A subject in its place: landscapes, streets, groups |
| Usual recipe | Wide aperture, close subject, longer focal length | Smaller aperture, distant subject, wider focal length |
| The risk | A small focus error ruins the photo | The background competes with the subject |

## How it works

### Why there is a zone: the circle of confusion

Light from one point on the subject leaves the lens as a cone and meets again at a point.
For a point at the focus distance, the cone closes exactly on the sensor. For a point
nearer or further, the cone closes behind or in front of the sensor, so the sensor
records a tiny disc instead of a point.

If the disc is small enough, you can't tell it from a point. The largest disc that still
looks like a point is called the **circle of confusion**. It is not a part of the lens.
It is an agreement about how closely the photo will be looked at. The usual standard is
a print about A4 size, seen from about 25 cm. On a Four Thirds sensor like the L10's,
that means a disc about 0.014 mm wide.

### The four controls

1. **Aperture.** A smaller opening (a bigger f-number) makes a narrower cone, so every
   blur disc is smaller and more of the scene passes as sharp. At close and middle
   distances, f/8 gives roughly twice the depth of field of f/4. → [[Aperture]]
2. **Magnification: the subject's size in the frame.** Distance and focal length work
   together. Make the subject twice as big, by going half as far away or by doubling
   the focal length, and the depth of field drops to about a quarter. Doing the same
   with the aperture alone would take four stops, from f/8 to f/2.
3. **Sensor size.** For the same framing and f-number, a smaller sensor gives deeper
   depth of field. It needs a shorter real focal length for the same view, so its real
   opening is smaller. To compare, multiply the f-number by the crop factor: f/1.7 on the
   L10 (crop factor about 2.2) gives about the depth of field of f/3.7 on full frame.
   → [[Sensor-Size-and-Crop-Factor]]
4. **Viewing size.** Print the same file twice as big, or zoom in on screen, and every
   blur disc grows with it. Details that looked sharp now look soft. The depth of field
   has shrunk, though nothing changed in the camera.

One trap with magnification. If you zoom in *and* step back so the subject stays the same
size, the depth of field stays about the same. The background still looks blurrier with
the longer lens, because the lens enlarges the background, and its blur with it.

### Where the zone sits

Blur grows faster for things in front of the focus point than for things behind it. So
the sharp zone usually reaches further behind your subject than in front of it.

| Where you focus | How the zone splits |
|---|---|
| Very close (macro, a tight portrait at 75 mm) | About half in front, half behind |
| Everyday distances with a wider lens | More behind: roughly one third in front, two thirds behind |
| At the hyperfocal distance | The back part reaches all the way to infinity |

Two examples on the L10, both focused at 2 m:

- **24 mm, f/1.7:** sharp from about 1.4 m to 3.3 m. That's 0.6 m in front and 1.3 m
  behind: about one third and two thirds.
- **75 mm, f/2.8:** sharp from about 1.87 m to 2.15 m. That's about 13 cm in front and
  15 cm behind: almost even.

At macro distances, such as the L10's 3 cm AF Macro, the zone becomes very thin and
splits almost evenly.

## How to use it

Decide first: should the background tell part of the story, or disappear?

**To make it shallower:**

1. **Get closer, or zoom in.** This usually does more than the aperture, because halving
   the distance cuts the depth of field to about a quarter.
2. **Move the subject away from the background.** The further the background sits behind
   the sharp zone, the more it blurs. A wall 5 m behind melts; a wall 50 cm behind
   doesn't.
3. **Open the aperture.**

**To make it deeper:**

1. **Step back, or zoom wider.**
2. **Close the aperture, but on the L10 stop at about f/8.** Past that, diffraction
   softens the whole photo: you gain depth but lose detail everywhere.
3. **Focus in the right place.** For a scene that runs to the horizon, focus at about
   twice the distance of the nearest thing that must be sharp. → [[Hyperfocal-Distance]]

Starting points on the L10:

| Shot | Settings | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Head-and-shoulders portrait | 75 mm, f/2.8, about 1.5 m away | About 15 cm is sharp: the whole face, with a soft background |
| Two people at different distances | f/4–f/5.6 | Enough depth for both faces; check by zooming in on playback |
| Street, walking around | 24 mm, f/5.6, focus at 2–3 m | About 1 m to infinity is sharp, so you rarely need to refocus |
| Landscape | 24 mm, f/5.6–f/8 | Deep enough, before diffraction costs much |
| A detail under 50 cm | 24 mm in AF Macro, f/2.8–f/4 | The zone is very thin; put it on the part that matters |

## On your kit

Same subject, 2 m away, each device at its widest aperture. The figures come from the
standard formulas, with crop factors worked out from each sensor's size. Treat them as a
guide, not a measurement.

| Device and setting | Sharp from about | Depth | Full-frame equivalent |
|---|---|---|---|
| L10, 75 mm, f/2.8 | 1.87 m to 2.15 m | About 27 cm | f/6.2 |
| L10, 24 mm, f/1.7 | 1.4 m to 3.3 m | About 1.9 m | f/3.7 |
| iPhone 15 Pro Max, 1× (24 mm, f/1.78) | 1.2 m to 5.5 m | About 4 m | About f/6 |
| Pocket 3 (20 mm, f/2.0) | 1.1 m to 10 m | About 9 m | About f/5.4 |
| Action 5 Pro (fixed focus) | 35 cm to infinity | Everything | About f/10 |

- **L10:** the only device where you choose, and its shallowest look comes from the long
  end. For a soft background, zoom to 75 mm and step back. At the same subject size,
  24 mm at f/1.7 has *less* depth on the face, but 75 mm blurs a distant
  background up to about twice as much. It also keeps faces in natural proportion.
  → [[Focal-Length-and-Perspective]]
- **iPhone:** its main sensor is about 3.5 times smaller across than full frame, so
  f/1.78 behaves like about f/6. The 0.5× lens (13 mm) is deeper still. Portrait mode
  adds blur in software, from a guessed depth map. → [[Computational-Photography]]
- **Pocket 3 and Action 5 Pro:** fixed apertures on small sensors. The Action 5 Pro goes
  furthest: a tiny opening behind a very wide lens means it never needs to focus.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating the aperture as the only control.** Distance and focal length often matter
  more. Halve the distance and the depth of field drops to about a quarter.
- **Shooting a group at f/1.7.** Faces at different distances go soft. Use f/4–f/5.6, or
  arrange people at the same distance from the camera.
- **Judging sharpness on the camera's screen.** A 3-inch screen makes almost everything
  look sharp. Zoom in on playback before you trust a frame.
- **Focusing on the nose or an ear at a wide aperture.** People look at the eyes. Put the
  focus on the eye nearest the camera. → [[Autofocus-Modes]]
- **Expecting a phone's f/1.78 to blur like a big camera's.** The f-number alone doesn't
  set depth of field; sensor size and real focal length do too.

## Try it

Twenty minutes, on the L10, in aperture priority (A) mode:

1. Put five objects (cups, bottles, shoes) in a diagonal line going away from you, about
   20 cm apart.
2. Zoom to 75 mm. Stand about 1.5 m from the first object. Focus on the second one.
3. Before each shot, write down which objects will look sharp. Shoot at f/2.8, f/5.6
   and f/8.
4. Repeat at 24 mm from the same spot. Then repeat at 24 mm from half the distance.
5. Check every frame zoomed in. Score each prediction right or wrong.

This is a small version of assignment **A1.6**.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- For a softer background on the L10, which helps more: f/1.7 at 24 mm, or 75 mm at f/2.8 from further back?
> 75 mm from further back. The longer lens enlarges the background, and its blur with
> it. Going from f/2.8 to f/1.7 gains only about one and a half stops of aperture.

> [!question]- You move from 2 m to 1 m from your subject, same lens and aperture. What happens to the depth of field, and why?
> It shrinks to about a quarter. The subject is now twice as big in the frame, and depth
> of field falls with the square of magnification. That is why distance is often a
> stronger control than aperture.

> [!question]- Why does the iPhone's main camera at f/1.78 give deeper depth of field than the L10 at f/1.7, for the same framing?
> Its sensor is much smaller, so it uses a much shorter real focal length for the same
> view, and its real opening is only a few millimetres wide. Multiply by the crop factor
> to compare: about f/6 on full frame for the iPhone, f/3.7 for the L10.

> [!question]- A photo looks sharp front to back on your phone, but parts look soft in a big print. Did the depth of field change?
> In effect, yes. Depth of field depends on viewing size. The big print enlarges every
> blur disc until you can see it. The file is the same; the standard for "sharp" got
> stricter.

> [!question]- You focus the L10 at 2 m, 24 mm, f/1.7. Is more of the sharp zone in front of 2 m or behind it?
> Behind: about 1.4 m to 3.3 m, so 0.6 m in front and 1.3 m behind. Blur grows faster
> for things in front of the focus point, so the zone reaches further back.
